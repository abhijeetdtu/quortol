HOST A: We start with a question that defines the city itself. Why does Nashua call itself the Gate City? It sounds like a border town, but it was actually a mill town built around water power and rail lines.

HOST B: Right, so the name implies movement or entry, but the source material says it’s about industry. Is there a connection between the gate idea and how the mills operated?

HOST A: Exactly. The industrial arc created an identity that lasted over a century. From incorporation in 1823 until the closure in 1948, the Nashua Manufacturing Company wasn't just a business; it was the social architect of the place.

HOST B: That’s a heavy claim for one company. How much control did they really have over daily life?

HOST A: They owned forty tenements on adjacent blocks and built two churches specifically to hire enough workers to man the machinery. If you didn’t live within walking distance of the complex, you weren’t really part of the workforce.

HOST B: So the geography was designed by the employer, not just by chance or natural growth.

HOST A: Precisely. And before we get to the mills, we need to understand why this spot mattered at all. Long before settlers arrived, Indigenous peoples of the Algonquian language group lived there.

HOST B: I know that from history class, but what does the name actually mean?

HOST A: “Nashua” means “beautiful stream with a pebbly bottom,” though some say it means “land between two rivers.” It describes the physical landscape that became the engine of transformation.

HOST B: Okay, so the river is central. But when did it officially become a town people could call home?

HOST A: The Massachusetts Bay Colony issued land grants in the 1650s. About 1666, Judge Edward Tyng arrived and saw the Nashua River area as a future economic stronghold.

HOST B: Wait, so there was already a plan for industry back then?

HOST A: Not exactly. He purchased land in what would become Tyngsboro, Massachusetts, seeing the potential. The town of Dunstable was chartered by the Massachusetts General Court on October 26, 1673.

HOST B: So it started as Dunstable. That sounds like a different place entirely.

HOST A: It did. In 1741, a boundary dispute placed the northern section of Dunstable in New Hampshire five years later, when it was incorporated as Dunstable, New Hampshire.

HOST B: And eventually, they renamed it after the river itself?

HOST A: Yes. In 1836, the New Hampshire legislature renamed the town Nashua after the Nashua River. There was even a split in 1842 over the location of the town hall—northern “Nashville” and southern “Nashua”—for eleven years before being reunited as the City of Nashua in 1853.

HOST B: Eleven years of confusion over where to put the town hall? That’s a lot of bureaucracy for one city.

HOST A: It highlights how contentious growth could be. But once settled, the industrial transformation began with Daniel Abbott. He is considered the “father of Nashua.”

HOST B: What did he actually do to start this whole thing?

HOST A: He chartered the Nashua Manufacturing Company on June 18, 1823. His initiative was to buy up land from the banks of the Merrimack along the Nashua River up to Mine Falls.

HOST B: So he bought the land specifically for power generation?

HOST A: He needed to harness and control available water power by building a thirty-foot dam at Mine Falls and a three-mile-long power canal.

HOST B: Three miles long? That seems like a lot of engineering just to move water.

HOST A: It was necessary. The canal fed the looms of New England’s textile empire. Actual cotton production began in 1826. By 1836, the company employed approximately one thousand people.

HOST B: And they were making cloth? How much cloth are we talking about?

HOST A: Estimates vary slightly in the records, but earlier estimates showed three mills and seven hundred ten looms producing approximately nine point three million yards of cloth annually.

HOST B: That’s a massive output for a single town. Did they have enough workers to keep that going?

HOST A: They hired mostly female workers who lived within easy walking distance of the complex. The company owned forty tenements on adjacent blocks and built two churches to hire enough workers to man the machinery.

HOST B: So they built infrastructure for their employees as well as the machines themselves.

HOST A: Yes. But transportation was just as critical. Six railroad lines crossed the mill town—Boston, Lowell and Nashua; Worcester and Nashua; Nashua and Acton—and more.

HOST B: Wait, six lines crossing one town? That sounds crowded.

HOST A: It was busy. With fifty-six trains arriving and departing daily in the years before the Civil War. These various railroads led to all sections of the country, north, east, south, and west.

HOST B: That visual of six tracks must look chaotic from above. Have you seen any maps that show this density?

HOST A: There is a bird’s-eye view from 1883 by Oakley H. Bailey & Co. It shows the Nashua Manufacturing Company, Jackson Mills, six churches, civic buildings, and the railroad depot.

HOST B: I can imagine looking at that lithograph and seeing a city defined by industry rather than nature.

HOST A: Exactly. The river did not stop working. By the 1860s, six railroad lines crossed the mill town with fifty-six trains arriving and departing daily. Workers lived within walking distance of the mills, and the company owned the largest contiguous portion of property, encompassing four hundred acres.

HOST B: So when they shut down later, it wasn’t just a factory closing; it was a neighborhood vanishing.

HOST A: When the Nashua Manufacturing Company closed its doors in 1948, nearly two thousand workers—roughly one-third of the city’s population—found themselves without employment.

HOST B: One-third of the population unemployed overnight? That sounds like a civic trauma rather than an economic event.

HOST A: It was exactly that. The closure was not merely an economic event but a civic trauma that reshaped the city’s trajectory, forcing a post-industrial reinvention that continues today through the Millyard district’s redevelopment.

HOST B: What happened to those buildings after the machines stopped turning?

HOST A: Following the collapse of the traditional mill businesses in the 1940s, these buildings saw new uses—residential, artist spaces, churches, and light manufacturing. The Millyard district is poised to be reborn as a thriving mixed-use destination.

HOST B: I remember reading about Clocktower Place. Was that one of those conversions?

HOST A: Yes. The former cotton textile mill building was historically restored in the 1980s into Clocktower Place Apartments. The building now houses three hundred twenty-six luxury apartment units.

HOST B: So they kept the architecture but changed the function entirely.

HOST A: They did. But before we get to the restoration, there were disasters along the way too. There are photographic prints documenting Nashua NH, including September 1938 hurricane/flood damage.

HOST B: Did that affect the mills specifically?

HOST A: The 1938 hurricane was a major disaster for the area generally, documented by photographers like Read & Sanderson. It shows the vulnerability of infrastructure built on riverbanks.

HOST B: That makes sense given how much water power they relied on. If the river swelled, could it break the dams?

HOST A: The source material doesn’t specify structural failure from the storm, but it highlights the physical record of the town’s resilience through such events.

HOST B: Let’s circle back to the end of the era though. Who made the decision to close in 1948?

HOST A: Textron Inc. had acquired the Nashua Manufacturing Company in 1945 and liquidated the mill in 1947. In 1948, after only three years, Textron shut down the Nashua mills.

HOST B: Three years is a very short time to own an entire city’s economy.

HOST A: Yes. Nearly two years leading up to the closure, Textron Inc. had acquired the company and then threw some two thousand people out of work when the city had a population of about twenty-five thousand.

HOST B: So the acquisition itself didn't fix things; it just set them up to fail quickly?

HOST A: Not necessarily failing, but shifting focus. Thanks to a group of forward-thinking Nashuans, the dark clouds of the 1948 shutdown began to dissipate over the next few years. The Nashua New Hampshire Foundation, established in 1941, worked to attract companies to the area by offering competitive rental and leasing rates.

HOST B: So they pivoted from manufacturing textiles to attracting other industries entirely.

HOST A: Exactly. They offered competitive rental and leasing rates to bring new industry into Nashua's Millyard after Textron shut down in 1948. There was an illustration showing Greater Downtown Nashua created in the late 1940s and early 50s by members of that foundation.

HOST B: That sounds like a blueprint for survival rather than just recovery.

HOST A: It was a plan for reinvention. But we should acknowledge the contradictions in how we know this history. Several minor contradictions appear in sources regarding settlement dates. Some say 1654, others say 1665, and one says 1673 as the permanent settlement.

HOST B: So even the start date is debated among historians?

HOST A: Yes. The 1654 date appears in the context of a fur trading town, while 1673 refers to the formal charter. Similarly, production numbers vary; one source says nine point three million yards annually on seven hundred ten looms, another says thirteen million yards.

HOST B: Does that mean we can’t trust the economic scale at all?

HOST A: Not exactly. The discrepancy likely reflects different years or different company components. We have to treat those numbers as estimates of a massive operation rather than precise accounting ledgers.

HOST B: It’s interesting how much visual evidence we have though. There are stereograph views of Nashua NH including mills, hotels, churches, and streets from the late 1800s.

HOST B: That contrast seems key to why it worked so well initially. Industry next to nature.

HOST B: So the river still flows, but it no longer drives the city?
