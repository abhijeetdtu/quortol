# The City That Brewed Itself: How Portland Became America's Coffee Capital Without Growing a Single Bean

Source blog: The City That Brewed Itself: How Portland Became America's Coffee Capital Without Growing a Single Bean
Slug: `portland-coffee-capital-wilde`

JOURNALIST: When people think of Portland coffee, they probably picture rainy streets, tiny cafes, someone weighing espresso like it’s a chemistry experiment. Very local, very handmade. But your piece says the whole thing depends on something Portland cannot produce at all.

AUTHOR: Right. That’s the joke hiding inside the reputation. Portland has built this very local coffee identity around a crop that comes from far away.

JOURNALIST: So before we get into rankings or tariffs or the business side, what are we actually talking about? A city with a lot of cafes? A roasting industry? A culture?

AUTHOR: All three, but the culture is what people see first. The cafes, the roasters, the rituals. Then underneath that you find the trade network, the margins, the imported beans. That’s where the story gets less cozy.

JOURNALIST: Okay, let’s talk about that ranking then. Because I know people call Portland the capital. Is that just a vibe?

AUTHOR: It’s not just a vibe. There are rankings behind it. WalletHub compared the hundred largest U.S. cities across twelve metrics last year and again in 2025 and 2026. Portland came out on top pretty clearly.

JOURNALIST: Ahead of Seattle?

AUTHOR: Yeah, and that’s the part that surprises people. Seattle is the famous coffee city, but Portland beats it pretty clearly in the numbers. In 2025, Portland scored around seventy-one out of one hundred. Orlando was second, Long Beach third. Seattle was fifth.

JOURNALIST: On what, though? Taste? Price? Number of shops?

AUTHOR: Mostly density and affordability. Portland has far more coffee shops per person than the national average, and a lot of them are highly rated without being absurdly expensive. A study found Portland has nearly twenty-eight coffee shops for every one hundred thousand residents. That’s more than double the national average.

JOURNALIST: And roasters?

AUTHOR: Even higher density there. Nearly two-and-a-half roasteries per one hundred thousand residents. Almost four times the national average. So it’s not just about drinking coffee. It’s about making it.

JOURNALIST: But how did this happen? Is there a master plan?

AUTHOR: Not really a master plan. The clean version is that the company failed because demand fell. But that’s not quite right. Demand was there. The problem was the cost of satisfying that demand. It started in 1999 with Stumptown Coffee Roasters on SE Division Street. Duane Sorenson opened it when Starbucks was everywhere, doing dark roasts in strip malls.

JOURNALIST: So they were pushing back against the giant?

AUTHOR: Exactly. They wanted lighter roasts, single origins, transparency. They called it the third wave. But the real shift came later, around 2003, when Sorenson began travelling directly to farms and negotiating prices with producers, cutting out the commodity-trading middlemen. He called it Direct Trade.

JOURNALIST: That sounds like a supply chain fix.

AUTHOR: It was. It paid premiums for quality and demanded transparency from both sides. That made Stumptown the defining brand of a movement. Later, in 2015, they launched ready-to-drink cold brew in brown bottles modeled on Olympia Beer stubbies. That made cold brew scalable and shelf-stable.

JOURNALIST: And that spread nationally?

AUTHOR: Within a few years, grocery stores were selling it, even Starbucks launched its own version. Sorenson sold Stumptown to JAB Holdings in 2015. But while Stumptown grew big, other shops stayed independent. Heart Coffee Roasters started in 2009 focusing on precision espresso. Coava Coffee Roasters founded in 2008 by Matt Higgins in his garage.

JOURNALIST: So you had these three big ones close together?

AUTHOR: By 2015, Stumptown, Heart, and Coava all operated state-of-the-art roasting facilities within walking distance of one another in Portland’s Central Eastside Industrial District. The Oregonian wrote that year that if Portland is the capital of global coffee culture, these three buildings are its new city hall.

JOURNALIST: That’s a tight ecosystem. But what about the smaller players? I hear there are hundreds of tiny roasters now.

AUTHOR: Yes, nearly one hundred microroasters operate in the metro area now. Many roast quantities as small as five or ten pounds at a time. The catalyst was Buckman Coffee Factory. It opened in 2015 by Cassy Gleason and her sister.

JOURNALIST: How does that work?

AUTHOR: They provide the industrial roasting equipment and the warehouse space. Microroasters rent time on the machines, sharing the cost of equipment that would otherwise price them out of the market. Around forty businesses now operate out of the space. It’s lower barriers, shared infrastructure, more participants.

JOURNALIST: So it’s like an incubator?

AUTHOR: Sort of. It makes a socialist blush and a capitalist cheer. The industry data reflects this growth. IBISWorld reports Oregon’s Coffee and Snack Shops industry has grown to one point four billion in market size across two thousand two hundred ninety-four businesses employing twenty-three thousand seven hundred workers.

JOURNALIST: That’s a lot of jobs.

AUTHOR: Nearly nine percent of the Portland metro area’s workforce is employed in food preparation and serving, with baristas included. Average hourly wages are around twenty dollars per hour. But none of this would be possible without a global logistics network most coffee drinkers never think about.

JOURNALIST: Which is what you mean by the invisible machinery?

AUTHOR: Yes. The United States is the world’s largest importer of green coffee by volume, buying six point three one billion dollars worth in 2024 alone. Brazil, Colombia, and Guatemala supplied fifty-nine percent of total import value.

JOURNALIST: And that’s where the risk lies?

AUTHOR: It’s there. The Specialty Coffee Transaction Guide tracks the prices roasters actually pay for green specialty coffee. The median FOB price for African specialty coffees was three point nine five dollars per pound in 2024. These are the prices that determine whether a Portland roaster can pay its rent or continue to exist at all.

JOURNALIST: So if those prices go up, or shipping gets harder, who feels it first?

AUTHOR: The margins on imports are roughly ten percent. That’s the same as the tariff itself. In the spring of 2025, the Trump administration imposed a ten percent tariff on virtually all U.S. imports, including green coffee beans. Importers said it leaves no room to absorb any kind of tariff costs.

JOURNALIST: Does that mean they raise prices?

AUTHOR: They have to pass them through all the way down to the consumer. A dozen coffee-industry leaders gathered at Buckman Coffee Factory for a roundtable with U.S. Rep. Suzanne Bonamici in May 2025. Paul Thornton, owner of Thornton Family Coffee Roasters, told her he had been buying coffee for forty years and had never worried about tariffs before.

JOURNALIST: He said what?

AUTHOR: He said they can’t tell customers the price of their beans for the next twelve months anymore. Mark Stell, founder of Portland Coffee Roasters, was blunter. His company paid three hundred seventy thousand dollars in tariffs in 2025 alone. Since December, they eliminated tariffs for coffee, but he noted they were still paying eight thousand dollars this month.

JOURNALIST: And prices went up generally?

AUTHOR: Yes. Coffee prices in the United States rose twenty-one percent year-over-year through October 2025, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Price Index. That was faster than any other tracked item.

JOURNALIST: So it’s not just one business struggling? It’s structural?

AUTHOR: Cassy Gleason framed it in larger terms. She said we are an import-dependent state. She thinks of these tariffs as not just hurting our industry, but hurting the very idea of free trade in general. She noted that the supply chain connects Portland to some of the most politically fragile regions on earth, like East Africa and Central America.

JOURNALIST: Which makes me wonder about who owns this culture now. Is it still just white guys in hoodies?

AUTHOR: Not exactly. The fourth wave is seeing historically marginalized communities owning the means of production across the value chain. Kalesa Coffee opened in Portland’s Gotham Building, run by three Filipino-American owners. One of them told Eater Portland that coffee had become pretty single origin-y, very bro-y, but they wanted something where people are celebrating their culture.

JOURNALIST: And there are others?

AUTHOR: Portland Cà Phê, a Vietnamese roastery, and Proud Mary, founded by an immigrant from a coffee-growing region. They represent a shift where coffee professionals from countries that grow coffee are now roasting and selling it in a consuming nation. A delicious inversion of the old colonial script.

JOURNALIST: But does that solve the displacement issue? Because you mention gentrification in your piece.

AUTHOR: Dennis Cote, a Harvard Extension School graduate student whose 2023 thesis examined third-wave cafes in Portland between 1995 and 2020, identified a tension at the heart of the city’s coffee culture. He wrote that gentrification is about displacement, not dis-spacement. Space becomes a place when it gets filled with memory and meaning.

JOURNALIST: So the cafes themselves can be symbols of erasure?

AUTHOR: Yes. The same establishments that brought specialty coffee to a wider audience also became symbols of gentrification and displacement in historically marginalized neighborhoods. The fourth-wave entrepreneurs are attempting to fill that space with something the third wave often neglected: cultural specificity and economic inclusion.

JOURNALIST: So we have this local industry, but it relies on global supply chains that are suddenly fragile. And the people running it are trying to fix the culture while fighting tariffs.

AUTHOR: That’s right. Collin Jones, owner of Crema Coffee & Bakery in Portland’s Buckman neighborhood, has run his shop since 2011. He has watched coffee prices rise and tariffs arrive but has not yet raised his prices. He told KATU he feels like most of these crises are temporary.

JOURNALIST: Is he being optimistic or just stubborn?

AUTHOR: Maybe both. It’s the most Portland attitude of all: the stubborn faith that quality and community will outlast any policy shock. But the city’s coffee economy rests on a foundation of imported green beans, thin margins, and global supply chains that are suddenly, conspicuously fragile.

JOURNALIST: So after all of that, is Portland’s coffee identity fake? Because if the beans aren’t grown there, and the money is squeezed by tariffs, what are they really selling?

AUTHOR: No. That’s the wrong lesson. It’s real. The cafes are real, the craft is real, the people building these businesses are real. But it’s not self-contained. It depends on farmers, importers, shipping routes, policy, weather, prices — all these things that don’t fit inside the cozy neighborhood story.

JOURNALIST: And once you see those things, does the romance disappear?

AUTHOR: Not exactly. Just less innocent. The rain will keep falling on Division Street. The espresso machine will keep hissing. But the question of how long Portland can remain the coffee capital of America while paying a tariff on a bean it cannot grow is one that no amount of good brewing can answer.

JOURNALIST: I think that’s enough for now. Thank you.
