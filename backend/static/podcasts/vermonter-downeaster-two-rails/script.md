# The Two New England Rails: Vermonter and Downeaster, a Tale of Two Trains

Source blog: The Two New England Rails: Vermonter and Downeaster, a Tale of Two Trains
Slug: `vermonter-downeaster-two-rails`

JOURNALIST: When you look at a map of New England rail, there are two lines that really stand out. One goes north-south, very long. The other goes east-west, shorter, denser.

AUTHOR: Right. The Vermonter and the Downeaster. They sit side by side on Amtrak schedules but they feel like different worlds.

JOURNALIST: That’s the thing. People see them as just trains. But reading your piece, it feels like they’re testing two different theories about how public infrastructure survives.

AUTHOR: Exactly. It’s not just about steel wheels on rails. It’s about how we organize the money and the politics around them.

JOURNALIST: Let’s talk about the money first because that’s where the contrast is sharpest. One route loses millions, one makes a profit on fares.

AUTHOR: Yes. In FY25, the Vermonter posted a $3.6 million operating loss. Total revenue was $9.4 million, but only $3.3 million came from ticket sales.

JOURNALIST: So less than a third of their costs were covered by passengers?

AUTHOR: Correct. Fare recovery dropped to 26 percent. It used to be much higher before the pandemic collapse.

JOURNALIST: And the Downeaster?

AUTHOR: Downeaster hit 50 percent fare recovery in FY24, up to 55 percent recently. Ticket revenue topped $13 million against expenses of $27 million.

JOURNALIST: That sounds like a fundamental difference in demand density.

AUTHOR: It is. The Vermonter is 611 miles long with thin population centers along the Connecticut River Valley. The Downeaster is 145 miles, mostly between Boston and Portland/Maine.

JOURNALIST: So the Vermonter has to serve small towns like Brattleboro or Montpelier where fewer people live?

AUTHOR: Exactly. Average trip distance is much longer for the Vermonter too, often hundreds of miles versus an average of 44 miles per passenger on the Downeaster.

JOURNALIST: But why does the money follow that pattern? Is it just geography or is it policy?

AUTHOR: It’s both. The Downeaster has NNEPRA, a single authority focused only on Maine rail. They have dedicated funding streams from state and federal formula funds.

JOURNALIST: So they can plan long term without asking for permission every year?

AUTHOR: Yes. Multi-year horizons for capital projects. The Vermonter depends on annual appropriations from three separate states—Vermont, Massachusetts, and Connecticut. Each has its own budget cycle.

JOURNALIST: That sounds like diplomatic work rather than engineering work.

AUTHOR: Precisely. Coordination required to sustain a three-state partnership is difficult. It’s not the same as having an empowered authority at the helm.

JOURNALIST: And if one state pulls back, what happens?

AUTHOR: The whole thing stalls. You see this in delays or deferred maintenance. For example, Vermont Rail Advisory Council discussed slow-order spots on New England Central Railroad that won't be addressed until summer 2026.

JOURNALIST: Let’s go back to the route itself though. The Vermonter seems to have had a rougher history physically.

AUTHOR: It did. From 1924 until 1995, the Montrealer carried passengers overnight with Pullman sleeping cars. But Amtrak budget cuts killed it in 1995.

JOURNALIST: So the daytime service started then?

AUTHOR: Yes, but initially it followed a circuitous routing through Palmer on CSX tracks because the historic Boston & Maine mainline fell into disrepair. It added an hour.

JOURNALIST: That’s inefficient for commuters.

AUTHOR: Exactly. Massachusetts changed that beginning in 2009 by pouring $73 million into rebuilding the Connecticut River Line. Track was replaced with continuously welded rail. Signals were upgraded.

JOURNALIST: And that fixed the problem?

AUTHOR: Mostly. On December 29, 2014, the Vermonter rolled into Northampton for the first time in decades. The backup move at Palmer was retired. The reroute saved 25 minutes between Springfield and Brattleboro.

JOURNALIST: But even with that fix, you mentioned ridership is still low compared to Downeaster.

AUTHOR: Ridership peaked at 99,280 in fiscal year 2019 before collapsing to 18,585 in FY21 during the pandemic. In FY25, it carried 115,940 passengers. That’s the busiest since 2005.

JOURNALIST: So it recovered from the pandemic collapse?

AUTHOR: Yes, steadily climbing back. But despite gains, the route remains one of Amtrak’s most heavily subsidized corridors now.

JOURNALIST: Why do you think the Downeaster succeeded where the Vermonter struggles? Is it just population density?

AUTHOR: Geography is partly right, but only partly. The Downeaster has a single-mission authority. NNEPRA is a board appointed by the governor and approved by the legislature. It can plan over multi-year horizons.

JOURNALIST: So it’s less bureaucratic friction?

AUTHOR: Less friction. The Vermonter depends on annual appropriations from three separate states. Connecticut alone contributes $10.6 million annually. The coordination required to sustain a three-state partnership is a diplomatic achievement in its own right.

JOURNALIST: But isn’t that also an advantage? More checks and balances?

AUTHOR: Sometimes. But it makes long-term planning a perpetual negotiation. Which is to say, a permanent exercise in managed disappointment.

JOURNALIST: Let’s talk about where they are going next. Both trains have ambitious expansion plans.

AUTHOR: Yes. Both face barriers that have less to do with engineering than with politics, money, and bureaucratic alignment.

JOURNALIST: Where does the Vermonter want to go?

AUTHOR: Montreal. Thirty miles of existing freight rail track separates St. Albans from Montreal’s Central Station.

JOURNALIST: That sounds close enough.

AUTHOR: Distance is laughably short. But obstacles are absurdly tall. The fundamental challenge is customs. To run a train across the border without stopping every passenger for inspection, both countries must agree on a pre-clearance facility.

JOURNALIST: So passengers get screened before boarding?

AUTHOR: Yes. A combined U.S. Customs and Border Protection and Canada Border Services Agency processing center at Montreal’s Central Station where passengers would be screened before boarding, then free to exit the train at any stop without further inspection.

JOURNALIST: And that doesn’t exist yet?

AUTHOR: Correct. As of early 2026, the project has entered a one-year “Opportunity and Design Analysis.” It is scheduled for completion in mid-2026. But the analysis itself is only a study of a plan for a design of a facility that has been under discussion for more than a decade.

JOURNALIST: That sounds like bureaucratic recursion.

AUTHOR: Exactly. A thing that points to another thing that points to another thing, and none of them is the thing itself. Dan Delabruere, VTrans Rail Bureau Director, told the Community News Service in 2025, “I don’t know.” When asked what the timeline is.

JOURNALIST: And for the Downeaster? Where does it want to go?

AUTHOR: North and east, deeper into Maine. The most concrete near-term prospect is the Rockland Branch Coastal Connection pilot. Extending from Brunswick 56 miles east to Rockland with stops in Bath, Wiscasset, Newcastle, and Waldoboro.

JOURNALIST: Is that easier than Montreal?

AUTHOR: Yes. The track is state-owned. The stations exist in various states of readiness. The question is whether demand justifies the roughly $100 to $200 million in signal upgrades and bridge replacements that full regular service would require.

JOURNALIST: What about Bangor?

AUTHOR: That’s the larger prize. A study commissioned by the Maine Rail Group projected that a twice-daily extension from Portland to Bangor would generate $61.7 million in annual economic benefits for the state of Maine.

JOURNALIST: So $61 million in benefits against what cost?

AUTHOR: Nate Moulton, director of freight operations at MaineDOT, estimated that track and equipment improvements would cost $375 million to $538 million. Existing service covers only about 50% of its costs with passenger fares.

JOURNALIST: So there’s a gap between official skepticism and advocacy optimism.

AUTHOR: Precisely. And the gap between them is precisely the kind of disagreement that further study is designed to resolve.

JOURNALIST: But you mentioned earlier that the Downeaster has been more successful financially.

AUTHOR: It has. In FY25, total expenses were $27 million, $3.1 million below budget. Cost recovery reached 55%, exceeding the 50% target. The café achieved 73% cost recovery.

JOURNALIST: That’s impressive compared to the Vermonter.

AUTHOR: Yes. The Vermonter revenue per passenger dropped $41.26 despite increasing ridership by 9,820. Fare recovery declined from 63.80% to 26.09%. Nolan Buonomano noted this was catastrophic for a route which was once one of the best financial performers.

JOURNALIST: Why did it drop so much?

AUTHOR: Because of the longer distance and thinner revenue base. The Vermonter serves small towns along the Connecticut River Valley where population is thin and stations are far apart. It shares tracks with freight trains that have priority.

JOURNALIST: So on-time performance suffers?

AUTHOR: Yes. The southbound Vermonter departed Northampton on schedule only 3 days out of 353 in 2024, with a median delay of 12 minutes.

JOURNALIST: Does that discourage ridership?

AUTHOR: It does. But geography is not destiny. The Knowledge Corridor reroute proved that strategic infrastructure investment can unlock demand; ridership at the new stations in Northampton and Greenfield grew 84% in the first month after the route change.

JOURNALIST: So if you fix the track, people come?

AUTHOR: Not necessarily. You need the political ecosystem too. NNEPRA is focused, single-mission authority. The Vermonter depends on annual appropriations from three separate states.

JOURNALIST: Is there a lesson here for other cities trying to bring rail back?

AUTHOR: The Downeaster has been more successful because it has been more fortunate in its circumstances: a shorter route, denser population centers, a single-minded governing authority, and a freight railroad that evolved from obstacle into partner.

JOURNALIST: But you also say the gap isn’t fixed.

AUTHOR: No. The same infrastructure investments that transformed the Downeaster are available to Vermont, Massachusetts, and Connecticut. Provided they can sustain the political will.

JOURNALIST: Which sounds easier said than done.

AUTHOR: Yes. The question now is whether the same formula can work for the 30 miles to Montreal, and whether the lessons of one success can be exported to a corridor still waiting for its own.

JOURNALIST: Or whether, as is so often the case in American infrastructure, the answer will be almost, but not quite.

AUTHOR: Exactly. That’s the honest version. Local culture can be real and still depend on the rest of the world. The trains are real. The craft is real. But it isn’t self-contained. It depends on farmers, ports, importers, weather, policy — all the things that disappear when you’re just standing in line for a cappuccino.

JOURNALIST: So is Portland’s coffee identity fake?

AUTHOR: No. That’s too easy. The identity is real. The cafes are real. The craft is real. It depends on global supply chains and local governance.

JOURNALIST: And we have to choose which model we want more of? Single authority or tri-state cooperation?

AUTHOR: We have to choose what fits our geography and politics. If you’re in Maine, NNEPRA works. If you’re the Valley, you need tri-state cooperation. But both struggle against the limits of federal funding and private freight tracks.

JOURNALIST: Which brings us back to the beginning. Two trains, two corridors, two very different trajectories.

AUTHOR: Yes. Two New England rails. One runs out of territory at St. Albans. The other runs into the interior of Maine.

JOURNALIST: And one is waiting for permission to cross the border while the other waits for money to go north.

AUTHOR: Exactly. Both face futures constrained by decisions made — or not made — in state capitols, corporate boardrooms, and a foreign capital.

JOURNALIST: So the story isn’t about trains.

AUTHOR: No. It’s about how we organize public life around steel wheels on rails.
