# The Fractured Mandate: How India's Political Parties Evolved from Independence to Coalition to Dominance

HOST A: It started in a Sanskrit college in Bombay with seventy-two people. Seventy-two social reformers, journalists, lawyers. They launched the Indian National Congress in December 1885.

HOST B: That seems small for an independence movement. Just seventy-two people.

HOST A: Exactly. But they were looking for civic dialogue between educated Indians and the British Raj. Four decades later, that same Congress leads India to independence. But by 2014, the Bharatiya Janata Party achieves a single-party majority unprecedented since 1984.

HOST B: So it went from a small group to a massive party, then fractured, then came back together differently? That sounds like a story about power shifting hands constantly.

HOST A: It is. The report says there are four distinct evolutionary phases. Congress dominance first, then breakdown and regional parties, then coalition era, and finally the return of single-party dominance under BJP.

HOST B: Right. And along the way, major parties split. Congress in 1969, DMK in 1972, Shiv Sena in 2022, NCP in 2023. What drove those splits?

HOST A: Ideological differences, succession disputes, strategic disagreements. But equally important were the reasons parties came together. To counter Congress hegemony in 1977, or to govern through coalitions after 1989.

HOST B: Okay, so understanding these dynamics means tracing not just the parties themselves, but the fractures that define them. Let's start before independence. How did this system emerge between 1885 and 1947?

HOST A: By 1947, the landscape had evolved through three decades of nationalist agitation. Several parties emerged, but none matched Congress's organizational reach. You have the Muslim League for separatism, Hindu Mahasabha for Hindu nationalism.

HOST B: So Congress was the big tent? The others were niche?

HOST A: Yes. Congress positioned itself as secular and inclusive. Its membership included Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, Christians from all castes. In contrast, the Muslim League represented only Muslims, and the Hindu Mahasabha represented only Hindus.

HOST B: That explains why Congress won more seats. If you appeal to everyone, you get more votes than if you appeal to one group.

HOST A: Precisely. Mass mobilization under Gandhi starting in 1920 transformed Congress from an elite organization into a mass movement. Campaigns like the Salt Satyagraha reached villages across India.

HOST B: Wait, so it wasn't just about policy. It was about who showed up on the streets?

HOST A: Exactly. Organizational network matters too. Congress had provincial committees and village-level committees everywhere. Others remained elite organizations with limited geographic reach.

HOST B: Okay, so by independence in 1947, Congress inherited a political vacuum because the Muslim League dissolved after partition and Hindu Mahasabha declined.

HOST A: Correct. They dominated every election until 1977. But even then, internal factionalism existed. The Syndicate—Kamaraj, Morarji Desai, Nijalingappa—represented a conservative right-wing faction.

HOST B: And that tension led to the big split in 1969?

HOST A: Yes. Indira Gandhi was expelled by S. Nijalingappa for indiscipline. She wanted populist policies to mobilize popular support, while the Syndicate advocated right-wing economic policies and closer ties with the West.

HOST B: So it was power and ideology all at once. What happened next?

HOST A: The split created Congress (R) led by Indira Gandhi and Congress (O) led by the Syndicate. In 1971, Congress (R) won 352 seats. Congress (O) fell from 65 to 16 seats.

HOST B: That sounds like a decisive victory for the new leadership.

HOST A: It was. But later, Indira Gandhi's leadership moved toward authoritarianism during the Emergency in 1975. Civil liberties were suspended, opposition leaders arrested.

HOST B: That must have created huge resentment.

HOST A: It did. Mass sterilization programs caused widespread public anger. After the Emergency ended, a coalition of opposition parties—the Janata Party—defeated Indira Gandhi in 1977.

HOST B: So voters punished her directly? Or just wanted democracy back?

HOST A: Both. Voters wanted to test democracy's resilience after the authoritarian turn. The Janata Party merged several groups including Bharatiya Jana Sangh and Congress (O).

HOST B: But that government didn't last long either, right?

HOST A: No. It collapsed in July 1979 due to internal contradictions. Morarji Desai resigned; Charan Singh formed a minority government. Ideological inconsistencies made governance unstable.

HOST B: So the party system wasn't stable even when it changed hands. What happened then?

HOST A: This phase saw the emergence of strong regional parties challenging Congress dominance in their states. You have DMK in Tamil Nadu, TDP in Andhra Pradesh, Shiv Sena in Maharashtra.

HOST B: Wait, so these weren't national parties trying to win everywhere? They were focused on specific regions.

HOST A: Exactly. Dravidian Parties like DMK espoused Tamil nationalism and opposed Hindi imposition. In 1967, DMK became the first party other than Congress to win a state-level election with a clear majority.

HOST B: That changes things. It means you don't need to be a national powerhouse to hold power if you control one state well.

HOST A: Right. The rise of these parties meant Congress could no longer rule every state alone. Even after Indira Gandhi returned in 1980 and her son Rajiv won massive victories in 1984, the map was changing.

HOST B: Then the coalition era begins in 1989. Why did that happen?

HOST A: The National Front government led by V.P. Singh came to power with outside support from BJP and Left parties. They implemented Mandal Commission recommendations on OBC reservations.

HOST B: So they tried to expand representation for lower castes?

HOST A: Yes. But this led to widespread protests. The government collapsed in 1990. Congress returned as a minority government under P.V. Narasimha Rao in 1991.

HOST B: And Rao moved toward economic liberalization?

HOST A: Sharp break from previous leaders. Opening India's economy, dismantling the license raj, introducing market reforms. Congress won only 260 seats in 1991 but governed through coalitions.

HOST B: So no single party could command a majority anymore. That forced them to work together.

HOST A: Exactly. Regional parties became crucial for government formation. You have DMK, AIADMK, TDP, Shiv Sena all acting as kingmakers or governing partners.

HOST B: And during this time, the BJP emerged as the principal challenger to Congress.

HOST A: Yes. The party's Hindutva ideology and mobilization around the Ram Janmabhoomi issue expanded its base. In 1984, it won only two Lok Sabha seats.

HOST B: Then by 2014, they were winning outright majorities? How did that shift happen so fast?

HOST A: Growing discontent with Congress rule, Modi's development-focused campaign, and BJP's organizational strength under RSS. In 2014, BJP won 282 seats outright—a clear majority on its own strength.

HOST B: That was the first instance since 1984 of a single party achieving an outright majority. Did that last forever?

HOST A: Not exactly. In 2024, BJP won 144 seats and did not reach 272 alone. NDA formed government but relied on regional allies for stable governance.

HOST B: So even the dominance phase isn't permanent? It seems like the system keeps fracturing again.

HOST A: Yes. Recent splits reshaped the landscape. Shiv Sena split in 2022 over disagreements on Chief Minister post. Uddhav Thackeray formed alliance with Congress; Eknath Shinde joined BJP.

HOST B: And NCP split in 2023 too?

HOST A: On July 2, 2023, Ajit Pawar broke away from NCP and joined the BJP-Shiv Sena alliance. Driven by ideological differences and power struggles.

HOST B: So splits keep happening because leaders fight for control or strategy.

HOST A: Common themes include succession disputes and strategic alliances. But also identity politics. DMK split involved MGR vs Karunanidhi over party accounts.

HOST B: It sounds like personal power struggles are as important as ideology sometimes.

HOST A: Absolutely. From 1969 to 2023, splits have reshaped the political landscape more than mergers have consolidated it. This reflects India's diversity.

HOST B: No single ideology can hold the country together alone then.

HOST A: Right. Coalitions are not just pragmatic necessities; they are democratic imperatives. The return of single-party dominance under BJP does not signal the end of regional parties.

HOST B: Even when a single party achieves a majority at the center, it must rely on regional allies for stable governance.

HOST A: Yes. The evolution is not linear; it is cyclical. Each phase builds on lessons of the previous one. Congress system collapsed; coalition politics emerged; single-party dominance returned.
