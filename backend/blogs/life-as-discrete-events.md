# Life Is a Collection of Events, Discrete: What the Brain Knows About Time That We Don't

*We experience our lives as continuous narratives — a smooth stream of consciousness from birth to death. But the neuroscientists, the physicists, and the philosophers have been converging on a different picture. The brain does not record a continuous film of experience. It takes snapshots. And the person you think you are may be nothing more than the sum of these discrete event-frames, stitched together by a memory system that edits, deletes, and rearranges without asking permission.*

---

![A 35mm film strip — discrete frames of visual information, the closest analog we have to how the brain segments continuous experience into events. Credit: Runner1616, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons.](/api/blog/images/life-as-discrete-events_film_strip.jpg)

On a warm morning in St. Louis, a volunteer lies inside the loud, whining bore of a 3-tesla fMRI scanner, watching a movie. On the screen, a man makes a pot of coffee — opens the cupboard, takes down a canister, removes the lid, scoops grounds into a filter, fills the reservoir, presses the brew button. The volunteer's task is simple: watch, remember. Nothing more. She does not press any buttons, does not mark any boundaries.

But her brain is pressing buttons she does not know she has.

Inside the scanner, a network of regions — the posterior temporal and parietal cortex, the lateral frontal cortex, a motion-sensitive area called MT+ — is firing in pulses at moments she will later, in a second phase of the experiment, identify as the boundaries between events. The opening of the cupboard door. The clatter of the scoop against the canister. The hiss of the brewer. Her brain, without her instruction, without her awareness, is carving the continuous stream of visual information into discrete chunks — *open cupboard, take canister, scoop grounds, press brew* — each one a self-contained unit with a beginning and an end.

This is Event Segmentation Theory (EST), and it may be the most important cognitive discovery you have never heard of.

[Zacks, Speer, Swallow, Braver, & Reynolds (2007). "Event perception: A mind/brain perspective." *Psychological Bulletin*, 133, 273-293.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2852534/)

[Kurby & Zacks (2008). "Segmentation in the perception and memory of events." *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 12, 72-79.](https://www.gvsu.edu/cms4/asset/92386C7F-BF75-96ED-67D9FAA81701D122/kurby__zacks_2008.pdf)

## The Brain's Cutting-Room Floor

![An MRI scan of the human brain. Event Segmentation Theory identifies specific regions — including the MT+ complex, posterior temporal cortex, and lateral frontal cortex — that fire transiently at event boundaries. Image: National Institutes of Health (public domain).](/api/blog/images/life-as-discrete-events_brain_fmri.jpg)

The central claim of EST is both simple and radical: perception is not the passive reception of a continuous world. It is an active, predictive process that spontaneously segments experience into meaningful units called events. We do not decide where the boundaries fall; the brain decides for us, using a mechanism rooted in prediction error.

Here is how it works. The brain maintains a working memory representation of "what is happening now" — an *event model*. This model generates predictions about what will happen in the next moment. As long as the world conforms to those predictions, the model remains stable. But when something changes — a new movement, a shift in location, a new character entering the scene — prediction error spikes. The brain detects the error, updates the model, and the subjective experience of an *event boundary* occurs. A new event has begun.

Most people assume they perceive the world as it is. They do not. They perceive the world as their brain expects it to be, and they only notice when the expectation fails. The continuous present is a hypothesis that holds until reality gives it a gentle push off a cliff.

[Zacks et al. (2001). "Human brain activity time-locked to perceptual event boundaries." *Nature Neuroscience*, 4, 651-655.](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn0601_560)

The fMRI evidence is striking. In the Zacks et al. (2001) study, participants watched movies of everyday activities while brain activity was recorded. Later, they segmented the same movies into events by pressing a button at each boundary. The brain regions that had shown transient increases in activity during passive viewing — MT+ (motion processing cortex), the posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS), lateral frontal cortex — correlated almost perfectly with the boundaries participants later identified voluntarily. The brain was doing the segmentation work whether the participant was paying attention to event structure or not.

To put it plainly: the brain edits film whether you buy a ticket or not. Event segmentation is not a deliberate analytical act. It is an automatic, obligatory component of normal perception — as involuntary as the blink reflex, as constant as breathing.

[Zacks, Tversky, & Iyer (2001). "Perceiving, remembering, and communicating structure in events." *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 130, 29-58.](https://doi.org/10.1037/0096-3445.130.1.29)

[Radvansky & Zacks (2017). "Event boundaries in memory and cognition." *Current Opinion in Behavioral Sciences*, 17, 133-140.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5734104/)

## Memory's Architecture

If the brain segments experience into discrete events during perception, what happens to those events afterward? They become the architecture of memory.

In a landmark study from the 1970s that still shapes the field, psychologist Darren Newtson showed participants films of everyday activities — a man checking out groceries, a woman assembling a bicycle — and asked them to press a button each time they perceived a meaningful unit ending. Later, participants were shown still images from the films. Some images were taken from event boundaries; others from moments within events. Recognition memory was significantly better for boundary images.

The discovery: event boundaries act as anchors for memory. Information presented at boundaries is encoded more strongly than information from within-event intervals. The moments of transition are the moments the brain bothers to keep.

[Newtson & Engquist (1976). "Foundations of attribution: The perception of ongoing behavior." *New Directions in Attribution Research*, 1, 223-247.]

This effect has been replicated and extended across multiple labs and decades. In one study, Schwan and Garsoffky (2004) edited movies by deleting intervals that contained event boundaries versus intervals between boundaries. Deletions at boundaries impaired memory more severely. In another, Boltz (1992) inserted commercial breaks into feature films — at event boundaries and between them. Commercials placed at boundaries *improved* memory for the film. Commercials placed between boundaries impaired it.

Think about that. A well-placed advertisement can make you remember a film better. There is a lesson here about attention, but not the one advertisers want you to draw.

[Schwan & Garsoffky (2004). "The influence of event structure on memory for film." *Media Psychology*, 6, 163-187.]

[Boltz (1992). "The incidental learning of naturalistic events." *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 18, 808-821.]

The implications are profound. Your memory of your own life is organized not by the clock but by the brain's own event segmentation. The "chapters" of your autobiography are not arbitrary — they reflect the prediction-error spikes your brain used to parse experience as it happened.

In 2013, a team led by Jesse Sargent and Jeffrey Zacks tested 208 adults aged 20 to 79. Participants segmented movies of everyday activities and then took memory tests. The result: event segmentation ability predicted memory performance independently of general cognitive abilities like processing speed, working memory capacity, and fluid intelligence. It was a *unique* predictor — a basic cognitive mechanism that the brain uses to transform perception into recollection. One does not need to be clever to remember well. One needs to know where one thing ends and another begins.

[Sargent, Zacks, Hambrick, Zacks, & Kurby (2013). "Event segmentation ability uniquely predicts event memory." *Cognition*, 129, 241-255.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3821069/)

## What Is an Event, Really?

When the psychologists talk about events, they mean something precise: "a segment of time at a given location that is conceived by an observer to have a beginning and an end." This is how Kurby and Zacks define it in their 2008 *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* review. An event is not a duration measured by a clock; it is a unit defined by perception.

Think about your morning. You did not experience it as a continuous 180-minute flow. You experienced it as a sequence: *alarm off, stumble to kitchen, start coffee, shower, dress, grab bag, out the door*. Each of these is an event. Each has a boundary — a moment when, if asked, you could say "that part ended, and this part began."

The philosopher Donald Davidson argued, beginning in the late 1960s, that events are fundamental particulars — irreducible entities in the ontology of the world. In essays like "The Individuation of Events" (1969) and "Events as Particulars" (1970), Davidson claimed that events are concrete, countable, spatiotemporally located individuals with causal powers. They are not mere conceptual conveniences. They are *real things* — as real as chairs and tables and people.

[Davidson (1969). "The Individuation of Events." In *Essays on Actions and Events*.](http://fitelson.org/125/Davidson_individuation_of_events.pdf)

[Davidson (1970). "Events as Particulars." *Noûs*, 4.](http://www.math-wiki.com/images/f/f6/Donald_davidson.pdf)

Davidson's proposal for individuating events is blunt: two events are identical if and only if they have the same causes and the same effects. This is a criterion rooted in the causal structure of the world, not in human psychology. But the convergence with EST is remarkable. The neuroscientists say the brain segments experience into discrete causal chunks. The philosopher says reality itself may be composed of such discrete causal chunks. The brain, in other words, may not be imposing an artificial structure on a seamless world. It may be *tracking the joints of reality*.

The philosopher and the neuroscientist, starting from opposite ends of the human enterprise, have arrived at the same furniture. The only disagreement is what color to paint it.

## The Universe as a Process of Unique Events

Physics, meanwhile, has been arriving at a similar destination by a different route.

At the Planck scale — a unit of time so small that it would take you ten million trillion trillion trillion Planck times to blink — the smooth geometry of general relativity breaks down. At roughly 10⁻⁴⁴ seconds, spacetime may not be continuous at all. It may be granular, discrete, composed of units as irreducible as pixels in a digital image.

In 2014, Marina Cortês and Lee Smolin published a paper titled "The Universe as a Process of Unique Events" in *Physical Review D*. They proposed a model of quantum spacetime based on "energetic causal sets" — discrete events linked by causal relationships, each labeled by energy and momentum. In this model, spacetime does not pre-exist. It *emerges* from the pattern of discrete events and their causal connections.

[Cortês & Smolin (2014). "The universe as a process of unique events." *Physical Review D*, 90, 084007.](https://journals.aps.org/prd/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevD.90.084007)

Smolin went further in a 2017 paper titled "A Theory of the Whole Universe as a Process of Unique Events." He proposed that the history of the universe is constituted of "views" — attributes of events that capture information about energy and momentum transferred from the causal past. In this ontology, the fundamental entities are not particles or fields or spacetime points. They are *events* — each one distinct, each one with a unique causal role in the universe's history.

[Smolin (2017). "A theory of the whole universe as a process of unique events." arXiv:1712.04799.](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/1712.04799v3.pdf)

If this sounds like Leibniz's Monadology to you, you are not alone — Smolin explicitly cites Leibniz's windowless monads as inspiration. The echoes of Whitehead's process philosophy are audible too. Physics, philosophy, and neuroscience are circling the same intuition: that the world at its most fundamental level is not a continuum but a fabric of discrete happenings.

Three disciplines, three methods, one conclusion. It would be amusing if it were not so unsettling.

## The Self as an Editing Problem

What does any of this mean for who you think you are?

The Event Horizon Model, proposed by Gabriel Radvansky and Jeffrey Zacks in 2017, describes how event boundaries shape long-term memory. One of its central findings is that memory for temporal order is better *within* events than *across* them. You remember the sequence of actions *inside* a given event with reasonable accuracy. But ask yourself to order two events that happened on different days — last Tuesday's lunch versus last Friday's commute — and your performance degrades dramatically.

[Radvansky & Zacks (2017). "Event boundaries in memory and cognition." *Current Opinion in Behavioral Sciences*, 17, 133-140.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5734104/)

This is why autobiographical memory feels less like a film and more like a photo album. You have vivid snapshots from certain moments — the boundary points, the transitions — but the intervals between them are hazy, compressed, sometimes lost entirely. The continuity of self is a construction, assembled after the fact by a brain that had better things to do than record everything. The brain is not a historian. It is a novelist working from notes.

The philosopher Derek Parfit, in *Reasons and Persons* (1984), argued that personal identity is not a matter of a continuous self persisting through time but a matter of psychological connectedness — overlapping chains of memory, intention, and character. If Parfit is right, and the neuroscience of event segmentation suggests he may be, then the self is not a continuous thread but a series of episodes, each partially connected to those that came before.

Your life, at the neural level, is a collection of discrete events — each one carved out by prediction error, each one stored as a separate memory file, each one subject to editing, deletion, or reorganization when you next retrieve it. You are not the author of your autobiography. You are the editor, and a rather unreliable one at that.

## What You Remember Is What You Segmented

The clinical implications are significant. Sargent et al. (2013) found that event segmentation ability declines with age, and that this decline partially mediates age-related memory deficits. Older adults who segment events more normatively — who identify boundaries at the same points as the group average — remember more. Those whose segmentation deviates from the norm remember less.

This raises a strange and unsettling possibility: if your brain segments events poorly, your life will literally have fewer well-defined chapters. The stream will be less structured, the memories less anchored, the autobiographical self less coherent. A life poorly segmented is a life poorly remembered — and a life poorly remembered may be a life poorly lived.

Interventions that improve event segmentation — teaching people to identify meaningful boundaries, cuing them at transition points — have been shown to improve memory for events even after a one-month delay. The implication is that event segmentation is not just a natural process but a trainable skill. You can learn to carve your experience more effectively. You can learn to remember your life better.

[Sargent, Zacks, Hambrick, Zacks, & Kurby (2013). "Event segmentation ability uniquely predicts event memory." *Cognition*, 129, 241-255.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3821069/)

## The Fragile Present

![An hourglass — a visual metaphor for the passage of time that, physics suggests, may be fundamentally granular rather than continuous at the Planck scale. Image: Novosadjanka, CC0/public domain, via Pixnio.](/api/blog/images/life-as-discrete-events_hourglass.jpg)

Perhaps the most disorienting implication is the one we feel but rarely articulate: the present moment is not what it seems.

EST proposes that the brain does not experience "now" as a point but as an event model — a stable representation that persists as long as prediction error remains low. The subjective present is not a dimensionless instant but a window of integration, several seconds long, during which sensory information is bound together into a coherent unit. When prediction error spikes, the window closes, the model updates, and a new present begins.

This means that what you call "the present moment" is actually a brief but bounded event — your brain's best guess at what is happening now, based on what just happened and what it expects to happen next. The present is not a point on a line. It is a segment. And it is constantly being replaced by the next segment, in a process that feels continuous only because the transitions are smoothed over by the very mechanism that produces them.

The present, in short, is a confidence trick that the brain plays on itself — a necessary one, perhaps, but a trick nonetheless.

Christodoulou and Rovelli, in their 2020 paper on the possible experimental detection of time discreteness, proposed that proper time itself — the most basic measure of temporal duration — might be quantized in multiples of the Planck time. If they are right, then the discreteness we experience psychologically is not a quirk of neural architecture but a reflection of the actual granularity of the universe.

[Christodoulou & Rovelli (2020). "On the possibility of experimental detection of the discreteness of time." *Frontiers in Physics*, 8, 207.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physics/articles/10.3389/fphy.2020.00207/full)

[Christodoulou, Di Biagio, & Martin-Dussaud (2022). "An experiment to test the discreteness of time." *Quantum*, 6, 826.](https://quantum-journal.org/papers/q-2022-10-06-826/)

We live inside a cascade of discrete events — from the Planck-scale grain of spacetime up through the event models of the brain to the autobiographical chapters of a life. At every scale, the continuum is an illusion, a useful shorthand for a reality that runs on discrete steps.

## Living in the Edits

What does it mean to live a life that is, at every level, a collection of discrete events?

It means that your sense of a continuous self is an achievement — something the brain constructs moment by moment, event by event, rather than something that simply exists. It means that your memories are not recordings but reconstructions, assembled from the boundary points your brain chose to privilege. It means that the sharpest moments of your life are the transitions — the job changes, the breakups, the moves, the births, the deaths — because those are the moments where prediction error spiked and the brain paid attention.

The sadness of this is matched only by its beauty. We do not remember the steady hours. We remember the door that opened, the hand that waved, the voice that said goodbye.

It means, finally, that the ancient philosophical puzzle of personal identity — the question of what makes you the same person from one moment to the next — has a partial answer in the neuroscience of event segmentation. You are the same person because your brain is constantly maintaining and updating event models that bridge the gaps between discrete perceptual chunks. The self is not a substance. It is a process. And a process is just a sequence of events.
