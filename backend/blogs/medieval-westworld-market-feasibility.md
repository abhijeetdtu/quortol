
# The Medieval Westworld: Can You Build a $40,000-a-Night Immersive Theme Park?

*What a data-driven feasibility study reveals about the limits of immersion, the size of the market, and the long road from Ren Faire to true Westworld.*

---

In March 2018, HBO and the marketing agency Giant Spoon took a ghost town outside Austin, Texas, and performed a remarkable act of resurrection: they turned it into Sweetwater — the fictional frontier settlement at the heart of *Westworld*. For three days, sixty actors performed from a 450-page script in ninety-minute loops, while guests stepped off a train car that had actually appeared in the series and walked into a Wild West that was no longer pretend in any ordinary sense. There were shootouts. There was a saloon. There were horses, whiskey, and a sheriff with a properly tragic backstory. The SXSW activation became the most talked-about experiential marketing event of the year. It was also a perfect paradox — five weeks of labor, three days of glory, and then the ghost town, with the good manners of a guest who knows when to leave, went quiet again [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/2018/3/26/17147584/westworld-hbo-immersive-entertainment-sweetwater-behind-the-scenes-sxsw-2018).

![HBO's recreation of Sweetwater, the frontier town from Westworld, built for a two-acre immersive activation at SXSW 2018. Guests arrived via a Delos shuttle and explored the town for 90-minute loops. Credit: Photo: HBO](/api/blog/images/medieval-westworld_sweetwater_sxsw.jpg)

Now imagine the same idea, but medieval. Not a weekend trick, but a permanent village. Timber-framed cottages, a working blacksmith, a brewery, a chapel, a great hall. Guests arrive in costume, surrender their phones — that most cherished of modern possessions — and spend three days building a life in the 14th century. They can forge a knife, bake bread, ride a horse to a neighboring hamlet, or, if the fantasy absolutely demands it, compete in a tournament.

The question is not whether people would want this. The experiential tourism market is already valued at **$1.1 to $1.3 trillion globally** (Statista, 2024), with paid structured experiences accounting for $250–310 billion of that total [Merca20](https://www.merca20.com/more-than-one-trillion-dollars-the-size-of-the-global-experiential-tourism-market/). Viator's experiences division grew from $55 million in 2020 to $840 million in 2024. GetYourGuide's downloads rose 355% over 2019 levels. The travel industry has reconfigured itself around a quiet but revolutionary discovery: people no longer buy services. They buy stories, emotions, and memories — and they will pay almost anything for the ones that feel real.

The question is whether the supply side can keep up.

---

## I. The Market That Already Exists

The evidence that medieval-themed entertainment possesses durable, cross-generational commercial appeal is not a matter of speculation. It has already been counted.

**Medieval Times Dinner & Tournament** operates nine castles across North America, drawing approximately 2.5 million guests annually. Revenue estimates range from $135 million (RocketReach, 2025) to over $500 million (IncFact, 2025). Each location runs a tightly choreographed two-hour dinner show with jousting, sword fighting, and horses — no overnight component, no deep immersion — and has sustained demand for forty years, which is roughly four decades longer than most cultural phenomena survive [IncFact](https://incfact.com/company/medievaltimesusa-irving-tx/).

![Knights on horseback at Medieval Times Dinner & Tournament. The chain operates nine castles across North America, drawing approximately 2.5 million guests annually. Credit: Phil Guest, CC BY-SA 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons](/api/blog/images/medieval-westworld_medieval_times_arena.jpg)

**Renaissance Faires** have grown into a nationwide industry. The Pennsylvania Renaissance Faire generates an estimated **$8.785 million in economic impact** over its 11-weekend season, drawing 11,000 visitors on sellout days with 815 workers (500 independent vendors, 265 direct employees, 50 performers). Attendance rose 5% from 2022 to 2023 [LancasterOnline](https://lancasteronline.com/business/local_business/pennsylvania-renaissance-faires-economy-paying-real-world-bills-with-elizabethan-fantasy/article_4d2000d4-36f1-11ee-95e4-db0e6d0718e9.html). The Texas Renaissance Festival, the nation's largest, was the subject of a court-ordered **$60 million sale** of its property and assets in 2024, following a succession battle so dramatic that HBO documented it in the series *Ren Faire* — proving that family drama is still more entertaining than any scripted joust [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/as-renaissance-fairs-become-big-business-can-they-retain-their-counterculture-roots-273757).

The growth trajectory is visible even at the grassroots level. The East Tennessee Renaissance Faire grew from **600 to 6,000 attendees in three years**, forcing a move to a larger venue — the sort of problem most businesses would consider a luxury. Chattanooga launched its first Renaissance Faire in spring 2026. The Glastonbury Abbey Medieval Fayre in the UK drew **8,500 attendees** over a single weekend in 2024, its largest ever [Glastonbury Abbey](https://www.glastonburyabbey.com/blogs/events-blog/glastonbury-abbey-medieval-fayre-2024-event-wrap.php). In Ireland, the Youghal Medieval Festival generates an economic spin-off of **€863,000** from a running cost of just **€29,000** — a 29-to-1 return on investment that would make any hedge fund manager reconsider his career, with attendee satisfaction rated 4.86 out of 5 [Irish Independent](https://www.independent.ie/regionals/cork/news/cork-festival-generates-spin-off-close-to-a-million-euro-for-seaside-town-each-year/a1149162328.html).

And then there is **Guédelon Castle** in northern Burgundy, France. Since 1997, a team of paid craftspeople has been building a 13th-century castle using only period tools and techniques — a project so patient and so absurd that it has become the most admired construction site in Europe. The site now attracts **over 300,000 visitors per year** at €12–15 per head, generating approximately **€5 million annually** and supporting 100 staff. It has become a global reference point for medieval building skills: when Notre Dame Cathedral burned in 2019, Guédelon's craftspeople were among the first consulted [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2023/10/01/1200546214/france-medieval-guedelon-castle-burgundy).

![Construction at Guédelon Castle in northern Burgundy, France, where builders and craftspeople use only 13th-century tools and methods. Credit: Cristina Baussan for NPR](/api/blog/images/medieval-westworld_guedelon_castle.jpg)

What all of these models share is a ceiling. None offers multi-day, fully immersive, overnight medieval living. The Renaissance Faire guest returns to a Hampton Inn at night, the illusion dissolving the moment the key card slides into the lock. The Medieval Times visitor drives home after dessert, the taste of turkey leg still fresh and the spell already broken. Guédelon operates as a pay-to-watch museum, not a stay-and-live experience. The overnight medieval immersion product — the Tier 2 experience that sits between a day at the Ren Faire and the science fiction of Westworld's AI hosts — is a vacant niche in a $1.3 trillion market.

## II. The Immersion Gradient: Four Tiers of Feasibility

Not all medieval experiences are equal. The distance from a suburban Renaissance Faire to a true Westworld is not one gap but four, and each has its own cost structure, its own technical demands, and its own brand of willing self-deception.

**Tier 1: The Daytime Spectacle.** This is the existing model. Guests arrive in modern clothes or rental costumes, park in a field, spend 4–8 hours watching staged jousting, shopping at vendor stalls, and eating turkey legs. Anachronisms are tolerated — cell phones, credit cards, modern shoes. Capital costs are low: tents, portable toilets, a temporary jousting list. Labor is seasonal and part-time. The addressable market is mass, with tickets at $30–55 per head. Nearly every major US market already has one.

**Tier 2: The Overnight Immersive.** This is the vacant niche — the space between a pleasant afternoon and a complete abandonment of the present. Guests arrive at a constructed medieval village, check all modern technology at the gate, and receive period clothing and a character role. They sleep in timber-framed cottages with concealed modern plumbing, a small betrayal of the fantasy that everyone quietly agrees to ignore. They eat period meals by candlelight. They interact with trained actors who remain in character for the duration of a 48- or 72-hour "loop." No phones. No cars. No anachronisms. This is the model that Westworld's early episodes depicted before the robots became sentient — a human-staffed immersive theatre experience at luxury resort pricing, with all the dramatic tension that implies.

**Tier 3: The Labor Colony.** Guests do not merely spectate or roleplay — they *work*. They learn to split firewood, milk a goat, thatch a roof, or forge a nail. The experience is marketed as "medieval survival school" rather than pure fantasy, which is a more honest description but a far harder sell. The authenticity ceiling is higher than Tier 2, but the addressable market is narrower. Few guests paying $1,000+ per night want a full day of manual labor in bad weather, and those who do are probably already living on a farm.

**Tier 4: The AI Westworld.** Lifelike robotic hosts, indistinguishable from humans, capable of improvisation, violence, and intimacy. This is the *Westworld* of HBO's imagination — and it is science fiction, not business plan. The CNBC analysis of the fictional park's economics estimated that manufacturing 14,000 hosts at a conservative $1.5 million each would cost **$21 billion**, before any AI development or facility construction [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2016/12/27/what-it-would-actually-cost-to-operate-westworld.html). Current humanoid robotics — Boston Dynamics' Atlas at $1M+ per unit, 330 pounds, barely human-passing — is not close. Disney's free-roaming Olaf robot at Disneyland Paris, a marvel of reinforcement learning, is a 35-inch snowman [The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/tech/895369/olaf-disney-imagineering-frozen-robot-up-close). Tier 4 is not feasible in the foreseeable future, which is probably just as well for the guests.

The realistic frontier is Tier 2: overnight immersion staffed by human actors. The relevant question is what it would cost and whether anyone has been brave enough, or foolish enough, to attempt it.

## III. The Construction Problem: You Can't Build a Village in a Weekend

The romantic assumption is that a medieval village can be assembled from prefabricated materials with a few months of construction. The real-world evidence suggests otherwise, with the quiet authority of a historian who has seen too many ambitious projects fail.

**Guédelon Castle** has been under construction for **27 years** with a full-time paid staff of skilled heritage craftspeople: stonemasons, carpenters, blacksmiths, roofers, painters, gardeners. They work without power tools, using hand-forged nails, hand-hewn beams, and stones quarried on-site. The castle is not yet finished. The project's founders estimate another 10–20 years. The total capital outlay was never calculated — Guédelon operates on a pay-as-you-go model funded entirely by its **300,000 annual visitors**, which makes it perhaps the most honest building project in human history [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2023/10/01/1200546214/france-medieval-guedelon-castle-burgundy).

![Matisse Lacroix, 20, a blacksmith at Guédelon Castle, works a large bellows to make the fire hot enough to melt iron. He forges nails and tools needed for the castle's construction. Credit: Cristina Baussan for NPR](/api/blog/images/medieval-westworld_guedelon_blacksmith.jpg)

**Campus Galli** in southern Germany has been building a Carolingian monastery according to the 9th-century Plan of St. Gall since 2013. The projected timeline: **40 years**. Staff: 20–30. The site operates seasonally — the crew rests from November through February, following medieval custom, which is a lovely gesture toward authenticity and a terrible concession to deadlines [Wikipedia](https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Campus_Galli).

**Burgbau Friesach** in Austria, constructing a medieval castle on a 6.5-hectare site, is financed by European Union cultural funds and staffed by a combination of heritage professionals and labor-market trainees [Burgbau Friesach](https://burgbau.at/?page_id=3192). Several similar projects across Europe — from Büdingen in Germany to Bärnau and Lütjenburg — confirm the pattern: medieval construction with period methods is measured in decades, not years, and measured in patience, not profit.

For a commercial theme park developer, building a medieval village with *modern* construction methods and period-appropriate finishes is the only viable path. The precedent exists in the themed entertainment industry. Disney's Pandora — The World of **Avatar** cost approximately **$500 million** to build. Universal's Wizarding World of **Harry Potter** (first phase) cost approximately **$250 million**. The Diagon Alley expansion added another **$400 million**. Disney's Star Wars: Galaxy's Edge was part of a **$3–4 billion** overall resort expansion [Quartz](https://qz.com/847356/can-you-bring-westworld-to-life-yes-but-it-wont-be-easy).

For a medieval Tier 2 village — 20–30 buildings, a great hall, stables, workshops, a defensive wall, guest cottages, concealed infrastructure — a realistic Phase 1 capital estimate is **$20–50 million**. That is roughly one-tenth the cost of a Harry Potter land, at a fraction of the guest capacity, which is the sort of arithmetic that makes investors either very excited or very nervous.

## IV. The Operating Model: What It Takes to Keep the Fantasy Alive

The SXSW Westworld activation employed **60 actors for a 2-acre park** operating 8 hours a day for 3 days. For a 200-acre medieval village operating 365 days a year with overnight guests, the staffing requirements scale in a way that would make even the most optimistic spreadsheet wince.

A **5-to-1 host-to-guest ratio** appears to be the minimum for credible 24-hour immersion. For 150 overnight guests, that means 750 actors. With three shift rotations (morning, evening, overnight) and days off, the total performer payroll reaches approximately 1,500. At rates comparable to Disney Cruise Line character performers ($65.75/day, a conservative baseline), the daily actor cost alone approaches **$100,000**. Annualized: **$36 million** — before any management, maintenance, kitchen, stable, or administrative staff have been paid, or fed, or given a place to sleep [Quartz](https://qz.com/847356/can-you-bring-westworld-to-life-yes-but-it-wont-be-easy).

The Westworld fictional model priced its experience at **$40,000 per guest per night** (Standard), $75,000 (Silver), and $200,000 (Gold). Those numbers are not as absurd as they first appear when measured against the operating cost structure. At 150 guests paying $2,500 per night — a fraction of the fictional rate, but still more than most people spend on a month's rent — annual revenue reaches approximately **$137 million** at full occupancy, enough to cover the actor payroll, operational costs, and amortize construction over a 10–15 year period. The real constraint is not price but *scarcity*. The SXSW activation generated buzz precisely because it lasted three days. A permanent park must continuously refresh its narrative to sustain demand, which is a much harder problem than pricing.

The other operational constraints are harder to solve. **Weather** limits the season for outdoor medieval experiences with the blunt authority of nature itself. Guédelon closes for winter. Camlann Medieval Village in Washington operates May through September [Seattle Times](https://www.seattletimes.com/pacific-nw-magazine/its-always-1376-a-d-at-this-medieval-village-re-creation-in-carnation/). Even Mediterranean locations face rain and heat. Year-round operation demands a southern US climate — Texas, Florida, Arizona — or substantial indoor covered space that breaks the period illusion, which rather defeats the purpose.

**Liability** for a park involving horses, fire, weapons, livestock, and manual labor is severe in a way that no amount of fantasy can mitigate. Mitchel Kalmanson, an insurance specialist, estimated the annual premium for *Jurassic Park* at **$1.5 million** — and that was before any dinosaur escapes [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2016/12/27/what-it-would-actually-cost-to-operate-westworld.html). A medieval park where guests are actively encouraged to shoot arrows, swing swords, and tend livestock would require bespoke insurance products, extensive waivers, and on-site medical staff — none of which existed in the 14th century, and none of which can be faked.

**Zoning and land use** for a 200-acre commercial recreational village with on-site accommodations is a regulatory marathon that tests the patience of even the most committed developer. Many US counties simply have no zoning classification for "immersive medieval theme park with overnight stays," which is not a category that municipal planning boards tend to anticipate. The owner would likely need a Planned Unit Development (PUD) variance, which can take months or years of negotiation with planning boards. Target states for permissive land-use environments include Texas, Missouri, Montana, and Pennsylvania — states with existing Ren Faire infrastructure and counties accustomed to large seasonal attractions [IC.org](https://www.ic.org/land-use-regulations-urban-planners-and-intentional-communities/) [Survipedia](https://survipedia.com/community/offgrid-legal/).

## V. The Hardest Limit: The Myth of Total Freedom

*Westworld's* central appeal is the absence of consequences. Guests can murder, seduce, and destroy without moral or legal repercussions because the "hosts" are machines — a premise that raises interesting questions about human nature and rather obvious ones about liability insurance. A real medieval theme park cannot offer this. Human actors cannot be harmed, even by consent, which is a legal principle that tends to put a damper on the more imaginative forms of wish fulfillment. Simulated violence — stage combat, paintball, choreographed melees — is the maximum achievable intensity.

The legal framework for immersive experiences with "dark" content is constrained in ways that no amount of medieval atmosphere can circumvent. Fire-breathing, jousting, and staged combat are regulated. Alcohol service to guests in character is standard — Medieval Times serves a full bar — but sexual content at the level depicted in *Westworld* is illegal in most jurisdictions outside of rural Nevada's licensed brothels, which rather limits the scope of what can be promised in the brochure.

This creates a fundamental gap between what the IP promises and what the law permits. The marketing slogan cannot be "Live Your Darkest Fantasies." It must be something closer to: "Experience What It Was Really Like to Live in the 14th Century." Which is a harder sell, and a more honest one.

## VI. The Phased Roadmap

No rational developer would attempt a full-scale Tier 2 village from scratch. The smart path follows three phases, each designed to fail in a small way so that the next one can succeed in a larger one.

**Phase 0 — Site Selection and Entitlement (Year 1):** Identify a rural county with permissive zoning for commercial recreation and on-site accommodations. Secure a PUD variance. Conduct environmental and water rights studies. Cost: $500,000–1 million.

**Phase 1 — The Daytime Attraction (Years 2–3):** Build 5–8 core buildings: a tavern, blacksmith, bakery, market square, and craft workshops. Open as a day-trip destination with 50–80 costumed interpreters, craft demonstrations, and period food service. Ticket price: $40–80. Target annual attendance: 100,000. Revenue: $5–10 million. This proves the market and generates cash flow. Capital cost: $10–20 million.

**Phase 2 — Overnight Immersion (Years 4–5):** Add 20–40 period guest cottages with concealed modern amenities. Launch 1- and 2-night packages at $500–1,500 per person per night, including meals, costume rental, and activities. Capacity: 60–120 overnight guests. Staff-to-guest ratio: 3:1. Additional capital: $10–15 million.

**Phase 3 — Full Narrative Expansion (Years 6–10):** Expand to a full working medieval farm with livestock, crop cycles, and seasonal festivals tied to the medieval calendar. Implement multi-day storylines with guest roles, quests, and faction dynamics. 3- to 5-day packages: $3,000–8,000 per stay. Capacity: 150–300 overnight guests. Actor count: 100–200. Running costs: $20–30 million per year. Total capital to this point: $40–60 million.

For context, the Texas Renaissance Festival — a seasonal Tier 1 attraction with permanent structures, dozens of buildings, an established vendor ecosystem, and 500,000+ annual visitors — was valued at **$60 million** in its 2024 forced sale. That valuation is the best available proxy for what a mid-scale medieval attraction is actually worth, which is either encouraging or sobering depending on how one reads the word "forced" [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/as-renaissance-fairs-become-big-business-can-they-retain-their-counterculture-roots-273757).

## VII. The Open Question

Every existing medieval-themed attraction shares a structural limitation that no amount of investment can fully solve. Camlann's interpreters go home to modern houses. Guédelon's craftspeople commute by car. Medieval Times' knights drive pickup trucks. The Ren Faire queen checks her iPhone between performances. The illusion is maintained for guests but does not extend to the people who operate it, which means the fantasy is always, at some level, a form of theater rather than a form of life.

![Camlann Medieval Village in Carnation, Washington — a living history museum where the year is perpetually 1376 A.D. and interpreters remain in character as 14th-century English villagers. Credit: Ivy Ceballo / The Seattle Times](/api/blog/images/medieval-westworld_camlann_village.jpg)

A Tier 2 immersive village could be built. The capital exists. The market data supports it. The operational model, while expensive, pencils out at luxury price points. The technology — human actors, period construction, concealed modern infrastructure — is available today. The obstacles are not technological. They are human, regulatory, meteorological, and actuarial.

The open question is whether any developer is willing to attempt the hardest part: sustaining a year-round immersive experience at the quality level that guests paying $1,500 per night will expect. The SXSW Sweetwater activation was brilliant because it was ephemeral — three days of intensity, then the set came down, and everyone went home satisfied that they had witnessed something unique. A permanent medieval immersive faces the same challenge as Broadway: every performance must be as good as opening night, and there is no closing night.

The village could be built. The harder question is whether it can be kept alive.
