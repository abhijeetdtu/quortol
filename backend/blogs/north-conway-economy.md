# The Four-Season Economy: North Conway by the Numbers

---

**North Conway, New Hampshire** — On a Saturday in mid-August, the parking lot at Settlers Green fills by 10 a.m. with Massachusetts, New York, and Quebec license plates, each having traveled a considerable distance to prove that a bargain is worth any amount of time spent acquiring it. Up at Cranmore Mountain, mountain bikers queue for the chairlift, which is to say they queue for the pleasure of returning to where they started. At the 1874 train depot, the Conway Scenic Railroad loads a vintage coach for a slow crawl through Crawford Notch — a journey that derives its charm from going nowhere in particular, and therefore pleases everyone. Meanwhile, on West Side Road, hikers set out for the 10.2-mile semi-loop up North Moat Mountain, hoping to beat the afternoon thunderheads — a hope as old as hiking itself, and about as often rewarded.

This is North Conway in the summer of 2026 — a village of roughly 2,300 year-round residents absorbing a seasonal population that, at peak, can feel ten times that size. The scene looks effortless, as it has for nearly 150 years, though an effortless appearance maintained over a century and a half generally requires a great deal of strenuous effort behind the scenes. But the data beneath it tells a more complicated story — one of a small town that has reinvented its economy four distinct times since 1765, and now manages the tension between record tourism revenue and a permanent population that is aging, housing-constrained, and increasingly income-split.

## The $3.56 Billion Engine

The White Mountains region, anchored by North Conway, generates an estimated **$3.56 billion** in annual tourism economic impact [Tourism Stats](https://tourismstats.website/region/whitemountains). At the state level, New Hampshire's tourism industry produced **$7.5 billion in visitor spending** in fiscal 2024 from **14.6 million visitors**, returning roughly **$380 million** in rooms and meals tax revenue [NH Division of Travel & Tourism 2026 Economic Impact Report](https://www.visitnh.gov/getmedia/a10ac1f0-45aa-41df-9cd6-63577fe08043/2026EconomicImpact.pdf). Every dollar the state invests in tourism promotion generates **$15 in revenue** — an extraordinary return, provided one does not examine too closely who earns it and who merely passes it along [SMARInsights, cited in NHBR](https://www.nhbr.com/winter-wonderland-white-mountains-north-country-reporting-a-rise-in-season-visitors/).

The White Mountain National Forest alone draws about **6 million visitors annually** — more than Yellowstone (4.7M) or Yosemite (4.1M) — driven largely by its proximity to major Northeast population centers [UNH News](https://www.unh.edu/news/2025/08/whos-visiting-new-hampshires-white-mountains). The 2020 survey documented a **60% surge in overall WMNF visitation** during COVID and a **nearly 300% increase** in designated wilderness area use, a spike researchers are still analyzing for long-term trends [NHPR](https://www.nhpr.org/nh-news/2025-09-22/they-want-to-what-youre-up-to-in-the-white-mountain-national-forest). It appears that when civilization becomes slightly less comfortable, a great many people discover a sudden passion for the wilderness.

## Employment: Who Works Here

The largest employment sectors in the Conway area tell you everything about the town's economic structure — and nothing that a casual visitor could not guess. According to NH Employment Security's 2024 QCEW data, the town of Conway's **total covered employment** stands at **8,347 workers** across all industries, down slightly from 8,802 in 2014, but with average weekly wages rising from **$607 to $961** over that decade [NHES GraniteStats](https://www2.nhes.nh.gov/GraniteStats/SessionServlet?city=000205&cityName=Conway&page=Community.jsp). A nearly sixty percent wage increase sounds like progress, though it depends rather a lot on whether one's wages were the ones that increased.

Carroll County as a whole posted **20,156 covered employees** in September 2025, with **average weekly wages of $973** — a 4.2% year-over-year increase [BLS QCEW](https://data.bls.gov/maps/cew/NH?chartData=2&distribution=1&geo_id=33000&industry=10&neg_color=orange&ownerType=0&period=2025-Q3&pos_color=blue&showHideChart=show).

The top five employers in the area reflect this mix: **Memorial Hospital** (250–499 employees), **Cranmore Mountain Resort** (250–499), **Conway School District** (274), **Eastern Slope Inn** (110), and big-box retail including **Walmart** (est. 1999) and **Market Basket** (opened 2023) [NHES Community Profile](https://www2.nhes.nh.gov/GraniteStats/SessionServlet?city=000205&cityName=Conway&page=Community.jsp). The town's largest employers, one notes, are the hospital to patch up the visitors, the resort to entertain them, the school district to educate the children of those who serve them, and the retailers to sell them things they did not know they needed.

## The Settlers Green Effect

When **Settlers Green** opened in February 1988 on the site of the former White Mountain Airport, it was the first outlet shopping center in New Hampshire. It had three anchor tenants — J.Crew, Eddie Bauer, and Banana Republic — in 89,000 square feet [Settlers Green Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Settlers_Green). It was, in short, a temple to the proposition that one should pay less for things one does not truly want.

Today, the complex spans **500,000 square feet** across four interconnected zones, with more than 60 stores and an estimated **$125 million in annual sales** [Settlers Green Leasing Package](https://www.settlersgreen.com/assets/documents/OVP-Management-Inc.-Leasing-Package.pdf). The 2023 expansion brought a **70,000-square-foot Market Basket**, along with new-to-market brands like Cotopaxi (its first-ever outlet store) and Swix (its first U.S. operation) [Settlers Green 2023 Highlights](https://www.settlersgreen.com/news/2023-leasing-and-development-highlights).

New Hampshire's **lack of sales tax** is a structural advantage that no amount of marketing could replicate. For Canadian families and Northeastern bargain hunters, Settlers Green is a destination in itself — a place where the absence of a tax feels almost as good as the presence of a sale. Since 1988, the center has donated more than $1.5 million to local nonprofits [Laconia Daily Sun](https://www.laconiadailysun.com/news/state/settlers-green-turns-35-outlet-village-adding-new-concepts/article_113b173a-6446-11ed-894e-f385548678be.html).

## Skiing: The Original Winter Economy

Before the outlets, there was the snow train. The Boston and Maine Railroad operated the first official **snow train** in 1931, and by 1932 trains were delivering skiers directly to North Conway [Cranmore Timeline](https://bigpicture.cranmore.com/pages/timeline). At the peak in the early 1940s, as many as **five trains would arrive on a Sunday**, carrying up to **4,000 skiers** for day trips from Boston — a system that delivered New Englanders to the mountains at a time when the only thing easier than getting there was falling down them.

The resort that caught them was **Cranmore Mountain**, founded by North Conway native Harvey Dow Gibson in 1937. With a **$15,000 WPA grant** (equivalent to ~$324,000 today), Gibson cleared 200 acres of Lookout Mountain and renamed it Cranmore. The **Skimobile** — a sit-car lift on a wooden trestle, invented by local mechanic George Morton — could carry as many as **122,000 passengers per season** before its removal in 1990 [Cranmore Timeline](https://bigpicture.cranmore.com/pages/timeline).

![The Skimobile at Cranmore Mountain in 1939 — a one-of-a-kind sit-car lift on a wooden trestle that carried up to 122,000 passengers per season.](/api/blog/images/north_conway_skimobile.jpg)

By 1939, Gibson had used his influence to secure the release of Austrian ski pioneer **Hannes Schneider** from Nazi Germany. Schneider's arrival cemented North Conway's identity as the "birthplace of American skiing" — a claim the village still makes, and with rather more justification than most such claims possess [New Hampshire Magazine](https://www.nhmagazine.com/navigating-north-conway/). In 2024, USA Today readers named North Conway **"Best Ski Town"** in its 10Best Readers' Choice Awards [NH Business Review](https://www.nhbr.com/the-changing-face-of-cranmore/).

Today, New Hampshire ski areas recorded **2,424,587 visits** in the 2024–25 season, a **3% increase** over the previous year and **6% above the 10-year average** [SKI NH / InDepthNH](https://indepthnh.org/2025/06/10/ski-industry-reports-past-season-up-3-percent-honors-individuals/). Under the Fairbank Group, which purchased Cranmore in 2010, more than **$65 million** has been invested in snowmaking, lifts, and summer attractions including a mountain coaster and downhill mountain bike park. A lift ticket that cost **$29 in 2000** now runs **$134** — an increase that seems steep until one remembers that $29 in 2000 bought a kind of innocence about what skiing would eventually cost [NewEnglandSkiHistory.com](https://www.newenglandskihistory.com/NewHampshire/cranmore.php).

## The Other Three Seasons

North Conway's economy is described as "four-season," and the visitor data bears that out — though it also reveals that some seasons are more equal than others.

![Seasonal occupancy rates and average daily rates for short-term rentals in North Conway show the dramatic swing between peak summer and shoulder seasons.](/api/blog/images/north_conway_seasonal_str.png)

**Summer** (June–August): Peak hiking season. Short-term rental occupancy can exceed **75–80%** in July and August, with average daily rates of **$333–$362** [StaySTRA](https://staystra.com/location/new-hampshire/north-conway/). The 2025 July 4th weekend saw **81% of businesses** reporting "good" or "great" performance in a Mt. Washington Valley Chamber of Commerce survey [Patch](https://patch.com/new-hampshire/across-nh/businesses-report-steady-july-4th-mt-washington-valley-fewer-canadian-guests). Eighty-one percent is encouraging, though one suspects the remaining nineteen percent had opinions worth hearing.

**Fall** (September–October): Foliage season drives some **3.7 million visitors** statewide, spending an estimated **$1.8 billion** [Laconia Daily Sun](https://www.laconiadailysun.com/real_estate/fall-foliage-fuels-new-hampshire-s-economy/article_aa06c4c3-18be-4f2d-a198-3962bd428a92.html). October occupancy for short-term rentals sits around **47%**, with ADRs of **$361** — high for a shoulder month, which is to say that New England's leaves command a premium that its weather could never quite justify.

**Winter** (December–March): Ski season is the economic backbone. February occupancy for STRs hits **56%**, with the highest ADRs of the year at **$392** [StaySTRA](https://staystra.com/location/new-hampshire/north-conway/). The 2023–24 winter advertising campaign spent **$538,000** and produced an estimated **64,000 trips** generating **$83 million in visitor spending** [NHBR](https://www.nhbr.com/winter-wonderland-white-mountains-north-country-reporting-a-rise-in-season-visitors/).

**Spring** (April–May): The true shoulder. April occupancy drops to **25.9%** and ADR falls to **$315**. Revenue per rental unit in April averages just over **$2,000**, compared to **$6,800** in August — a swing of more than 3x (The August figure reflects observed StaySTRA data; the stated summer occupancy range of 75–80% would suggest a higher theoretical value, indicating that data sources and aggregation methods may differ.) [StaySTRA](https://staystra.com/location/new-hampshire/north-conway/). It is the season that tourism officials speak of hopefully, and that everyone else endures.

## The Demographic Paradox

Here is where the numbers get uncomfortable — and, for those who enjoy a paradox, rather interesting.

North Conway's permanent population in 2026 is estimated at **2,271 residents** by World Population Review, down from a modeled 2020 estimate of 2,410. (The official 2020 Decennial Census recorded 2,116.) The year-over-year decline from 2025 to 2026 was -0.22% [World Population Review](https://worldpopulationreview.com/us-cities/new-hampshire/north-conway). The wider town of Conway, by contrast, grew slightly to **10,296** in 2024, up from 9,822 in 2020 [U.S. Census QuickFacts](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/conwaytowncarrollcountynewhampshire/PST045222).

The **median age in North Conway** is **61.8 years** — nearly 23 years older than the national median of 38.9. In the town of Conway, **35.1% of residents are 65 or older** [U.S. Census QuickFacts](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/conwaytowncarrollcountynewhampshire/PST045222). The under-18 population is just **12.4%**. This is a community growing older faster than it attracts young families — a demographic trajectory that resembles a retirement party no one thought to end.

The **median household income** in North Conway is **$71,198** — about 90% of the national median. Per capita income is **$54,337**, slightly above the state average [World Population Review](https://worldpopulationreview.com/us-cities/new-hampshire/north-conway). But the **poverty rate is 15.65%**, nearly double New Hampshire's statewide rate of 7.3% [Census Reporter](https://censusreporter.org/profiles/16000US3353860-north-conway-nh/). That is a striking figure for a community at the center of a $3.56 billion tourism economy — though perhaps not so striking when one considers that tourism economies are designed to move money through a place, not to leave it there.

**Housing data** tells part of the story. Of the **1,816 housing units** in the North Conway CDP, roughly **38.6% are vacant** at any given time, and **34.3% are seasonal or vacation properties** [NeighborhoodScout](https://www.neighborhoodscout.com/nh/conway/north-conway). This is not blight — it is a resort housing market. But it means that more than a third of the village's physical plant sits empty for large portions of the year, contributing to an affordability squeeze for year-round workers. It is, in short, a housing crisis dressed up as real estate appreciation.

## What the Numbers Don't Show

The data captures flows — visitors, dollars, workers, housing units. It is less good at capturing the structural tensions of a four-season resort economy, perhaps because tensions are harder to count than dollars.

**Canadian visitation**, which historically made up a significant share of North Conway's summer and fall traffic, has remained "soft" since the pandemic. In the 2025 July 4th Chamber survey, **nearly half of businesses** reported seeing fewer Canadian travelers than in previous years [Patch](https://patch.com/new-hampshire/across-nh/businesses-report-steady-july-4th-mt-washington-valley-fewer-canadian-guests). The gap has not been fully closed by increased visitors from New York and New Jersey — a reminder that even the most successful resort economy depends on the willingness of other people to cross borders.

**Infrastructure strain** is another persistent concern. Businesses cited **traffic** as the most frequent visitor complaint, followed by **parking shortages** and **restaurant wait times** [Patch](https://patch.com/new-hampshire/across-nh/businesses-report-steady-july-4th-mt-washington-valley-fewer-canadian-guests). The village's road network — essentially two-lane NH Route 16 / US 302 — was not designed for peak foliage weekend volumes, which is to say it was designed for a time when people had more patience and fewer places to be.

**Climate volatility** poses a longer-term risk. Cranmore's average annual snowfall of **86 inches** varies dramatically — from a low of **29 inches in 2015–16** to a high of **141 inches in 2016–17** [OnTheSnow](https://www.onthesnow.com/new-hampshire/cranmore-mountain-resort/historical-snowfall). The 2023–24 season brought 102 inches; 2024–25 delivered just 66. Snowmaking infrastructure has made the resort more resilient, but warmer winters remain the industry's central unspoken anxiety — the thing everyone discusses in terms of "investment in snowmaking capacity" when what they mean is "we are hoping the planet cooperates."

## The Long Arc

What makes North Conway's economy unusual is not the numbers themselves but the durability of the pattern. The village has been a tourism destination since the 1820s, when newspaper engravings of the Wiley Slide avalanche drew the first summer visitors. It became an **art colony** in the 1850s when Benjamin Champney bought a house on what is now Main Street; more than **400 artists** passed through over the following decades, their White Mountain School paintings serving as de facto tourism advertising — which is to say that art, even then, was pressed into the service of commerce, and bore it remarkably well. The **railroad arrived in 1872** and the depot — that Russo-Victorian confection designed by Nathaniel J. Bradlee — opened in 1874. **Skiing arrived in the 1930s** via Cranmore and the snow trains. **Outlet retail arrived in 1988** with Settlers Green [New Hampshire Magazine](https://www.nhmagazine.com/navigating-north-conway/).

Each wave built on the previous one. The artists advertised the landscape. The railroad made it accessible. The ski industry added an entire winter season. The shopping center added a reason to come even when the weather was marginal. Today, North Conway is one of the few places in New England where a visitor can plausibly spend a long weekend skiing, hiking, riding a scenic railroad, and buying discounted Patagonia jackets — all within a 3-mile radius. It is a place that solved the problem of what to do with a landscape by giving people an endless variety of ways to consume it.

The numbers will continue to evolve. The 2025 White Mountain National Forest Visitor Use Monitoring survey, conducted by UNH researchers, will provide fresh data on whether the COVID-era visitation surge has normalized or reset baseline demand [NHPR](https://www.nhpr.org/nh-news/2025-09-22/they-want-to-what-youre-up-to-in-the-white-mountain-national-forest). The state's "Wildly Responsible" campaign, launched in 2025, represents an attempt to manage the environmental footprint of record crowds — an admirable goal, and one that sits in the same uneasy relationship with tourism as moderation does with appetite.

But the underlying structural reality appears stable: a small, aging permanent population serving a much larger transient one, generating billions in economic activity while grappling with housing shortages, infrastructure limits, and the quiet precarity of a model that depends, every season, on the weather, the exchange rate, and the willingness of 4 million people a year to get in the car and drive north. It is a system that works beautifully, provided one does not examine it too closely — which, to be fair, is true of most beautiful systems.
