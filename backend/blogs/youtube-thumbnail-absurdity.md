# The Economics of Absurdity: Why Your YouTube Feed Looks the Way It Does

---

You meet it at midnight, thumb hovering over the feed: a man whose mouth is open wider than anatomy politely permits, an eyebrow parked in geostationary orbit, a red arrow gesturing at something the viewer will never be allowed to see, and beneath it all a title in full capitals — THE PRESIDENT RESIGNED!!! You click, because the alternative is the eternal scroll, and the video is a thirty-second commercial for a stock-trading app. YouTube's own enforcement guidance would call that thumbnail a textbook offense: it names "the president resigned!" as the model of a title that promises what the video never delivers [Google India Blog, Dec 18, 2024](https://blog.google/intl/en-in/products/platforms/strengthening-enforcement-against-egregious-clickbait-on-youtube/). The absurdity is not a malfunction. It is the merchandise.

More than 20 million videos are uploaded to YouTube every day, and Shorts alone now averages over 200 billion daily views; in the United States, YouTube has been the top streaming service by watch time for nearly three years, per Nielsen as of January 2026 [YouTube for Press](https://blog.youtube/press/). Attention is the scarcest commodity the media has ever traded, and the thumbnail is the wrapper in which all of it is sold. What looks like a civilization-wide descent into idiocy is really a market finding its equilibrium — and the paper trail is remarkably tidy: a 2024 study in the Journal of Business Research that examined 16,215 video covers, a research corpus of 38,517 posts — each linking to a news headline — judged by five raters apiece, and years of YouTube's own policy documents, in which the platform first invents the incentive, then apologizes for it, then polices it, and finally sells you the instrument for continuing to do it.

---

## 1. The most expensive three seconds in media

Every video on YouTube competes in an auction it never consented to enter, and the bidding unit is the fraction of a second a thumb lingers over a grid of images. The recommendation system decides what rises to the surface by reading how viewers respond — their watch history, likes, dislikes, and satisfaction surveys — with external evaluators brought in to judge quality [How YouTube Works: Recommendations](https://www.youtube.com/howyoutubeworks/product-features/recommendations/). The practical consequence is brutally simple: a video that is not clicked does not exist. It might as well have been uploaded to a locked drawer. Every cover therefore bids in a market where the prize goes to the loudest voice, and the press statistics confirm the sheer volume of the market [YouTube for Press](https://blog.youtube/press/).

## 2. What the data says about the loudest bidder

In 2024, Geng Cui, Yuho Chung, Ling Peng, and Qiaofei Wang published "Clicks for money: Predicting video views through a sentiment analysis of titles and thumbnails" in the Journal of Business Research. Examining 16,215 YouTube video covers, they found that strong sentiments in thumbnails — positive or negative alike — drive views; that strong sentiment in captions does the reverse; that titles with positive rather than negative sentiment generate more views; and that sentiment markers such as punctuation, all capitals, and emoticons all nudge the click [EconPapers / Journal of Business Research 183 (2024), DOI 10.1016/j.jbusres.2024.114849](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbrese:v:183:y:2024:i:c:s0148296324003539). The authors explain this through cognitive schema theory: a thumbnail is not read but matched — an instant template-check against the viewer's existing mental furniture, which is exactly why a man mid-scream, a red arrow, and three exclamation points outclass subtlety.

The phenomenon predates YouTube's golden age of absurdity. The Webis Clickbait Corpus, assembled by researchers in the Webis group, collected 38,517 posts — each linking to a news headline — from 27 major US news publishers between November 2016 and June 2017; five annotators rated each on a four-point scale, and the majority judged 9,276 of them — roughly a quarter — to be clickbait [Webis-Clickbait-17](https://webis.de/data/webis-clickbait-17.html), [DOI 10.5281/zenodo.5530410](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.5530410). That was merely the news. The commercial video industry, as two further research teams observe, has an even starker structure: "captivating descriptions and intriguing thumbnails designed to increase the number of views, and thereby increase the revenue for the person who posted the video" create "an incentive for people to post clickbait videos, in which the content might deviate significantly from the title, description, or thumbnail" [arXiv:2107.12791](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.12791); meanwhile "unscrupulous content creators... trick users into clicking on their videos to increase their advertisement revenue" [arXiv:2112.08611](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.08611). Note what the detectors had to do merely to keep pace: an ensemble model reached 92.89% accuracy on the BollyBAIT dataset and 95.38% on the Misleading Video Dataset [arXiv:2112.08611](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.08611). Catching the bait demands a machine learning pipeline of surgical precision — because the bait is the norm.

## 3. The algorithm that asked for it

The absurdity was never a bug in the machine; the machine advertised for the position. In January 2019, YouTube's official blog confessed that "viewers were getting frustrated with clickbaity videos with misleading titles and descriptions ('You won't believe what happens next!')" and that the company had answered by updating its recommendation system "to focus on viewer satisfaction instead of views," measuring likes, dislikes, surveys, and time well spent, "all while recommending clickbait videos less often" [YouTube Blog, Jan 25, 2019](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/continuing-our-work-to-improve/). It was an official admission of the structural tension that defines the platform: the algorithm rewards engagement, the algorithm penalizes cheap engagement, and creators must somehow know the difference.

Five years later, YouTube began selling the weaponry. Its official A/B testing feature — "Test & Compare" — lets creators run up to three titles and thumbnails against one another, and crowns a winner based on the combination that earns the highest watch time [YouTube Help: A/B test titles & thumbnails](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16391400), [YouTube Help: Test & compare thumbnails](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13861714). The tool's own documentation acknowledges the tightrope: "Great titles and thumbnails serve an important purpose beyond getting viewers to click... so that they don't waste their time clicking on the wrong videos," and so the platform optimizes "for overall watch time over other metrics, like click-through-rate" [YouTube Help: A/B test titles & thumbnails](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16391400). In other words: refine your packaging, but do not refine it too well — which is precisely the instruction every headline writer on earth has ignored since the invention of the headline. To complete the circle, YouTube now offers Ask Studio, which will generate custom thumbnails for you with artificial intelligence [YouTube Help: Add custom thumbnails](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/72431). The platform has become its own thumbnail factory.

## 4. The regulator arrives, on a schedule

The platform's own policy documents form an escalating confession. Its spam policy bans "malicious clickbait": "using maliciously misleading titles, thumbnails, descriptions, or imagery to trick users into clicking on a video that does not deliver what was promised" — with the example of a title promising a full sports match over a video that shows only a clip [YouTube Spam Policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2801973). On December 18, 2024, Google announced it was "strengthening our efforts to tackle egregious clickbait on YouTube," defining it as content where "the title or thumbnail includes promises or claims that aren't delivered within the video itself, especially when that content focuses on breaking news or current events," because it "can leave viewers feeling tricked, frustrated, or even misled" — enforcement would begin slowly in India, and initial removals would come without strikes while creators learned the rules [Google India Blog, Dec 18, 2024](https://blog.google/intl/en-in/products/platforms/strengthening-enforcement-against-egregious-clickbait-on-youtube/).

Then, on July 15, 2025, YouTube renamed its "repetitious content" monetization policy to "inauthentic content" and clarified that it covers content "that is repetitive or mass-produced" — the AI-slop provision [YouTube channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392). The same page outlaws "unsatisfying or off-putting content," including "deceptive or misleading imagery or narratives, such as realistic visuals tricking viewers into believing a fake celebrity death or natural disaster has occurred," and, for children's content, anything that "uses titles or thumbnails to trick kids into thinking it's educational, when it's really not" [YouTube channel monetization policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392). Read as a corpus, the rules amount to an admission: the incentives the platform built produced a genre of packaging so dishonest that the platform now needs a second set of rules, and a machine learning army, to unmake it.

## 5. The absurdist equilibrium

Here the reporting hands over to interpretation, and the interpretation is this: absurdity is the terminal stage of a mature attention market. Once shock saturates the feed, each marginal unit of surprise is worth a little less every year — the mouth must open wider, the arrow must point harder, the stakes must be higher ("the president resigned!"), until the only escalation left is the one nobody expected: honesty about absurdity. The deadpan title — the video that promises to spend a hundred days living inside a supermarket and means it — out-absurds the shouter precisely because it declines to shout. The data even explains why: titles with positive sentiment outperform negative ones, and the strongest sentiment of all may be the affectless declaration of the plainly ridiculous [EconPapers / Journal of Business Research 183 (2024)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbrese:v:183:y:2024:i:c:s0148296324003539). It is a market equilibrium with a sense of humor, which is to say it is the only equilibrium the attention economy has ever produced that is worth watching.

## 6. The viewer's three seconds

The human scale survives all of this, and it is small: one viewer, one thumb, three seconds, two hundred billion times a day [YouTube for Press](https://blog.youtube/press/). Every one of those verdicts is a small economic decision, and the market has been honest with us about what it wants. It wants the open mouth and the red arrow; it wants the hundredth "you won't believe this"; it wants, increasingly, the serene deadpan of a title that promises a man living in a supermarket and delivers exactly that. The thumbnail is the folk art of the attention economy — garish, desperate, frequently dishonest, occasionally sublime — and like all folk art, it tells the truth about the people who make it and the people who click it. The absurdity was never the problem. It is the market's way of telling us what we actually wanted all along: to be briefly, gorgeously lied to, and then, at last, to be told the truth about the lie.
