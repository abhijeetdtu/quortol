# The Ancient Cure: What Modern Clinical Trials Have Discovered About a 2,000-Year-Old Philosophy

---

On a Tuesday morning in 2021, a dead man spoke. He was Epictetus — former slave, celebrated philosopher, and for roughly 1,900 years, unavailable for comment. Yet his voice, preserved in the *Discourses* and the little handbook called the *Enchiridion*, reached forty-five high worriers scattered across the United Kingdom who had just completed an unusual eight-day experiment. Their instructions: read a passage from a Stoic text, summarize it, then complete three exercises rooted in the ancient discipline. Predict misfortune. Examine your judgments. Assess your day.

The results, published in *Cognitive Therapy and Research*, one of the field's most respected journals, were striking enough that the lead author, Alexander MacLellan, used language that researchers rarely permit themselves. The Stoic training group showed an 18% reduction in rumination — the tendency to dwell on sad, depressive, or hopeless thoughts, and one of the strongest predictors of future depression. Self-efficacy, the belief in one's ability to cope with stress, increased by 15%. The language participants used in their daily journals grew measurably less anxious over the training period. [MacLellan & Derakshan, *Cognitive Therapy and Research*, 2021 — DOI: 10.1007/s10608-020-10183-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10608-020-10183-4)

This was not a study of a new drug, a novel therapeutic technique, or a cutting-edge digital intervention. It was a trial of a philosophy that reached its peak of influence during the Roman Empire — a philosophy the academic establishment had, until very recently, treated as a historical artifact rather than a living tool. The trial was small — only 45 participants — and its authors hedged their conclusions with the caution of scholars who know their colleagues are watching. But it was a randomized controlled trial, the gold standard of clinical evidence, and it tested something that had never been rigorously tested before: whether the practical exercises of an ancient school of thought could produce measurable improvements in mental health.

The results, as it turned out, were not an anomaly. They were the first evidence in a cascade that has since become one of the most surprising stories in modern psychotherapy — a story in which the science is catching up to a wisdom that has been waiting, quietly, for two millennia.

## The Direct Line

The present begins in 1956. That year, at the annual convention of the American Psychological Association in Chicago, a psychologist named Albert Ellis delivered a paper titled "Rational Psychotherapy" that would fundamentally alter the course of mental health treatment. Ellis's central argument was radical for its time: emotional disturbances are largely caused not by events themselves, but by the irrational beliefs people hold about those events. Change the beliefs, Ellis argued, and the emotional suffering follows. [Ellis, A. (1957). Rational Psychotherapy. *Journal of General Psychology*, 59, 35-49.](https://albertellis.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/Rational-Psychotherapy.pdf)

Ellis was explicit about where this insight came from. In his landmark 1962 book *Reason and Emotion in Psychotherapy*, he wrote that "many of the principles incorporated in the theory of rational-emotive psychotherapy are not new; some of them, in fact, were originally stated several thousand years ago, especially by the Greek and Roman Stoic philosophers." He credited Epictetus directly, quoting the philosopher's famous dictum: "Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of them." [Ellis, A. (1962). *Reason and Emotion in Psychotherapy*. Lyle Stuart.]

Seven years later, another psychiatrist, Aaron T. Beck, published *Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders*, which became the founding text of what we now call cognitive behavioral therapy — the most empirically validated form of psychotherapy in existence. Beck, too, opened his book with an epigraph from Epictetus. In his introductory chapter, he wrote that "the philosophical origins of cognitive therapy can be traced back to the Stoic philosophers." [Beck, A. T. (1976). *Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders*. International Universities Press.]

The lineage is not a matter of speculation or scholarly interpretation. It is explicit, acknowledged, and documented in the founding texts of both major branches of cognitive behavioral therapy. As the philosopher Donald Robertson has documented in his comprehensive study *The Philosophy of Cognitive-Behavioural Therapy*, when Stoicism and CBT are reduced to their essential components, the foundational framework is identical: cognitive activity influences emotion and behavior; we can examine and change that cognitive activity; doing so alleviates psychological suffering. [Robertson, D. (2020). *The Philosophy of Cognitive-Behavioural Therapy*, 2nd ed. Routledge.]

A 2020 academic paper in the journal *Ēthikē* traced this lineage in detail, demonstrating how Epictetus's emphasis on the distinction between what is within our power and what is not — the famous "dichotomy of control" — became the cornerstone of Ellis's Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy. [Mantzanas, M. K. (2020). Philosophical Psychology: The Philosophical Theory of Epictetus and its Influence on the Psychological Theory of Albert Ellis. *Ēthikē*, 5, 17-28. — DOI: 10.12681/ethiki.22686](https://doi.org/10.12681/ethiki.22686)

And yet for decades, this was treated as an interesting historical footnote — a curiosity rather than a clinical program. It would take another sixty years and a new generation of researchers to ask the question that, once asked, seems inevitable: If the philosophy inspired the therapy, why not test the philosophy itself?

## The Evidence Arrives

The 2021 MacLellan trial was the opening move. But its small sample size and lack of a fully independent control group meant it could only suggest, not prove.

Then came the breakthrough.

Georgie King, a trainee clinical psychologist at Royal Holloway, University of London, conducted the first methodologically rigorous randomized controlled trial of Stoic training — a two-week online program involving three daily Stoic practices and readings every other day. One hundred nineteen participants were randomly assigned to either the Stoic training group or a waitlist control. Assessments were conducted at baseline, immediately after the intervention, and at one-month follow-up. The results, published in King's 2024 PhD thesis, were the strongest evidence yet that Stoic practice can produce meaningful clinical change. [King, G. (2024). Modern Stoicism and Mental Health: A Randomised Controlled Trial. PhD thesis, Royal Holloway, University of London.](https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/en/publications/modern-stoicism-and-mental-health-a-randomised-controlled-trial/)

The training group showed significantly lower depression and experiential avoidance — the tendency to flee from uncomfortable inner experiences — at both post-intervention and follow-up. Anxiety was significantly reduced at follow-up. Clinically significant improvement in depression occurred in 38% of the training group immediately after the program and 34% at one-month follow-up. For anxiety, 25% showed clinical improvement post-training, rising to 32% at follow-up. Effect sizes were medium to large — a technical way of saying that the changes were not merely statistically significant but practically noticeable in people's lives.

At roughly the same time, a separate team at Hull York Medical School published a mixed-methods study in *BMC Medical Education* testing a Stoic-based online training package called SeRenE — Stoic Reflection for Resilience and Empathy — on third-year medical students. The rationale was urgent: empathic erosion and burnout are well-documented crises in medical education, and existing psychological training had not fully addressed them. Twenty-four students completed twelve days of Stoic training co-developed with psychotherapists. The results were significant across multiple measures. Stoic ideation increased (d = 0.64). Resilience increased (d = 0.50). Empathy increased with a large effect size (d = 0.99). [Brown, M. E. L. et al. (2022). Can stoic training develop medical student empathy and resilience? A mixed-methods study. *BMC Medical Education*, 22, 355. — DOI: 10.1186/s12909-022-03391-x](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-022-03391-x)

The qualitative data from the medical student study was perhaps even more telling. Students reported that the Stoic practice of negative visualization — deliberately imagining worst-case scenarios — helped them develop what the researchers called "empathic bravery": the courage to sit with patients' suffering without being overwhelmed by it. They described a sense of emotional preparedness that mindfulness training, with its emphasis on present-moment awareness, had not given them. "Thinking forwards and preparing for what could go wrong engaged students in planning and fostered a sense of self-control and self-efficacy," the authors wrote — core domains of personal resilience that are associated with academic success and professional effectiveness.

A separate line of research out of Birkbeck, University of London, published in 2021, also found significant correlations between increases in Stoic ideation and reductions in negative affect, alongside increases in positive affect and self-efficacy, lending support to the idea that Stoic practice builds psychological resilience through measurable mechanisms. [MacLellan, A. & Derakshan, N. (2021). Effects of Stoic Training and Adaptive Working Memory Training on Emotional Vulnerability in High Worriers. — DOI: 10.1007/s10608-020-10183-4](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/id/eprint/41952/)

## The Largest Trial Yet

The evidence base is about to grow substantially larger. In November 2025, a proposed randomized controlled trial by Alexander MacLellan (now at the University of Bath) and Michael W. Lewis (Hamilton College) received in-principle acceptance from *Peer Community in Registered Reports* — a rigorous publication model in which a study is accepted for publication before results are known, based solely on the quality of its methodology. The trial will recruit a minimum of 270 undergraduate students from three U.S. universities — Oregon State, California State Long Beach, and La Salle University — screened for high levels of worry and randomly assigned to either a Stoic training group or an active control condition. Over two weeks, participants will complete ten structured training sessions delivered via a smartphone app, with ecological momentary assessments of mood throughout. [MacLellan, A. & Lewis, M. W. (2025). The effects of Stoic philosophy training on resilience, mental health, and wellbeing in students: a randomised controlled trial. *Peer Community in Registered Reports*. — OSF: xndyj](https://osf.io/xndyj/)

This is the first large-scale, well-powered, multi-site trial of Stoic training. It will measure resilience, well-being, self-efficacy, worry, brooding, fear of negative evaluation, and dysfunctional attitudes — a comprehensive battery of outcomes that will either confirm or complicate the promising signal from earlier studies. The trial will also examine whether changes in Stoic attitudes mediate improvements in mental health, addressing one of the key unanswered questions in the field: is it specifically the adoption of Stoic beliefs that drives the benefits, or is something else at work?

## What Stoicism Actually Is

The most common misunderstanding about Stoicism — the one every researcher in this field encounters — is the confusion between philosophical Stoicism and what the Stoics themselves would have called "apatheia" misconstrued as emotional suppression. The colloquial meaning of "stoic" — stiff upper lip, gritted teeth, the suppression of feeling — is not only different from philosophical Stoicism; on many measures, it is its opposite.

The Stoic Attitudes and Behaviours Scale (SABS), developed over a decade by an interdisciplinary team of philosophers and psychologists and published in 2025 in *Cognitive Therapy and Research*, provides the first validated instrument to measure authentic Stoic practice. The scale was tested on over 8,000 participants across 116 countries. It identifies seven dimensions of genuine Stoicism: beliefs about control (recognizing what is and is not within our power), beliefs about happiness (understanding that well-being depends on character, not externals), Stoic mindfulness (paying close attention to one's judgments), the practice of virtue (wisdom, courage, justice, self-control), benevolence and compassion, ethical development, and a Stoic worldview that places one's life in a broader perspective. [LeBon, T. et al. (2025). The Development and Validation of the Stoic Attitudes and Behaviours Scale. *Cognitive Therapy and Research*, 50, 207-228. — DOI: 10.1007/s10608-025-10635-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10608-025-10635-9)

The results were unambiguous. People who scored high on philosophical Stoicism reported greater life satisfaction, stronger resilience, lower anger, and lower anxiety. People who scored high on colloquial "stiff upper lip" stoicism — measured by a separate scale — reported poorer outcomes. The two measures were negatively correlated, meaning that the more a person endorsed authentic Stoic attitudes, the less likely they were to engage in emotional suppression. [LeBon et al., 2025, as summarized in *Psychology Today*, September 2025](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/365-ways-to-be-more-stoic/202509/how-the-science-of-stoicism-can-boost-your-well-being)

This distinction has practical implications. A 2024 paper in *Frontiers in Psychology* on Stoic-based resilience training for military service members and public safety personnel explicitly warns against "pseudo-stoicism" — the culture of emotional hardening that has historically dominated these professions — and argues that authentic Stoic practice, focused on functional reconnection and values-based action, offers a more effective path to psychological resilience. The paper introduces the Functional Disconnection/Functional Reconnection (FD/FR 2) model, which has been embedded in an existing training program called Before Operational Stress (BOS), early evidence suggests it improves mental health outcomes in first responders. [Ioachim et al. (2024). Strengthening the military stoic tradition. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 15:1379244.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1379244/full)

Even the relationship between Stoicism and mindfulness has been empirically explored. A 2024 study in the journal *Mindfulness* found a strong positive correlation between Stoic attitudes and mindfulness traits (r = 0.65), stable across two separate measurement periods. The authors concluded that "these two conceptual frameworks share previously unexplored similarities" — suggesting that Stoicism, with its roots in the Western classical tradition, may offer a culturally familiar entry point to many of the same benefits that Eastern contemplative practices provide. [Cavanna et al. (2024). Live the Present Moment! — Developing the 100-I-K Scale to Examine the Relationship Between Mindfulness and Stoic Attitude. *Mindfulness*, 15, 2860-2874.](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12671-024-02468-6)

## The Therapy the Philosophy Built

None of this research exists in a vacuum. It sits atop one of the largest evidence bases in all of clinical psychology. Cognitive behavioral therapy — the direct intellectual descendant of Stoic philosophy — has been tested in hundreds of randomized controlled trials involving tens of thousands of patients. A comprehensive 2023 meta-analysis published in *World Psychiatry*, the flagship journal of the World Psychiatric Association, analyzed 409 randomized controlled trials of CBT for depression involving 52,702 patients — the largest meta-analysis ever conducted of any specific psychotherapy for any mental disorder. The results: CBT showed moderate to large effects compared to control conditions (g = 0.79), effects that remained significant at 6- to 12-month follow-up. Response rates were 42% for CBT versus 19% for control conditions. CBT was as effective as antidepressant medication in the short term and more effective at 6 to 12 months. [Cuijpers, P. et al. (2023). Cognitive behavior therapy for depression: a comprehensive meta-analysis. *World Psychiatry*, 22(1), 105-115. — DOI: 10.1002/wps.21069](https://doi.org/10.1002/wps.21069)

The philosophical premises that CBT operationalized — that our interpretations of events, not the events themselves, drive our emotional responses; that those interpretations can be identified, examined, and restructured; that doing so alleviates suffering — were stated with remarkable clarity by Epictetus in the first century of the common era. The evidence that they work is now among the most robust findings in the mental health literature. The ancient philosophy did not merely inspire the modern therapy. It anticipated its central insight so precisely that a first-century slave could have walked into a twenty-first-century therapist's office and recognized the entire framework.

## What Remains Unknown

The evidence is promising, but it is not yet conclusive. The existing trials are small by pharmaceutical standards. The largest published study involved 119 participants; the largest ongoing trial aims for 270. Drug companies routinely test new antidepressants on thousands of patients. The Stoic training studies also rely heavily on self-report measures, which are vulnerable to expectancy effects — participants who volunteer for a Stoicism study may want to believe it works. The active control condition in the upcoming MacLellan trial — summarizing fiction — is designed to address this, but the concern is legitimate.

There are also open questions about mechanisms. Is the benefit specific to Stoic practices, or would any structured reflective practice produce similar results? Do the effects last beyond one month? Which of the seven SABS dimensions drives the most improvement? These are the questions that the next generation of research — larger trials, longer follow-ups, active control groups — is designed to answer.

The MacLellan and Lewis trial, currently recruiting across three U.S. universities, will provide the first well-powered test. The researchers have committed to open science practices — preregistration, data sharing, Bayesian analyses — that should allow the field to move beyond the preliminary signal toward a clearer picture of what Stoic training can and cannot do.

## The Quiet Return

There is something quietly remarkable about the fact that this research is happening at all. Stoicism is not a new intervention developed by a pharmaceutical company or a tech startup. It is a philosophy that reached its zenith under Marcus Aurelius, that was absorbed into Christian thought, that receded into the background of intellectual life for centuries, and that has re-emerged in the twenty-first century not through the efforts of a marketing department but through the slow, painstaking work of clinical researchers who noticed something interesting in the data.

The Stoics themselves would have appreciated the irony. They taught that external events — including the rise and fall of philosophical schools — are not ultimately what matters. What matters is the judgment we bring to those events. If Stoicism is experiencing a renaissance, it is because a growing body of evidence suggests that its ancient techniques for examining and reforming those judgments actually work — not as a matter of doctrine, but as a matter of data.

Epictetus, who began his life as a slave and ended it as one of the most influential teachers of the ancient world, would not have been surprised that his words found their way into a randomized controlled trial nineteen centuries later. He would have been surprised only that it took so long.
