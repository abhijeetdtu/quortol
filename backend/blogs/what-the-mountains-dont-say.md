# What the Mountains Don't Say: A Journey Through Appalachia's Two Americas

![A little spinner in a Georgia Cotton Mill, 1909. Photograph by Lewis Wickes Hine, Library of Congress, National Child Labor Committee Collection. No known restrictions on publication.](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/pnp/nclc/01600/01638r.jpg)

Consider two photographs, separated by seventy-five years and six hundred miles — and by every comfortable assumption Americans hold about industrial progress.

The first is a Lewis Hine image from 1909, shot inside Bibb Mill Number One in Macon, Georgia. A row of adolescent girls stands at cotton-spinning frames, faces smudged, hair pulled back, bodies caught in the repetitive geometry of textile machinery. Hine's caption notes that "some boys were so small they had to climb up on the spinning frame to mend the broken threads." The youngest workers in that mill were ten years old ([Library of Congress, 1909](https://www.loc.gov/item/2018675038/)).

The second photograph was taken by Arthur Rothstein in July of 1938, a Farm Security Administration image of a steelworker inside a Pittsburgh mill. The man stands alone against the immense darkness of the industrial shed, his face lit by the glow of a furnace, arms crossed, body braced against heat that must have been unbearable ([Library of Congress, 1938](https://www.loc.gov/item/2017776765/)).

These two images — the child textile worker of the Georgia Piedmont and the steelworker of the Monongahela Valley — tell the origin story of modern Appalachia with a directness no statistic can match. They are the twin poles around which the region's economic soul has been stretched for more than a century. And they explain, with a clarity no policy paper can claim, why the mountains today contain two Americas moving in opposite directions.

## The Map That Never Stays Still

The Appalachian Regional Commission, created by President Lyndon Johnson in 1965 to study and improve the economic conditions of the region, divides its territory into five subregions: Northern, North Central, Central, South Central, and Southern Appalachia. These designations, revised in 2009, account for "topography, demographics, economics, and transportation" — a bureaucratic phrase whose principal accomplishment is to conceal a landscape of extreme internal difference ([ARC, Subregions](https://www.arc.gov/map/subregions-in-appalachia/)).

The region stretches 200,000 square miles from southern New York to northern Mississippi. It includes all of West Virginia and parts of twelve other states. But the ARC's five-part taxonomy, however useful to administrators, obscures a simpler and more revealing division — the one between North and South, with Central Appalachia (eastern Kentucky, southern West Virginia, southwestern Virginia) caught between them as a third region that has experienced the deepest distress of all ([City Journal, 2023](https://www.city-journal.org/article/appalachias-north-south-divide); [USDA Rural America, Vol. 17 Issue 4](https://ers.usda.gov/sites/default/files/_laserfiche/publications/46984/19349_ra174d_1.pdf?v=23738)).

## The South: Where the Mountains Grow Young

Of all the Appalachian subregions, Southern Appalachia is the one that least resembles the popular image of a region in decline. Its population grew by 13.2 percent between 2010 and 2023 — a rate that actually surpassed the national average. Certain counties in Appalachian Georgia grew by more than 20 percent ([ARC, Population and Age](https://www.arc.gov/about-the-appalachian-region/the-chartbook/appalachias-population/)).

![Trapper boy working in the Turkey Knob Mine, Macdonald, West Virginia, 1908. Photograph by Lewis Wickes Hine, Library of Congress. No known restrictions.](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/pnp/nclc/01000/01062_150px.jpg)

This growth is no accident. It is the product of a demographic shift that has been half a century in the making. The 1990s, in particular, marked a reversal of historic patterns: for the first time in living memory, more people moved into Appalachia than left it, and the overwhelming majority of those new arrivals settled in the southern tier. Between 1995 and 2000, over 1.2 million people moved into Southern Appalachia from outside the region, while the northern subregion lost nearly 100,000 more people than it gained ([ARC, Emerging Patterns of Population Redistribution, 2005](https://www.arc.gov/report/emerging-patterns-of-population-redistribution-and-migration-in-appalachia/)).

The reasons are not mysterious. The same Sunbelt migration that transformed Atlanta, Charlotte, and Nashville has spilled over into the mountain counties that surround them. The sprawl of greater Atlanta now reaches into the north Georgia mountains, bringing subdivisions, distribution centers, and coffee shops to places that a generation ago were still dominated by cotton fields and textile mills. The Great Smoky Mountains, the Blue Ridge Parkway, the retirement magnets of western North Carolina and eastern Tennessee — all have drawn people southward, chasing warmer weather, lower costs, and the particular beauty of the southern highlands.

Southern Appalachia's median household income is the highest of any subregion, and income per capita is rising faster there than anywhere else in the region ([PRB, New Data Reveal Appalachia's Economic Improvements, 2025](https://www.prb.org/resources/acs-chartbook-2025/)). Its poverty rate dropped by nearly two percentage points between the 2014-2018 and 2019-2023 survey periods, with the most dramatic declines in Appalachian Georgia, Kentucky, Tennessee, and Virginia ([ARC, Income and Poverty](https://www.arc.gov/about-the-appalachian-region/the-chartbook/income-and-poverty-in-appalachia/)).

But beneath the encouraging headlines, a deeper vulnerability persists — one the southern economy has carried since the nineteenth century.

## The Textile Inheritance

The textile mill was the original engine of industrialization in the American South — and it was, from the very beginning, a low-wage, low-skill, low-productivity proposition. Unlike the industrial North, which generated its own technology and its own entrepreneurial class, the South industrialized by borrowing the already-routinized methods of others. Southern mill owners competed on cost, not innovation. They built their fortunes on cheap land, cheap labor, and the absence of union organization ([UNC Global Research Institute, The Roots of Southern Deindustrialization](https://gri.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/246/2019/03/The-Roots-of-Southern-Deindustrialization-Coclanis-Carlton.pdf)).

An ARC study makes the pattern unmistakable: between 2000 and 2010, North Carolina's manufacturing employment dropped by 44 percent — from 761,000 jobs to 429,000. The furniture industry lost nearly 60 percent of its workforce. Textiles lost two-thirds ([ARC, Creating Regional Advantage](https://www.arc.gov/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/CreatingRegionalAdvantageinAppalachia.pdf)).

Lewis Hine's photographs from 1909 to 1914 show us the human face of this original industrialization. In one image from Tifton, Georgia, the entire A.J. Young family — mother and six children — stands outside the cotton mill where they all work. The mother earned $4.50 a week; the children collectively earned the same. The husband had died, leaving her with eleven children. The family had left the farm two years before to work in the mill ([Library of Congress, 1909](https://www.loc.gov/item/2018674997/)).

That family's story — the movement from rural poverty to mill-town poverty — is the foundational narrative of Southern Appalachia's industrial experience. And when those mills closed, starting in the 1990s and accelerating through the 2000s, the region lost not just jobs but an entire way of life.

Yet Southern Appalachia has diversified more successfully than its northern counterpart. The auto parts industry has expanded dramatically, with plants in Alabama, Georgia, and Tennessee supplying the foreign-owned assembly lines that now ring the region. Capital investment in auto parts manufacturing in some Appalachian states has been 40 to 100 percent higher than the national average ([ARC, Analysis of Global Competitiveness of Industry Clusters](https://www.arc.gov/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/AnalysisofGlobalCompetitivenessofSelectedIndustriesClusters.pdf)). The carpet industry concentrated in Dalton, Georgia, has held on.

And, increasingly, the southern mountains have become a destination. Retirees, remote workers, and second-home buyers have transformed once-sleeping towns like Asheville, North Carolina; Greenville, South Carolina; and Chattanooga, Tennessee into booming small cities. The same scenery that once isolated the region now sells it.

## The North: Where the Mountains Grow Old

If Southern Appalachia is growing younger, Northern Appalachia is aging at a rate that should unsettle anyone who lives there. In 2000, 16 percent of Northern Appalachia's residents were already 65 or older — a proportion that made it one of the oldest regions in the United States, comparable to Florida. The net out-migration of young adults has been underway for decades, leaving behind an increasingly elderly population ([PRB, The Aging of Appalachia](https://www.prb.org/resources/report-the-aging-of-appalachia/)).

![Jones and Laughlin steel mills, Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania, circa 1900-1915. Detroit Publishing Company, Library of Congress. No known restrictions on publication.](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/pnp/det/4a10000/4a10104r.jpg)

The numbers tell a brutal story. Between 1990 and 2000, Northern Appalachia grew at an annual rate of 0.06 percent — essentially zero. In the early 2000s, it tipped into actual population decline. The northern subregion's metropolitan areas shrank by 2 percent in the 1990s, even as the rest of the country boomed. Only the South grew ([ARC, Emerging Patterns, 2005](https://www.arc.gov/wp-content/uploads/2005/04/EmergingPatternsPopulationRedistributionandMigrationinAppalachia1.pdf)).

The steel mills that made Northern Appalachia the industrial heart of America are gone or transformed. The Homestead Works, once the crown jewel of Andrew Carnegie's empire, closed in 1986. The Duquesne Works shut down in 1984. The Jones and Laughlin mills, which covered acres of Pittsburgh's South Side with smokestacks and blast furnaces, are now parking lots and riverfront apartments. The Library of Congress photograph of the Homestead Steel Works — a panoramic view from around 1905 showing an almost unimaginable expanse of industrial infrastructure, smoke rising from a dozen stacks against a pale sky — is a document of a lost world ([Library of Congress, 1900-1915](https://www.loc.gov/item/2016802468/)).

These losses were not replaced. Between 1998 and 2003 alone, manufacturing employment in Appalachian New York, Pennsylvania, and Ohio declined by 3 to 4 percent of total employment — a staggering figure that represents entire towns losing their economic reason for existence ([ARC, Creating Regional Advantage](https://www.arc.gov/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/CreatingRegionalAdvantageinAppalachia.pdf)).

The consequences are visible in the diseases of despair data. In 2022, the combined mortality rate from overdose, suicide, and alcoholic liver disease was 152.9 deaths per 100,000 in Central Appalachia — nearly double the rate in Southern Appalachia (80.2 per 100,000). North Central Appalachia was almost as bad, at 145.7 per 100,000. The opioid crisis has hit this part of the region with particular ferocity: West Virginia had an opioid-related overdose mortality rate of 104.7 deaths per 100,000 in 2023, more than three times the national rate ([ARC, Appalachian Diseases of Despair Update, 2025](https://www.arc.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Appalachian-Diseases-of-Despair-Update-2025.pdf)).

A photograph from 1908 shows a boy called a "trapper" — his job was to open and close ventilation doors in the Turkey Knob Mine in MacDonald, West Virginia. The boy had to stoop because the ceiling was so low. The picture was taken more than a mile underground. The caption notes that the boy was still a child ([Library of Congress, 1908](https://www.loc.gov/item/2018673782/)). A century later, the mines that employed him are largely closed, and the region is still searching for something to take their place.

## Central Appalachia: The Hardest Hit

No part of the region has suffered more than Central Appalachia — the coal country of eastern Kentucky, southern West Virginia, and southwestern Virginia. Here, the economic story is simpler and more devastating than either the North's or the South's.

Coal production in Central Appalachia has collapsed. In 2001, the region produced nearly 270 million short tons of coal. By 2021, that figure had fallen to about 55 million short tons — a decline of 80 percent. The coal seams that remain are thinner, deeper, and more expensive to extract than the ones that built the industry. Worker productivity in Central Appalachian mines fell by half between the early 2000s and 2020 ([ARC, Coal Production and Employment, 2022](https://www.arc.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/2022-09-Coal-Production-and-Employment-in-Appalachia.pdf)).

The consequence is not merely economic but demographic. Central Appalachia's population has been bleeding outward for decades. During the 1990s, when the rest of Appalachia was actually gaining population from in-migration, Central Appalachia continued to lose people. Young adults — the educated, the ambitious, the ones who might have rebuilt the region — left in disproportionate numbers. And those who stayed were often the ones with the fewest options ([ARC, Emerging Patterns, 2005](https://www.arc.gov/wp-content/uploads/2005/04/EmergingPatternsPopulationRedistributionandMigrationinAppalachia1.pdf)).

The diseases of despair data for Central Appalachia are the worst in the region by almost every measure. In 2022, the subregion had the highest mortality rates for overdose, suicide, and alcoholic liver disease. It also had the lowest educational attainment in Appalachia: the share of adults with a bachelor's degree in Central Appalachia was 19.5 percentage points below the national average ([ARC, Education](https://www.arc.gov/about-the-appalachian-region/the-chartbook/education-in-appalachia/)).

A photograph from around 1900 shows a mule-drawn coal car outside a Kentucky mine, the animal standing placidly beside the track while a miner looks on. The image captures the pre-industrial character of an industry that would soon be completely transformed — and that would eventually leave behind the region it had enriched ([Library of Congress, c. 1900](https://www.loc.gov/item/2012648310/)).

## What the Mountains Don't Say

The mountains themselves are neutral. They take no side in any economic argument. The same topography that made Southern Appalachia a destination for tourists and retirees made Northern Appalachia a transportation corridor for steel and coal. The same ridges that isolated Central Appalachia from the mainstream economy also preserved cultural traditions that the rest of America has long since discarded.

What the mountains do not say — what the statistics reveal but the landscape conceals — is that the region's fate has never been determined by geography alone. It has been shaped by industrial strategies, by political decisions, by migration patterns, by the relentless movement of capital toward cheaper labor and newer markets.

Southern Appalachia is not succeeding because its mountains are more beautiful or its people more industrious. It is succeeding because it happens to lie in the path of the great American migration to the Sunbelt, because Atlanta's exurban sprawl has reached its foothills, because the climate is milder and housing cheaper than in the North. These are accidents of geography and history, not moral virtues.

Northern Appalachia is not failing because its people have lost their work ethic or their ingenuity. It is failing because the industrial system that sustained it for a century — the mills, the mines, the factories — has moved on, and nothing has yet taken its place. The same forces that built Pittsburgh into the "Steel City" also, in time, dismantled it.

Central Appalachia is not suffering because coal was a mistake. Coal was, for generations, the only game in town — and when the game changed, the region had no backup plan. The same cheap energy that powered American industry for a century was extracted at a human cost the nation has never fully reckoned with.

In 1909, Lewis Hine photographed a young girl working alone at a spinning frame in a Georgia cotton mill. She is small, barely tall enough to reach the machinery. The light from the mill windows falls across her face, which is expressionless — not sad, not angry, just tired. The caption says only: "A little spinner in a Georgia Cotton Mill" ([Library of Congress, 1909](https://www.loc.gov/item/2018675038/)).

She is the ancestor of every Appalachian story that followed. She is the beginning of the textile economy that built the South and the forerunner of the mill closures that undid it. She is the first chapter in a long, unfinished story about what happens when a region ties its fate to an industry that does not care about it.

The mountains remember. They hold the bones of the miners, the rusting machinery of the mills, the abandoned company stores and the empty downtowns. But they do not tell us what comes next. That is a story the people who live there are still writing.
