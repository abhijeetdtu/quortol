# The Bench Economy: What the IPL Pays Its Invisible Men

---

Mumbai, April 2026. In a hotel room somewhere in the city's satellite belt, a 24-year-old fast bowler sits on the edge of a bed that cost more than his parents' first car. The television is on, tuned to the match he is not playing in. His teammates are on screen, chasing 185 under lights at Wankhede. He is in full cricket kit because the rules require him to be ready for a moment that will only arrive through another man's misfortune. He is the standby. The reserve. A player whose career advances only when someone ahead of him collapses.

His contract says he earns ₹30 lakh this season — the new base price for uncapped players in IPL 2025, raised from ₹20 lakh in previous cycles [IPL Governing Council, 2024](https://sportstar.thehindu.com/cricket/ipl/ipl-news/ipl-auction-rules-overseas-players-earnings-cut-base-price-hiked-capped-uncapped-categories-details/article68698420.ece). If he plays every match, he collects another ₹7.5 lakh per game — ₹1.05 crore on top, as per the match fee system introduced by BCCI secretary Jay Shah in September 2024 [ESPNcricinfo](https://www.espncricinfo.com/story/ipl-2025-bcci-introduces-match-fees-for-players-1453001). If he never takes the field — and roughly 40% of contracted players see zero or near-zero game time any given season — he gets the ₹30 lakh and nothing else. The league pays him for his availability. Whether he is ever needed is a question the contract declines to answer.

The IPL is a league of 10 teams, each permitted to carry between 18 and 25 players [Sporting News, 2024](https://www.sportingnews.com/in/ipl/news/ipl-2025-mega-auction-rules/3793968cadf1a875f79a8515). That is roughly 230 contracted cricketers per season. On any given match day, 110 take the field (including the Impact Player substitute). The rest watch. Some of them are millionaires, like Krishnappa Gowtham, who was paid ₹9.25 crore by CSK in 2021 to play zero matches [Times Now, 2021](https://www.timesnownews.com/amp/sports/cricket/ipl/article/from-anrich-nortje-to-robin-uthappa-strongest-playing-xi-for-players-who-didnt-play-single-match-in-ipl-2021/755836). Some of them are young men from towns like Rewa, Madhya Pradesh, who signed their first IPL contract and became overnight the most famous person in a 50-kilometre radius. What unites them is a peculiar transaction: they are paid, sometimes handsomely, to be invisible.

This is the bench economy of the IPL — a parallel financial system within cricket's richest league, where the commodity being traded is not performance but presence. It raises a question the league's celebratory narrative prefers to ignore: what is the actual cost, measured in years and careers and the slow attrition of promise, of being the 18th man in a 25-man squad?

---

## The Mathematics of Exclusion

The arithmetic has a quiet cruelty about it. Before the introduction of the Impact Player rule in 2023, each match consumed 11 players from a squad of up to 25. That left 14 on the bench. The Impact Player rule — which allows teams to substitute one player mid-match — expanded the match-day group to 12, narrowing the theoretical bench to 13 [IPL Governing Council, 2024](https://www.iplt20.com/news/4109/ipl-governing-council-announces-tata-ipl-player-regulations-2025-27). In practice, the bench is often larger on game day because teams carry additional reserves and net bowlers.

Over a 14-match league season, a franchise will typically use 16 to 18 players. The remaining 7 to 9 — roughly a third of the squad — will have minimal or no on-field participation. Some of these players go entire seasons without stepping onto the field. Some go multiple seasons.

Nishant Sindhu was part of the Indian Under-19 World Cup-winning squad in 2023, bought by Chennai Super Kings at the auction. He spent three consecutive IPL seasons — 2023, 2024, and 2025 — on the books of CSK and later Gujarat Titans. He has never played a single IPL match [CricTracker, 2025](https://www.crictracker.com/cricket-appeal/3-players-who-were-bought-multiple-times-but-never-played-a-single-ipl-match-4428/). Kyle Mills, the New Zealand pacer, was bought twice — once by Kings XI Punjab in 2008 and again by Mumbai Indians in 2009. Zero matches [CricTracker, 2025](https://www.crictracker.com/cricket-appeal/3-players-who-were-bought-multiple-times-but-never-played-a-single-ipl-match-4428/). Pavan Deshpande was signed by Royal Challengers Bengaluru in 2018 and again in 2020. He never played [CricTracker, 2025](https://www.crictracker.com/cricket-appeal/3-players-who-were-bought-multiple-times-but-never-played-a-single-ipl-match-4428/).

These are not fringe cases. They are structural outcomes of a system where squad size exceeds match-day capacity by more than 100 percent.

## What the Bench Pays

In 2022, N Jagadeesan sat in Chennai Super Kings' dugout for two complete seasons before making his debut. He had been bought at his base price of ₹20 lakh in 2018. "Being on the bench definitely made me doubt myself, a hundred percent," he told Wisden India [Wisden, 2022](https://www.wisden.com/cricket-interviews/exclusive-jagadeesan-being-on-the-bench-at-csk-made-me-doubt-myself-i-would-keep-thinking-if-i-was-good-enough). "I would keep thinking if I was good enough and if I would be capable of playing."

The doubt vanished the night he finally played — 33 runs off 28 balls against Royal Challengers Bangalore in Dubai, 2020. But he played only six more games for CSK. In 2021, he was back on the bench full-time. Then he was released. "On a personal point of view, yes, I could only wish that I had a lot more game time," he said. But he added, speaking of CSK's stacked lineup and his role as a wicketkeeper-batter, "I knew for a fact that the combination was not going to suit me."

Jagadeesan's story captures a paradox at the heart of the bench economy. A player can understand the logic of his exclusion perfectly and still be hollowed out by it. Reason and self-doubt do not share a dressing room. The doubt returns every practice session when the XI is pinned to the board and his name is not on it.

Arjun Tendulkar experienced the same paradox across five seasons with Mumbai Indians. Signed in 2021 at ₹20 lakh, re-signed in 2022 at ₹30 lakh, and again in 2025 at ₹30 lakh, he played exactly five matches in five years — four in 2023, one in 2024, zero in 2025. Asked in 2026 whether he felt he deserved more opportunities, he did not equivocate. "Who doesn't feel that?" he told Shubhankar Mishra's podcast [Times Now, 2026](https://www.timesnownews.com/sports/cricket/no-one-wants-to-sit-on-bench-arjun-tendulkar-breaks-silence-on-not-playing-enough-for-mumbai-indians-article-153973103). "No one wants to sit on the bench."

The bluntness of that line — "no one wants to sit on the bench" — is striking precisely because it states aloud what the league prefers to leave implied. The IPL bench is not a finishing school or a development incubator, though franchises often frame it that way. It is a holding pen. Players are acquired, stored, and occasionally deployed. The ones who never get called up absorb the season in hotel rooms and practice nets, kept match-fit for a moment that may never arrive.

Adam Zampa, the Australian leg-spinner who took 6 for 19 on his IPL debut for Rising Pune Supergiant in 2016, described the experience in starker terms. "I went through all kinds of emotions, right from how good it is just to be a part of it to I am sick of this. Just get me home. I am not playing" [The Final Word Cricket Podcast, reported by SportsTak, 2024](https://thesportstak.com/cricket/indian-premier-league-22-mar-2024/story/i-am-sick-of-this-just-get-me-home-not-playing-adam-zampa-ipl-experience-rues-not-getting-multi-million-dollar-3054143-2024-08-11).

Zampa's frustration carried a specific sting. After his six-wicket haul, he expected to be a regular. He was not. Rajasthan Royals had R Ashwin and Yuzvendra Chahal in the spin department. There was simply no room. "I never got a multi-million dollar payday from the IPL," he said.

## The Financial Gradient

The bench economy operates on a steep financial gradient, and the gradient is what makes the arrangement tolerable — or at least negotiable.

At the top: a capped Indian player retained for ₹18 crore who sits out half the season earns ₹18 crore anyway. The contract is the contract. The IPL salary system states plainly that a player available for the full season is entitled to their full salary "regardless of the number of matches they play or are selected for" [Times of India, 2024](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/sports/cricket/ipl/top-stories/ipl-salary-system-explained-10-key-points-you-need-to-know/articleshow/115626093.cms). A franchise that releases a player before the contract expires must pay the full term.

At the bottom: an uncapped player at the minimum base price of ₹20 lakh (raised to ₹30 lakh in 2025) who sits the entire season takes home ₹20 lakh. That is life-changing money for a 22-year-old from a tier-2 cricket city. It is also roughly what a Ranji Trophy regular earns over a full domestic season of match fees — ₹40,000 to ₹60,000 per day, across 8 to 10 four-day matches, plus List A and T20 tournament fees [Gully Cricket, 2026](https://gully.cricket/indian-cricket-player-salary-breakdown/). A top domestic-grade player might earn ₹8 to ₹20 lakh in annual retainer under the MCA's new domestic contract system introduced in April 2026 [GK365, 2026](https://gk365.in/current-affairs-articles/sports/mca-domestic-player-contract-system-2026/).

The IPL bench, even unpaid a single match fee, doubles that domestic income — for two months of work, most of it spent watching.

This is the bargain. The player trades match practice, career development, selection leverage, and the irreplaceable experience of performing under pressure for a cheque that changes his family's financial trajectory. It is a rational choice. It is also, in the long run, a costly one.

## The Opportunity Cost of the Bench

Shardul Thakur, now a senior India international, spent IPL 2016 on the bench at Kings XI Punjab before being released mid-season. He was Mumbai's highest wicket-taker in the Ranji Trophy that year but did not play a single match in the IPL. "It is frustrating because if you ask anyone in the squad, I was working as hard as anyone else in the training sessions," he told ESPNcricinfo [ESPNcricinfo, 2016](https://www.espncricinfo.com/story/feeling-of-being-left-out-hurts-me-shardul-thakur-1010975). "It is the feeling of being left out which is hurting me."

What stung most was not the lack of match time but the absence of explanation. Pawan Negi, bought by Delhi Daredevils for ₹8.5 crore in 2016 — making him one of the most expensive Indian buys that year — faced the same silence. "I still don't know. I kept wondering why wasn't I given more opportunities. And the team management didn't tell me the reason either," he told Sportskeeda [Sportskeeda, 2016](https://www.sportskeeda.com/cricket/pawan-negi-speaks-disappointing-ipl-9). He bowled nine overs in eight matches that season.

Venkatesh Iyer, who joined Royal Challengers Bengaluru in 2026 after being part of KKR's 2024 title-winning campaign, brought a more philosophical perspective. "It's not easy. But it's extremely important to stay motivated because you can easily get desperate," he told Rediff [Rediff, 2026](https://www.rediff.com/cricket/report/ipl-2026-venkatesh-iyer-spills-secrets-on-rcb-bench-life-and-comeback/20260422.htm). "You can easily lose out on your work ethic. So, I train my mind in such a way that I'm going to play every game."

But Iyer also admitted what no training regimen can fix: "It's something that you can't prepare for."

The data bears out the cost. Colin Ingram, the South African batter who played only 3 of 14 matches for Delhi Daredevils in 2011, told ESPNcricinfo that season: "I did feel a little left out and had to try and remind myself to stay sharp so that I would be ready when my time came. There were times when it was frustrating" [ESPNcricinfo, 2011](https://www.espncricinfo.com/story/south-africa-s-fringe-talent-spurned-at-the-ipl-518273). Ingram and five other South African players — including a young Faf du Plessis, who did not play a single match that season — missed the last round of South Africa's domestic first-class fixtures to sit on IPL benches. They became richer. They lost irreplaceable game time.

## The Impact Player Paradox

Introduced in 2023 and retained through at least 2027, the Impact Player rule was sold as an opportunity-expander — a way to involve more players in each match. Instead, it has shrunk the space for all-rounders and concentrated opportunity among specialists [The Hindu, 2026](https://www.thehindu.com/sport/cricket/impact-player-rule-ipl-team-balance-all-rounders-analysis/article70821215.ece).

A synthetic control analysis from Columbia University found that the Impact Player rule added an average of 12.3 runs per innings to IPL scores above global T20 trends. But the more significant finding was structural: the number of overs bowled by top-order batsmen dropped 38%, from 11.2 per match before the rule to 6.9 after [Columbia University Working Paper, 2026](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~misra/impact-player-2026.pdf). Teams no longer need part-timers. They can carry a specialist batter who never bowls and swap him for a specialist bowler.

Rahul Dravid, India's head coach during the rule's introduction, told Sportstar he was "not particularly fond" of the Impact Player rule from a national team perspective [Sportstar, 2025](https://sportstar.thehindu.com/cricket/ipl/ipl-news/rahul-dravid-ipl-allrounders-impact-player-rule-rajasthan-royals/article69425777.ece). "As a coach, you want to develop all-rounders, and under the old 11 vs 11 format, certain players would have had more opportunities to bat or bowl in different situations."

Rohit Sharma put it even more directly on the Club Prairie Fire podcast in 2024: "I am not a big fan of the Impact Player. It is going to hold all-rounders back" [Wisden, 2025](https://www.wisden.com/series/ipl-2025/cricket-news/ipl-2025-why-the-impact-player-is-not-really-a-villain-for-indias-all-rounders).

For the bench player, the Impact Player rule creates an additional barrier. Not only are there 13 spots on the bench instead of 14, but the strategic logic that once justified carrying an all-rounder — "he gives us balance" — has been weakened. A specialist is now more valuable than a utility player. The bench, counter-intuitively, becomes more specialized and harder to break into.

## The Culture Shock

For players arriving from small-town India, the bench experience is compounded by a disorientation that has nothing to do with cricket. Ishwar Pandey, the fast bowler from Rewa, Madhya Pradesh, who played for Pune Warriors in 2013 and later Chennai Super Kings, described checking into his first IPL hotel room and encountering a bathtub. "Pehle jankari yeh le gayi, ke hai kya yeh cheez?" he told the Indian Express in 2026 [Indian Express, 2026](https://indianexpress.com/article/sports/cricket/inside-the-ipl-culture-shock-bathtub-glamour-ishwar-pandey-10597634/). "I just didn't know what it was. Do I have to bathe in it or sit in it?"

The air conditioning had buttons he could not decipher. He had to call for help. "Coming from Rewa, I had never stayed in such rooms in my life. It feels like we are in heaven."

But heaven, Pandey discovered, has its own disorienting logic. The glamour of the IPL — the celebrity owners, the after-parties, the hotel lobbies packed with fans and agents — can mask the precarity underneath. "There comes a phase where you get scared," he said. "Whether I am saying the right thing or wrong. Language is a problem."

Pandey watched the next generation of small-town arrivals go through the same cycle. "They think this is life. They don't know where their career is going and where they're going."

The culture shock is not incidental to the bench economy. It is a feature. The league recruits aggressively from India's vast domestic talent pool — 1,224 uncapped players registered for the IPL 2025 mega auction alone, alongside 320 capped players and 30 from Associate nations [Times of Sports, 2025](https://www.timesofsports.com/cricket/ipl/squad-2025-players-list/). Many of these players come from towns with no history of producing IPL cricketers. For them, the contract itself is proof of arrival. The bench is where that proof gets tested.

## The Unspoken Calculus

The franchises are not being irrational. The squad of 25 is an insurance policy for a high-variance, injury-prone sport played in extreme heat. A death bowler pulls a hamstring in the third over — the replacement needs to be IPL-ready. A top-order batter goes for a duck three matches running — the franchise wants options. The bench absorbs shocks.

But the bench also serves a financial function that franchises do not advertise. By stockpiling talent at base prices, teams ensure that rival franchises cannot access those players. An uncapped Indian player bought for ₹20 lakh and benched all season is not available to the team that might actually play him. It is a form of talent hoarding, enabled by a salary cap that makes bench-warmers cheap.

And the players know this. Tymal Mills, bought by Royal Challengers Bangalore for ₹12 crore in 2017, played a few games, got injured, and never fully recovered his IPL career. The price tag became a burden. "I feel getting bought for that much money hurt my reputation a little bit, because I didn't perform to those standards," he told the Times of India [Times of India, 2025](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/sports/cricket/news/exclusive-tymal-mills-on-the-rs-12-crore-price-tag-with-rcb-in-2017-and-the-baggage-it-brought/articleshow/117634023.cms). "If I would have had the same results but I was only bought for 3 or 4 crore, then the perception would have been a bit different."

Even now, Mills said, every year during the IPL auction, "I'm getting tagged and tweeted and mentioned as one of the worst buys ever."

## What Remains

In Rewa, Ishwar Pandey is still the man who made it. Builders arrive with brochures. Local politicians visit. Strangers make requests he does not know how to handle. "Many don't know me by face but the whole town thinks that there is an India cricketer from this place and they can get anything done through me."

He played for Pune Warriors, Chennai Super Kings, and Rising Pune Supergiant. He was part of the Indian squad but never played for India. The bathtub, he figured out eventually. The rest took longer.

"Players have to keep in mind that their focus should be on the game," Pandey said. "If we don't focus on the game, we won't get what we are getting."

But the game, for so many of them, is the thing they are not getting. Every April, a new cohort arrives — 230 young men spread across 10 teams, in 10 cities, carrying contracts that make them richer than they have ever been. Some will become stars. Some will play a handful of matches. Some will sit through entire seasons, watching, waiting, being paid to be available.

And every April, a boy from a new small town checks into a hotel room, sees a bathtub he does not know how to use, and wonders if he is good enough.
