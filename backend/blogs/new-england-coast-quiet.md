# The Coast and the Quiet: New England's Internal Distance

The man boarding the 7:15 Red Line train at Braintree, Massachusetts, on a June morning is not thinking about the fact that he lives in the fourth-most-densely-populated state in the union — because he is too busy standing (there are no seats), pressed against a stranger's backpack, his phone counting down a 32-minute arrival at South Station. He pays $2,726 a month for a mortgage on a house whose median value in his area has climbed past $562,100 [Census Bureau QuickFacts: Massachusetts](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/massachusetts). He shares his car-free commute with a transit system that moves 1.2 million people daily. His neighbors include families from Brazil, China, Haiti, and the Dominican Republic. A quarter of the households in his state speak a language other than English at home [Census Bureau QuickFacts: MA, ME, NH](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/ME,MA,NH/PST040216). Density, in his experience, is not an abstraction. It is the polite name for having a stranger's backpack pressed into your ribs at 7:15 in the morning.

Three hundred miles north, on a gravel road in Aroostook County, Maine, a woman in a Ford F-150 checks her mailbox at the end of a driveway that is a mile and a half long. She knows her nearest neighbor's name, the make of their truck, and how many cords of wood they stacked last fall — a degree of intimacy that the man on the T could not achieve with the person currently occupying his personal space. The median home value in her state is $296,600 — barely half what the man on the T is paying [Census Bureau QuickFacts: Maine](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/ME/POP060220). She drives 45 minutes to the grocery store. The population density of the county she lives in is 3.9 people per square mile — roughly the same as central Montana [Census Bureau QuickFacts: Maine Geography](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/ME/POP060220). Her state's population is one-fifth the size of the transit system that carries the man to work. Solitude, for her, is not a vacation. It is the natural state of things.

They live in the same region. They vote in the same presidential primary. They pay the same highest-in-the-nation electricity rates. And they might as well inhabit different centuries. They do.

---

New England occupies the peculiar position of being both one of the smallest and one of the most culturally coherent regions in the United States — six states united by colonial history, town-meeting governance, high educational attainment, Democratic presidential voting habits, and residential electricity bills that exceed those of every other region in the continental country [EIA 2024 Average Retail Price](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/sales_revenue_price/pdf/table_4.pdf). It is roughly the size of Washington state — 71,992 square miles — yet the internal gaps between its states rival the disparities between entire American regions [Census Bureau QuickFacts: All six states](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/table/PST045216/50,44,33,23,25,09). Rhode Island packs 1,061 people into every square mile; Maine gets by on 44. A household in Massachusetts earns a median of $103,960 per year; a household in Maine, $74,733. Vermont's population (644,663) could fit inside Boston's metropolitan statistical area with room to spare [Census Bureau QuickFacts: Vermont](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/50/PST045216). These are not subtle gradients. They are structural chasms hiding in plain sight, masked by the shared accent, the common red-brick-and-white-clapboard aesthetic, and the mutual exasperation with Interstate 95 — the great unifier of inconvenience.

## The Scale of the Small

The first thing to understand about New England's geography is that Maine is lying — not deliberately, but because the state is physically larger than the other five states combined. Maine's land area of 30,845 square miles exceeds the total of Connecticut, Rhode Island, Massachusetts, Vermont, and New Hampshire added together (31,848 square miles) [Census Bureau QuickFacts: All six states](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/table/PST045216/50,44,33,23,25,09). You could drop Rhode Island into Aroostook County — the northernmost chunk of Maine — and still have room to wander. Northern Maine is geographically part of the Canadian Maritimes in every way that matters except the passport check, a fact its residents do not find nearly as amusing as the map suggests.

This produces a density gradient that has no equivalent in any other comparably sized American region. The three coastal states — Rhode Island, Massachusetts, and Connecticut — form a continuous urbanized arc stretching from New Haven to Providence to Boston. Their combined population density is 902 people per square mile. The three interior states — Vermont, New Hampshire, and Maine — are largely rural. Their combined density is 69 people per square mile [Census Bureau QuickFacts: All six states](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/table/PST045216/50,44,33,23,25,09). The factor of thirteen between these two numbers is not a gradient. It is a wall — one that is entirely invisible from an airplane and entirely inescapable from the ground.

At the extremes, the contrast becomes almost absurd. Rhode Island, the second-most-densely-populated state in the nation after New Jersey, packs 1,061 people per square mile [Census Bureau Population Density Data Table](https://www2.census.gov/programs-surveys/decennial/2020/data/apportionment/population-density-data-table.pdf). Maine, the least-densely-populated state east of the Mississippi, packs 44. The human experience of space in these two states — the proximity of neighbors, the availability of sidewalks, the ambient noise level of daily life — is not merely different. It is inverted. Rhode Islanders, one suspects, have never understood what Mainers mean by "quiet." Mainers, one knows, have never needed to.

And yet: the interior states are growing faster. Between the 2020 Census and the July 2025 estimates, Maine added 3.8 percent to its population — the fastest growth rate in New England and the 15th-fastest nationally. New Hampshire added 2.7 percent, Connecticut 2.2 percent. Vermont, astonishingly, grew by only 0.2 percent — effectively flat, a demographic stagnation that has become a subject of growing concern in Montpelier [Census Bureau State Population Estimates 2025](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/table/PST045216/50,44,33,23,25,09). Vermont's population of 644,663 is smaller than the city of Boston's metropolitan population growth since 2010. The state, it seems, is not so much losing people as declining to acquire them.

The conventional wisdom — that the rural north is emptying out while the coastal south swells — is, for now, exactly backward. The pandemic-era migration to the northern forest has not fully reversed.

## The Demography of Difference

The coastal states are not merely denser. They are qualitatively different places — which is to say, they are inhabited by qualitatively different people.

Connecticut is 19.2 percent Hispanic, 13.3 percent Black, and 5.5 percent Asian. Its foreign-born population stands at 15.7 percent. A nearly identical profile describes Rhode Island (18.8 percent Hispanic, 9.5 percent Black, 4.1 percent Asian) and Massachusetts (14.0 percent Hispanic, 9.9 percent Black, 8.3 percent Asian), where 18 percent of residents were born outside the United States [Census Bureau QuickFacts: CT](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/09/PST045216). [Census Bureau QuickFacts: RI](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/44/PST045216). [Census Bureau QuickFacts: MA](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/massachusetts).

Vermont is 93.2 percent White alone. Maine is 93.2 percent White alone. New Hampshire is 92.3 percent White alone. The Hispanic population of Vermont (2.7 percent) is roughly one-seventh the size of Connecticut's. The foreign-born share of Maine (4.1 percent) is less than a quarter of Massachusetts's [Census Bureau QuickFacts: VT, RI, NH, ME, MA, CT](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/table/PST045216/50,44,33,23,25,09). The northern states, one might say, are not so much un-diverse as extraordinarily consistent — consistent in a way that the coastal states, with their Babel of languages and cuisines, could never manage.

What this means, in practical terms, is that a child growing up in Bridgeport, Connecticut, or Providence, Rhode Island, or Boston, Massachusetts — cities where no single racial or ethnic group constitutes an overwhelming majority — inhabits a fundamentally different social landscape than a child growing up in Burlington, Vermont, or Portland, Maine, or rural Grafton County, New Hampshire. The difference is not theoretical. It shapes everything from the languages heard on the school bus to the cuisines available on the high street to the informal networks of opportunity that young people absorb by osmosis — which is to say, everything a census cannot capture but a life cannot escape.

Twenty-five percent of Massachusetts households speak a language other than English at home. In Vermont, the figure is 5.5 percent. In Maine, 6.0 percent [Census Bureau QuickFacts: MA, ME, NH](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/ME,MA,NH/PST040216). [Census Bureau QuickFacts: VT](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/50/PST045216). These numbers trace not merely a demographic difference but a difference in worldview — the difference between a society that regularly confronts its own pluralism and one that largely does not. One learns, in the interior, to recognize one's own kind. One has no choice, on the coast, but to learn the ways of everyone else's.

## The Knowledge Premium

If demography divides New England, then education is what paradoxically unites it — even as it exposes a different kind of internal distance, which is always the most insidious kind.

All six New England states rank among the top 20 in the nation for bachelor's degree attainment [FRED: Bachelor's Degree or Higher by State](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/release/tables?eid=391444&rid=330). Massachusetts ranks second nationally, behind only the District of Columbia, with 47.3 percent of adults holding a bachelor's degree or higher. Vermont ranks fourth (43.8 percent), Connecticut seventh (42.5 percent), New Hampshire ninth (40.6 percent), Rhode Island 14th (37.7 percent), and Maine 18th (36.2 percent). By comparison, the national average is 33.7 percent [U.S. Data Explorer: Bachelor's Degree by State 2024](https://usdataexplorer.com/ranking/bachelors-degree-or-higher/states/). Every New England state exceeds the national rate — a uniformity that is, by American standards, extraordinary, and by the same standards entirely insufficient to prevent the region from arguing about everything else.

But the gap between Massachusetts and Maine — 11 percentage points — is larger than the gap between Maine and Mississippi (12 points). The region's educational advantage is real, but it is not evenly distributed. It concentrates in the Boston metropolitan corridor and radiates outward, dissipating as the road miles accumulate. Education, like wealth, follows the laws of physics: it pools in the lowest point, which in this case happens to be Cambridge.

The Federal Reserve Bank of Boston quantified this dynamic in a January 2026 study of four decades of administrative wage records. The finding was striking: per capita personal income in New England grew from $10,731 to $87,655 between 1980 and 2024 — the largest increase of any U.S. census division [Boston Fed: Educational Attainment and Wage Growth](https://www.bostonfed.org/publications/new-england-public-policy-center-research-report/2026/educational-attainment-wage-growth-in-new-england.aspx). As of 2024, 53 percent of New England workers aged 25 to 64 held at least a bachelor's degree, and 23 percent possessed advanced degrees — well above national averages of 44 percent and 17 percent, respectively. The region's economic success, the study concluded, is inseparable from its educational intensity. The flywheel works. But it works best in places that already have momentum — which is to say, it works best in places that least need the help.

The income gap that results is the most tangible measure of New England's internal distance. Massachusetts's median household income of $103,960 is the fourth-highest in the nation [Census Bureau ACS 2024](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/2025/acs/acsbr-025.html). New Hampshire follows at $99,031, then Connecticut at $95,781, Rhode Island at $87,796, and Vermont at $81,203. Maine, at $74,733, ranks 33rd nationally — a position closer to Missouri than to Massachusetts [Census Bureau QuickFacts: ME](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/ME/INC110221). The difference of $29,227 between the region's richest and poorest median household incomes is, in raw terms, larger than the gap between California ($100,205) and West Virginia ($57,872) — two states separated by 2,500 miles and a continent of political and cultural difference.

The region's poorest state and its richest state are separated by a five-hour drive on Interstate 95. They are separated by $29,000 in annual income. They are separated by very little else, which is precisely what makes the distance so striking. Proximity, one learns, has nothing to do with closeness.

## The Cost of Being Coastal

If income flows from education, then housing costs flow from density — and on this measure, the coastal tier exacts a punishing premium.

The median home value in Massachusetts is $562,100. In Rhode Island: $404,200. In New Hampshire: $402,500. In Connecticut: $366,900. In Vermont: $316,600. In Maine: $296,600 [Census Bureau QuickFacts: All six states](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/table/PST045216/50,44,33,23,25,09). The pattern is not random. It maps almost perfectly onto the population density gradient, with one instructive anomaly: New Hampshire, which is less dense than Connecticut, has more expensive housing — because it sits in the Boston commuter shed, absorbing spillover demand from a state whose own housing supply cannot keep pace. The lesson is clear: even the desire to escape Massachusetts exacts a Massachusetts toll.

The cost of a mortgage tells the same story in monthly terms. Massachusetts homeowners with a mortgage pay a median of $2,726 per month — among the highest in the nation, exceeded only by California, Hawaii, New Jersey, and the District of Columbia [Census Bureau: The Cost of Homeownership Continues to Rise](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025/acs-1-year-estimates.html). Rhode Island homeowners pay $2,234, New Hampshire $2,367, Connecticut $2,429. Vermont drops to $1,907, and to $1,729 in Maine — still substantial, but less than two-thirds of the Massachusetts burden [Census Bureau QuickFacts: Housing data](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/table/PST045216/50,44,33,23,25,09). One pays, it seems, for the privilege of being where other people are. One saves by going where they are not.

Rent tells a parallel story. The median gross rent in Massachusetts ($1,762) exceeds Maine's ($1,139) by more than $600 per month — an annual rent differential of more than $7,400. That $7,400 is, in effect, the annual premium on being coastal: the price of proximity to everything except affordability.

Yet homeownership rates invert the pattern. Maine has the highest rate in New England (74.3 percent), followed by Vermont (73.2 percent) and New Hampshire (72.8 percent). Massachusetts has the lowest (62.5 percent), followed by Rhode Island (63.6 percent) and Connecticut (66.5 percent). The coastal tier, where housing costs the most, is where the fewest people own their homes. The interior, where housing is cheaper, is where ownership is the norm. Affordability and ownership track each other closely. Density and ownership are inverse. One might almost say that the closer one gets to the American Dream, the less likely one is to own a piece of it.

And then there is electricity. New England as a whole pays the highest residential electricity rates in the contiguous United States — an average of 27.68 cents per kilowatt-hour in 2024, compared to a national average of roughly 15.5 cents [EIA Table 4: Average Retail Price 2024](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/sales_revenue_price/pdf/table_4.pdf). Massachusetts residents pay 29.35 cents; Connecticut, 28.75; Rhode Island, 28.65. But Maine pays 24.29 cents, New Hampshire 23.40, and Vermont — the state with the lowest residential rate in New England — pays 21.90 cents, nearly a third less than Massachusetts. The interior is marginally more affordable not just in shelter but in keeping the lights on — though "affordable" is, in this context, a relative term used by people who have never seen a bill from any other region.

The Boston Fed study notes that New England's per capita income growth has been the highest in the nation over four decades. But that growth has been captured disproportionately by those who could afford the coastal premium. The region's economic success is real. It is also regionally concentrated and increasingly stratified by housing cost. The rich get richer, and everyone else gets a longer commute.

## The Politics of Proximity

Politically, New England presents a united front to the rest of the country — a front that is, like most united fronts, considerably more united in appearance than in reality. All six states voted for Kamala Harris in the 2024 presidential election. The region's electoral votes — 33 in total — have gone to the Democratic nominee in every presidential election since 1992, with two exceptions (New Hampshire voted for George H.W. Bush in 1988, and for George W. Bush in 2000 — the only electoral votes a Republican has taken from New England in the 21st century) [FEC 2024 Official Presidential Results](https://www.fec.gov/resources/cms-content/documents/2024presgeresults.pdf).

But the margins tell a more interesting story than the outcomes. Harris won Vermont by roughly 30 points — her strongest performance in the region. She won Massachusetts by 25 points. Connecticut by 14.5. Rhode Island by 12. Maine by 6.9. New Hampshire by approximately 2.4 points [Elections Daily: Analyzing the 2024 Election in New England](https://elections-daily.com/2025/02/12/analyzing-the-2024-election-results-in-new-england/). The spread from Vermont to New Hampshire — the range of Democratic performance within a single region — is nearly 28 percentage points. The region appears to vote as a bloc only if one does not look too closely at the numbers, which is, of course, exactly what everyone does.

New Hampshire functions, in effect, as New England's swing state. It has the region's most independent electorate, the shortest coastline (18 miles), and the deepest attachment to its "Live Free or Die" identity — an identity its residents take seriously enough to exercise in both directions simultaneously. It voted for a Republican governor (Kelly Ayotte, elected in 2024 with 53 percent of the vote) while simultaneously casting its four electoral votes for Harris. The state's second congressional district — the western and northern reaches — voted for Harris by a margin barely exceeding 3 points. New Hampshire, in short, is the region's conscience: it cannot decide what it believes, and it believes in that indecision wholeheartedly.

Maine, too, splits its electoral sovereignty: its statewide vote went to Harris by 6.9 points, but the 2nd Congressional District — the vast, rural northern and eastern portion of the state — awarded one electoral vote to Donald Trump, as it did in 2016 and 2020. Maine is one of only two states (Nebraska is the other) that allocate electoral votes by congressional district. This system, which elsewhere produces only mathematical curiosity, in Maine generates a genuine political schism: the state is of two minds, and it has arranged its electoral machinery to prove it.

Vermont, meanwhile, is the most consistently Democratic state in the union by presidential voting margin — yet in 2024, Republicans posted their strongest gains in the state relative to any other in New England. Trump flipped Orleans County, a first for the GOP since 2000. Republicans gained 19 seats in the state Senate and six in the state House, breaking Democratic supermajorities. Senator Bernie Sanders, the state's independent icon, posted his weakest reelection margin to date [Elections Daily](https://elections-daily.com/2025/02/12/analyzing-the-2024-election-results-in-new-england/). Even in the most reliably blue state in the union, it seems, the ground shifts — slowly, but perceptibly, like a glacier that has decided to become a mudslide.

Voter turnout, too, tells a revealing story. Maine had the highest voter turnout as a percentage of voting-age population in New England (72.9 percent), followed by New Hampshire (72.2 percent) — both among the highest rates in the nation. Massachusetts (61.8 percent) and Connecticut (61.7 percent) had some of the lowest [Dave Leip's Atlas: 2024 Turnout](https://uselectionatlas.org/RESULTS/data.php?datatype=national&f=0&year=2024). The interior states vote in higher proportions than the coastal states — a pattern that holds across every election cycle and that suggests a civic culture in which the act of voting is more deeply embedded in community identity, perhaps because community identity is harder to come by and therefore more actively maintained. In the interior, one votes because one belongs. On the coast, one votes because one has been reminded to by a text message.

## The Unifying Threads

Given the scale of these internal differences, one might reasonably ask what holds New England together at all. The answer, as with most answers, is a set of structural commonalities that run deeper than the disparities — though "deeper" does not necessarily mean "more interesting."

Educational attainment is the most significant. Every New England state exceeds the national average for bachelor's degree attainment — and by substantial margins. The region as a whole educates its population at a level that would be the envy of any country in Europe, and that, in this context, is the highest compliment one can pay to a group of American states.

Employment is consistently strong. The New England division had an average unemployment rate of 3.6 percent in 2024, below the national average of 4.0 percent. Vermont (2.3 percent) had the second-lowest unemployment rate among all states, behind only South Dakota [BLS: Geographic Profile of Employment 2024](https://www.bls.gov/opub/geographic-profile/home.htm). [BLS: Unemployment rates 2024](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2025/unemployment-rates-averaged-1-8-percent-in-south-dakota-5-6-percent-in-nevada-in-2024.htm). The region, whatever its internal arguments, has not yet resorted to the indignity of widespread joblessness.

Health insurance coverage is nearly universal. Massachusetts, with only 3.3 percent of adults under 65 uninsured, has the lowest uninsured rate in the nation — the legacy of its 2006 healthcare reform, the model for the Affordable Care Act. The highest uninsured rate in New England is Maine's 7.1 percent — still below the national average of roughly 8.3 percent [Census Bureau QuickFacts: All six states](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/table/PST045216/50,44,33,23,25,09). The region has decided, collectively, that health is a right rather than a luxury — a position that puts it at odds with roughly half the country and entirely at peace with itself.

Broadband access is uniformly high. Between 90.3 percent of Maine households and 92.9 percent of New Hampshire households subscribe to broadband internet — a narrow range that speaks to the region's relatively even infrastructure investment [Census Bureau QuickFacts: MA, ME, NH](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/ME,MA,NH/PST040216). Even the most remote farmhouse, it seems, is not too remote for Netflix.

And, everywhere in New England, the electricity bill arrives with a number that surprises — a shared burden of infrastructure age, fuel import dependence, and regional market design that unites the six states in a collective grumble. The average New England household paid 27.68 cents per kilowatt-hour in 2024 — 78 percent above the national average [EIA 2024](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/sales_revenue_price/pdf/table_4.pdf). In this, at least, the commuter on the T and the woman in Aroostook County are true equals. Nothing unites people quite like a shared grievance, and nothing generates a grievance quite like a New England electricity bill.

## The Distance Between

New England is not one thing. It is six things that happen to share a border, a colonial history, a surprising number of Dunkin' Donuts locations, a collective astonishment at the price of electricity, and a presidential voting pattern that makes the rest of the country regard it as a single liberal bloc — a bloc whose internal fractures are visible only from the inside, and only to those who care to look.

The density gradient from Providence to Presque Isle is not merely a matter of how many people occupy a square mile. It predicts educational attainment, income, housing costs, racial diversity, foreign-born population, homeownership rates, and the likelihood that a household speaks a language other than English at home. Within a region smaller than Washington state, these six variables shift by more than they do across entire swaths of the American interior. New England, in other words, contains multitudes — and the multitudes do not always get along.

And yet the things that bind them are not trivial. The educational intensity that puts all six states in the national top 20. The labor market that keeps unemployment below the national average. The universal healthcare coverage that leaves almost no one behind. The broadband cable that connects the most remote farmhouse to the same network as the Back Bay brownstone. The shared exasperation with a power grid that charges twice what the rest of the country pays. The historical memory of mill strikes, town meetings, covered bridges, and leaf-peeping season. These are not small things. They are the threads from which a region's identity is woven, even when the fabric is more patched than patterned.

The commuter on the T and the woman in Aroostook County share a region. They share an accent — or close enough. They share a set of assumptions about what constitutes a good life, a fair society, a proper town common. But the ground beneath their feet — the density of opportunity, the price of shelter, the face of the crowd on the street, the distance to the nearest neighbor — could not be more different. They are cousins, not siblings; the family resemblance is there, but the lives are their own.

New England's homogeneity is real. Its internal distance is equally real. The region does not need to be resolved into a single story. It needs to be seen clearly for what it is: a small place with very large differences, held together by history, habit, and the gravitational pull of its dominant city — a city that nearly half its population has never lived within a hundred miles of, and whose influence they nonetheless feel every time they open an electric bill, look at a home listing, or watch a presidential candidate try to figure out which New England they are actually campaigning in. Boston, it turns out, does not need to be loved to be felt. It only needs to exist.

The distance between the two New Englands is not a problem to be solved. It is a condition to be described. And the description, once made, raises a question the region is only beginning to confront: whether a place this internally varied can continue to function as a single political and economic unit — or whether the coast and the quiet are destined to drift further apart, connected only by the roads that run between them, the power lines that bind them, and the shared memory of the winters that shaped them both.
