# The Universe's Own Eye: How Telescopes Taught the Cosmos to See Itself

There are 48.25 grams of gold on the James Webb Space Telescope's mirrors — roughly the mass of a golf ball, spread atom-thin across 25 square meters of beryllium [[Source: NASA Fact Sheet]](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/fact-sheet/). Gold, however, is not made casually. The universe forges it in the collision of neutron stars, in the convulsive flares of magnetars, in the seconds-long violence of supernovae; a single observed merger in 2017 may have manufactured the equivalent of an Earth's mass of the stuff [[Source: NASA, "Where Does Gold Come From?"]](https://science.nasa.gov/universe/stars/neutron-stars/magnetars/where-does-gold-come-from-nasa-data-has-clues/) [[Source: NASA HEASARC, GW170817]](https://heasarc.gsfc.nasa.gov/docs/objects/heapow/archive/transients/gw170817_gold.html). A small portion of that ancient debris now sits parked 1.5 million kilometers from Earth, at the second Lagrange point, catching photons that left their home galaxy when the universe was less than 300 million years old [[Source: NASA Webb blog, 2024]](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/webb/2024/05/30/nasas-james-webb-space-telescope-finds-most-distant-known-galaxy/).

This is the quiet scandal of the telescope: it is a machine made of the thing it studies. The silicon in its glass was cooked in the cores of stars and scattered by their explosions; the beryllium of its mirror was ground to a figure so precise it holds its shape at temperatures that would shatter steel; the gold was born in the wreck of dead stars [[Source: NASA Space Place, "What Is a Supernova?"]](https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/supernova/) [[Source: NASA, "Webb's Mirrors"]](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/webbs-mirrors/). Over thirteen and a half billion years, the universe grew something that could look back at itself — and it is only now, with a mirror kept so cold that its own heat cannot drown the faint glow it was sent to catch, that the looking has begun in earnest.

## The Eye as Theory

Before there were telescopes there were theories of vision, and they were wrong in a way that matters. Euclid, writing his *Optics* around 300 BC, argued that the eye sees by emitting visual rays that reach out and touch the world — a geometry of sight as a kind of touching from a distance [[Source: A. Mark Smith, critical edition of Alhacen's *De aspectibus*, Transactions of the American Philosophical Society]](https://archive.org/stream/TransactionsOfTheAmericanPhilosophicalSociety/TAPhS%2091%2C%204%20A.%20Mark%20Smith%20-%20Alhacen%27s%20Theory%20of%20Visual%20Perception%3B%20A%20Critical%20Edition_Volume%20One%20Introduction%20and%20Latin%20Text_djvu.txt). For more than a thousand years, the most learned people on three continents believed the organ of sight was a lantern.

The man who turned the lantern around was Ibn al-Haytham — Alhazen to the Latin West — born in Basra in 965, working in Cairo, author of the seven-volume *Book of Optics* (completed between 1028 and 1038). He dismantled the extramission theory with an argument of startling plainness: if the eye shot rays to the stars, he reasoned, then looking at the sky would require a corporeal substance to fill the space between heaven and earth, and the eye would be consumed by the effort. Instead, light enters the eye; sight is a physical transaction between world and watcher [[Source: Springer, *A Critical Edition of Ibn al-Haytham's On the Shape of the Eclipse*]](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-319-47991-0) [[Source: Ibn al-Haytham, *Perspectiva*, excerpt]](https://erenow.org/common/history-philosophy-science-reader/26.php).

He proved it experimentally, in a way that deserves the word. To understand why a solar eclipse seen through a small hole appears as a crescent, he built a dark chamber — the camera obscura — and studied how images form through apertures of different sizes and shapes, varying the hole, the distance, the source, in a systematic campaign that reads like the world's first optics laboratory [[Source: Springer critical edition]](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-319-47991-0) [[Source: Manuscript of *Maqālah fī ṣūrat al-kusūf*, Qatar Digital Library]](https://www.qdl.qa/en/archive/81055/vdc_100044790015.0x00004a). The principle of the pinhole camera had been noticed even earlier — the Chinese philosopher Mo Zi described it before 391 BCE — but Ibn al-Haytham was the first to treat the dark room as an instrument of science [[Source: Springer critical edition, historical introduction]](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-319-47991-0).

None of this yet was a telescope. But the ground was laid: the realization that light obeys geometry, and that a hole — or later, a lens — can organize it. The universe was waiting for a better eye; the material for one had been waiting even longer.

## The Sand That Bent Light

Glass began as jewelry. The earliest man-made glass appears in Mesopotamia around the 23rd century BC, and by the 16th century BC the first glass vessels were being formed and pressed in the Near East [[Source: Thilo Rehren, "The Origin of Glass and the First Glass Industries," 2021]](https://www.academia.edu/59628278/The_origin_of_glass_and_the_first_glass_industries_Rehren_2021_). At Qantir-Piramesses in the Egyptian Delta, archaeologists have found the actual furnaces: glass melted from quartz pebbles and plant ash at 900–950 °C for raw glass, 1,000–1,100 °C for colored ingots, in a palace industry that shipped its product across the Mediterranean — 175 disk-shaped ingots went down with the Uluburun shipwreck around 1300 BC [[Source: Rehren & Pusch, *Science*, 2005]](https://www.academia.edu/1031315/Late_Bronze_Age_Glass_Production_at_Qantir_Piramesses_Egypt_Rehren_and_Pusch_2005_Science_308_) [[Source: Rehren 2021]](https://www.academia.edu/59628278/The_origin_of_glass_and_the_first_glass_industries_Rehren_2021_).

Long before that, though, somebody noticed the optical trick. Ancient Egyptian artisans of the IV and V Dynasties — around 2620–2400 BC — set lenses of polished rock crystal into the eyes of funerary statues, and those "eyes" have the unnerving habit of appearing to follow the observer; a 2000 study recreated their optical properties in the laboratory and found them genuinely lens-like, the oldest known lenses in the world [[Source: Enoch, *Ophthalmic and Physiological Optics*, 2000]](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1046/j.1475-1313.2000.00496.x). The dead were given crystal eyes before the living thought to give themselves one.

Glass would not become a serious optical material until spectacle-makers in the Dutch town of Middelburg began grinding lenses for the old and the vain. And there, in 1608, the telescope stepped out of the workshops and into the archives. On October 2, 1608, the States General of the Dutch Republic examined a patent application from Hans Lipperhey, a spectacle-maker born in Wesel, for "a device to observe things at a distance" [[Source: IMSS, "Galileo's Telescope" — complete texts]](https://brunelleschi.imss.fi.it/esplora/cannocchiale/dswmedia/risorse/complete_texts.pdf) [[Source: Huib Zuidervaart, "The True Inventor of the Telescope," KNAW, 2010]](https://pure.knaw.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/458452/2010_Zuidervaart_01_TrueInventor.pdf). The patent was refused — the device was judged too simple to keep secret, and within a fortnight two other men claimed the same trick — but the Republic commissioned Lipperhey to build telescopes anyway, on one telling condition: the lenses must be "rock crystal or glass of the very best quality" [[Source: Zuidervaart, KNAW]](https://pure.knaw.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/458452/2010_Zuidervaart_01_TrueInventor.pdf) [[Source: Cabinet, University of Oxford, "Invention of the spyglass, 1608"]](https://www.cabinet.ox.ac.uk/invention-spyglass-1608).

Who invented the telescope was contested within months and has never quite settled; what matters is what happened next.

## The Tube of Lead

Galileo Galilei was in Padua in the spring of 1609 when the rumor reached him — about ten months before he published, he later wrote — that a certain Dutchman had made a spyglass, "by the aid of which visible objects, although at a great distance from the eye of the observer, were seen distinctly as if near" [[Source: Galileo, *Sidereus Nuncius* (1610), trans. Van Helden]](https://www.reed.edu/math-stats/wieting/mathematics537/SideriusNuncius.pdf). He did not rush out to buy one. He sat down, derived the instrument from the science of refraction, and built his own: "a tube, at first of lead, in the ends of which I fitted two glass lenses, both plane on one side, but on the other side one spherically convex, and the other concave" [[Source: *Sidereus Nuncius*, trans. Van Helden]](https://www.reed.edu/math-stats/wieting/mathematics537/SideriusNuncius.pdf). His first instrument magnified three times, then sixty, then — "sparing neither labor nor expense" — about a thousand times [[Source: *Sidereus Nuncius*]](https://www.reed.edu/math-stats/wieting/mathematics537/SideriusNuncius.pdf). One of his surviving telescopes, now in Florence, carries a 51-millimeter objective of glass, with a focal length of 1,330 millimeters [[Source: Museo Galileo, "Galileo's Telescope"]](https://catalogue.museogalileo.it/object/GalileosTelescope.html).

In January 1610 he turned it on the heavens, and the heavens objected. The Moon, he saw, was not a perfect sphere: "it is therefore left manifest that lunar eminences are loftier than terrestrial ones" — he measured lunar mountains at four Italian miles against the best Earth could offer of under one [[Source: *Sidereus Nuncius*]](https://www.reed.edu/math-stats/wieting/mathematics537/SideriusNuncius.pdf). On the night of January 7, 1610, looking at Jupiter, he noticed three small bright stars in a line, which moved; within days there were four, "never seen from the beginning of the world right up to our day," circling the planet as Venus and Mercury circle the Sun [[Source: *Sidereus Nuncius*]](https://www.reed.edu/math-stats/wieting/mathematics537/SideriusNuncius.pdf). It was the first published scientific work based on telescopic observation, and it contained a quiet bomb: if the Moon is a world and Jupiter a sun with planets, then the Earth is not the only center of anything [[Source: 1610 Venice edition, Internet Archive]](https://archive.org/details/Sidereusnuncius00Gali).

## The Revolt Against Glass

There was one problem with Galileo's instrument: the glass itself. Lenses bend different colors of light by different amounts, so every refracting telescope — then as now — fringed its images with rainbows and blur. Isaac Newton, having worked out the theory of color in the late 1660s, concluded that no lens could ever focus white light cleanly, and in 1668 he built a telescope that did not use a lens at all: a concave mirror of polished speculum metal — "two Metallin Speculum's, the one Concave (instead of an Object-glass)" — that reflected light to focus instead of refracting it [[Source: Newton, "An Accompt of a New Catadioptrical Telescope," *Philosophical Transactions* No. 81, 1672]](http://www.newtonproject.sussex.ac.uk/view/texts/normalized/NATP00007). The prototype was a toy — about an inch of aperture, magnifying perhaps 40 times. A second, better instrument followed, with an effective aperture of more than an inch and a third and a magnification of about 38 times. Yet in a letter to Henry Oldenburg of the Royal Society, Newton reported that with this later instrument he "could read in one of the Philosophical Transactions, placed in the Sun's light, at an hundred foot distance" — a performance he compared favorably against a six-foot refractor [[Source: Newton to Oldenburg, 19 March 1671, Newton Project]](https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/normalized/NATP00030).

![Newton's reflecting telescope — a concave metal speculum mirror in place of a glass lens, 1672. Public domain (1855 plate).](/api/blog/images/telescopes-eyes-of-the-universe_newton_telescope.jpg)

The age of the reflector had begun, and with it a strange reversal: the telescope, which had begun as glass, now declared war on glass. For two centuries the biggest instruments on Earth were metal. William Herschel cast a 48-inch speculum mirror for his forty-foot telescope at Slough, completed in 1789 — the largest in the world for half a century, polished by a team of 24, funded in part by a £4,000 grant from George III, who in return put Caroline Herschel on the payroll as her brother's assistant, making her the first woman in England paid as an astronomer [[Source: Royal Museums Greenwich, "Mirror" collection record]](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-10947) [[Source: Royal Museums Greenwich, "40-foot Herschelian telescope tube"]](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-11109) [[Source: Science Museum Group, "Original mirror for William Herschel's forty-foot telescope"]](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co56770/original-mirror-for-william-herschels-forty-foot-telescope-1785). And in 1845 the Third Earl of Rosse finished the Leviathan of Parsonstown at Birr Castle — a 72-inch, 1.83-meter speculum giant, the largest telescope in the world until 1917, with which he first discerned the spiral structure of the nebulae, the coiled handwriting of galaxies no one yet knew existed [[Source: Birr Castle, "The Great Telescope"]](https://birrcastle.com/the-great-telescope/) [[Source: UNESCO-IUA Astronomical Heritage, "Lord Rosse's Leviathan"]](https://web.astronomicalheritage.net/show-entity?identity=198&idsubentity=1).

## The Glass Returns, Enormous

Metal mirrors tarnished within weeks, so the big reflectors lived lives of constant repolishing. Glass came back because it could be coated — a thin film of silver or aluminum laid on a figured glass disk, brilliant, untarnishable, and much easier to cast than speculum. The 200-inch Hale Telescope at Palomar, dedicated in 1948, was the apotheosis: a 15-ton disk of Pyrex glass cast by Corning in 1934–35, ground and figured at Caltech for eleven and a half years, and aimed on its first official night by Edwin Hubble himself [[Source: Caltech, "The 200-inch Hale Telescope"]](https://sites.astro.caltech.edu/palomar/about/telescopes/hale.html) [[Source: Caltech, "Dedication of the Palomar Observatory and the Hale Telescope," 1948]](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/3f6qm-11x39). It pushed the observable horizon of the universe from 500 million to a billion light-years [[Source: Caltech, *Engineering and Science*, May 1949]](https://calteches.library.caltech.edu/997/).

But Palomar was still looking through an ocean of air, which shimmers and blurs. The next leap required leaving the planet. The Hubble Space Telescope — a 2.4-meter primary of ultra-low-expansion glass, honeycombed to cut its weight from 8,000 to 1,825 pounds — launched aboard the shuttle Discovery on April 24, 1990 [[Source: NASA Science, "About Hubble"]](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/overview/about-hubble/) [[Source: NASA, "A Brief History of the Hubble Space Telescope"]](https://www.nasa.gov/history/hubble/index.html). Then the famous catastrophe: the mirror had been ground wrong, its edges too flat by two microns — a fiftieth of the width of a human hair — and the first images came back soft, "stars surrounded by large, fuzzy halos of light" [[Source: NASA Science, "Hubble History Timeline"]](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/overview/hubble-timeline/hubble-history-timeline-full-text/). The fix was as audacious as the failure: astronauts flew up in December 1993 and fitted the telescope with a pair of spectacles — the Corrective Optics Space Telescope Axial Replacement, a machine of mirrors designed to cancel the flaw, plus a new camera with corrective optics built in [[Source: NASA, "30 Years Ago: Hubble Launched"]](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/30-years-ago-hubble-launched-to-unlock-the-secrets-of-the-universe/). On December 18, 1993, astronomers gathered around a monitor at the Space Telescope Science Institute and cheered as the first corrected image came down, "free of the blurriness that had plagued previous Hubble images" [[Source: NASA Science, "The History of Hubble"]](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/overview/the-history-of-hubble/).

![The Hubble Space Telescope released into orbit from the shuttle Discovery, April 1990. NASA public domain.](/api/blog/images/telescopes-eyes-of-the-universe_hubble_orbit.jpg)

Hubble could now detect objects ten billion times fainter than the unaided eye [[Source: NASA Science, "Hubble Design"]](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/hubble/observatory/design/). In 2004 it stared at one patch of seemingly empty sky in the constellation Fornax for 11.3 days, through 800 exposures — and produced the Hubble Ultra Deep Field: nearly 10,000 galaxies, the smallest and reddest of them having existed when the universe was just 800 million years old, their photons arriving "at a trickle of one photon per minute" [[Source: NASA, "The Hubble Ultra Deep Field"]](https://science.nasa.gov/asset/hubble/the-hubble-ultra-deep-field/) [[Source: NASA, "Hubble Digs Deeply, Toward Big Bang," 2004]](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/nasa-hubble-digs-deeply-toward-big-bang/). Some of those photons, NASA noted, "began traversing the universe even before Earth existed" [[Source: NASA, "Hubble Digs Deeply"]](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/nasa-hubble-digs-deeply-toward-big-bang/).

## The Cold Gold Eye

Hubble sees in visible and ultraviolet light — the wavelengths that would have reached us from the universe's first stars have been stretched by the expansion of space into infrared. To see the beginning, one must build a telescope for the dark and the cold, and that is exactly what Webb is. Its 6.5-meter primary — the largest ever launched — is 18 hexagonal segments of beryllium, chosen because it holds its shape at cryogenic temperatures, each segment coated with gold to reflect infrared [[Source: NASA, "Webb's Mirrors"]](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/webbs-mirrors/) [[Source: NASA Fact Sheet]](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/fact-sheet/). It flies behind a five-layer sunshield the size of a tennis court, at the second Lagrange point, a million and a half kilometers out, and its instruments must run below 50 kelvin so that the telescope's own heat does not drown the faint glow it was sent to catch [[Source: NASA, "Webb Fact Sheet"]](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/fact-sheet/) [[Source: NASA, "Overview: About Webb"]](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/about-overview/). Launched on December 25, 2021, it opened its golden eye on the sky in July 2022 [[Source: NASA, "NASA Reveals Webb Telescope's First Images," 2022]](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/webb/nasa-reveals-webb-telescopes-first-images-of-unseen-universe/).

![Webb's 18 gold-coated beryllium mirror segments — about 48 grams of star-forged gold, spread across 25 square meters. NASA public domain.](/api/blog/images/telescopes-eyes-of-the-universe_webb_mirror.jpg)

In its first years Webb has done what it was built to do: it has found galaxies that should not exist yet. The JADES survey's image of a single patch of sky contains more than 45,000 galaxies, and hundreds of them lived less than 600 million years after the Big Bang [[Source: NASA, "JWST Advanced Deep Extragalactic Survey (NIRCam Image)"]](https://science.nasa.gov/asset/webb/jwst-advanced-deep-extragalactic-survey-nircam-image/). In January 2024, NIRSpec spent nearly ten hours on one faint smudge, JADES-GS-z14-0, and measured its redshift at 14.32 — light that left the galaxy less than 300 million years after the beginning of time, making it the most distant known galaxy [[Source: NASA Webb blog, "Most Distant Known Galaxy," 30 May 2024]](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/webb/2024/05/30/nasas-james-webb-space-telescope-finds-most-distant-known-galaxy/). It is more than 1,600 light-years across and shines with the light of several hundred million suns. The astronomers who found it ask the obvious question, the one every generation of telescope-builders has asked of the thing their machine just revealed: "How can nature make such a bright, massive, and large galaxy in less than 300 million years?" [[Source: NASA Webb blog, 2024]](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/webb/2024/05/30/nasas-james-webb-space-telescope-finds-most-distant-known-galaxy/). The instrument is answering with data; the theory is still catching up.

## The Oldest Photograph

There is a limit to how far back any telescope can see, and it is not a limit of size or engineering. It is a wall of fire. For the first 380,000 years after the Big Bang, the universe was a hot, dense fog through which light could not travel freely; then it cooled enough to become transparent, and the light that had been trapped poured out in every direction [[Source: NASA Science, "Planck"]](https://science.nasa.gov/resource/planck/) [[Source: ESA, "Cosmic microwave background seen by Planck"]](https://sci.esa.int/web/planck/-/51553-cosmic-microwave-background-seen-by-planck). That escaping light is still arriving, everywhere, all at once — the cosmic microwave background, the oldest light in the universe, the radiation from the moment the universe first became visible to itself [[Source: NASA, "Best Map Ever of the Universe" (Planck), 2013]](https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/best-map-ever-of-the-universe/).

![The Planck mission's all-sky map of the cosmic microwave background — light imprinted when the universe was 380,000 years old, the oldest photograph in existence. NASA/ESA public domain.](/api/blog/images/telescopes-eyes-of-the-universe_planck_cmb.jpg)

![How far back the universe can see — years after the Big Bang reached by each milestone (log scale). Sources: NASA Science (Planck); NASA Webb blog; ESA/Webb; NASA Hubble Ultra Deep Field.](/api/blog/images/telescopes-eyes-of-the-universe_lookback_timeline.png)

When Planck mapped it with exquisite precision, the mottled pattern it found — temperature variations of a few parts in a hundred thousand — was a fossil of the universe's infancy, the "seeds of all future structure: the stars and galaxies of today" [[Source: ESA, Planck CMB image]](https://sci.esa.int/web/planck/-/51553-cosmic-microwave-background-seen-by-planck) [[Source: NASA/Planck photojournal]](https://science.nasa.gov/photojournal/best-map-ever-of-the-universe/). The universe, it turns out, keeps its own baby pictures. A telescope is just the way it learned to look at them.

Consider the arc again. The eye that Euclid believed shot rays at the stars was, in fact, a receiver; the dark chamber Ibn al-Haytham built in Cairo to study an eclipse was the ancestor of every camera and every telescope since; the glass that began as beads in Mesopotamia and eyes of crystal in Egypt became lenses, became mirrors, became the 10-meter segmented Zerodur giants of Keck and the cold gold of Webb [[Source: Ibn al-Haytham critical edition, Springer]](https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-319-47991-0) [[Source: Keck Observatory, "Telescopes"]](https://keckobservatory.org/our-story/telescopes/) [[Source: NASA Fact Sheet]](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/fact-sheet/). Every stage of that arc is the same act repeated: the universe taking some of its own forged material — sand from a star, metal from a supernova, gold from a neutron-star collision — and shaping it into an eye.

The telescopes grew alongside the questions. Galileo's lens, small enough to hold in one hand, settled whether the Moon was a world. Newton's inch-and-a-third mirror settled whether light could be trusted to behave. Palomar doubled the visible universe. Hubble found 10,000 galaxies in a patch of sky the size of a grain of sand held at arm's length. Webb found a galaxy that was already ancient when the universe was 300 million years old, and behind it, 380,000 years after the beginning, stands the oldest light of all — the point beyond which no eye, however patient, will ever see. We have reached the wall. The instrument has taught the cosmos to read its own history as far back as history exists, and the last thing it will ever reveal is a photograph of its own birth, taken with gold that was made in a dying star, aimed by minds that were made of the same dust. The universe looked at itself, and for the first time, it understood what it was seeing.
