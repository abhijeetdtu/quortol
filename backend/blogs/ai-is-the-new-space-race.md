# AI Is the New Space Race — and We Are Already Losing Perspective

## What Seven Historical Rivalries Reveal About the $700 Billion Competition for Artificial Intelligence Supremacy

---

On the morning of October 5, 1957, the United States awoke to discover that the sky was no longer American property. Sputnik 1 — a polished aluminum sphere of merely 83.8 kilograms, carrying little more than a radio transmitter and a great deal of political symbolism — had been orbiting the Earth for an entire day, broadcasting a signal that any ham radio operator could hear. The Soviet Union had not troubled to announce it was coming. It simply launched, and the world discovered that empires are built not on warnings but on surprises.

Within twelve months, the United States had created the National Aeronautics and Space Administration, passed the National Defense Education Act (funneling billions into science and mathematics education), and committed itself to matching — and then exceeding — every Soviet achievement in space. By 1962, standing before a crowd of 40,000 at Rice University, John F. Kennedy had articulated the terms of engagement: "We choose to go to the Moon in this decade and do the other things, not because they are easy, but because they are hard." It is a curious feature of the human character that we only choose the hard things when someone else might do them first.

The moon landing came seven years later. The Apollo program ultimately cost $25.8 billion in its-era dollars — roughly $309 billion adjusted for inflation, or approximately $655 billion when measured as a share of GDP. [Planetary Society, 2025](https://www.planetary.org/space-policy/cost-of-apollo) It employed 400,000 people, consumed 4% of the federal budget at its peak, and represented the single largest peacetime technological mobilization in American history. It also represented something else: a government that believed its citizens would tolerate tax money being spent on things that had not yet been invented.

Today, that record has been broken — not by a government, not by a military alliance, but by four private technology companies operating out of Cupertino, Seattle, Menlo Park, and Redmond. In a single calendar year, Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, and Meta plan to spend approximately $700 billion on artificial intelligence infrastructure. [Introl, Feb 2026](https://introl.com/blog/hyperscaler-capex-690-billion-microsoft-azure-power-bottleneck-2026) That is more than the entire GDP of Switzerland, or Sweden, or Poland, in twelve months. It is roughly equal to the inflation-adjusted cost of the entire Apollo program, spent every twelve months, by four corporate treasuries that answer to shareholders rather than citizens.

And no one can say with confidence what the return on that investment will look like. Which is, perhaps, the point.

The AI race has been called the new space race so often that the comparison has become a cliché. But clichés become clichés because they point to something true — and then fail to follow through on the implications. The actual, data-driven comparison between AI and earlier technological rivalries reveals something more unsettling than a simple parallel. It reveals that the AI race is proceeding at a speed, scale, and structural complexity that has no historical precedent. And the lessons of history — about rivalry, about investment, about the relationship between money and technological progress — are being ignored by almost everyone placing a bet. History does not repeat itself, but it is remarkably consistent about whom it punishes.

## I. The Chronology of Competitive Technology Races

Before examining what makes the AI race unique, it is worth establishing what came before. Each of these historical episodes contains a structural insight that applies — or does not apply — to the present. It is always easier to see the pattern in someone else's mistakes.

| Era | Duration | Peak Annual Spend (USD) | Primary Funder | Defining Event | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Manhattan Project** | 1942–1946 | ~$4.5B (2008$) | US Government | Trinity test, July 1945 | Nuclear monopoly (4 yrs) |
| **Space Race** | 1957–1969 | ~$48B (GDP-share adj.) | US Government | Apollo 11, July 1969 | US moon landing |
| **Nuclear Arms Race** | 1948–1989 | ~$320B (1985) | US Government | Reagan buildup, 1980s | Soviet collapse, treaties |
| **Telecom Bubble** | 1996–2002 | ~$300B/yr (capex) | Private sector | 95% dark fiber, 2002 | Massive asset impairment |
| **Railway Boom** | 1860–1890 | ~$8B (era dollars) | Private + land grants | Transcontinental, 1869 | Overbuilding, consolidation |
| **AI Competition** | 2020–present | ~$700B (2026) | Private sector (Big Tech) | DeepSeek R1, Jan 2025 | Undetermined |

*Sources: [Manhattan Project: Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-manhattan-project-cost/); [Space Race: Planetary Society](https://www.planetary.org/space-policy/cost-of-apollo); [Nuclear Arms: CRS](https://sgp.fas.org/crs/natsec/RL32510.pdf); [Telecom: Telegeography](https://www2.telegeography.com/); [Railways: Historical Statistics of the US](https://www.census.gov/history/www/reference/publications/historical_statistics_of_the_united_states.html); [AI: Introl, Feb 2026](https://introl.com/blog/hyperscaler-capex-690-billion-microsoft-azure-power-bottleneck-2026)*

The table reveals the first important observation: the AI race is the first major technology competition in American history that is being funded primarily by the private sector rather than the state. The Manhattan Project, NASA, and the Cold War defense buildup were all government programs funded through taxation and debt issuance. The telecom bubble and the railway boom were private-sector affairs — and both ended in spectacular overinvestment, followed by bankruptcies, consolidation, and the eventual, belated realization that building infrastructure ahead of demand is only prudent if demand eventually shows up. The private sector has a genius for confusing its own enthusiasm with economic certainty.

## II. The Manhattan Project: What a Single-Goal Mobilization Looks Like

The Manhattan Project is the most frequently invoked analogy for modern AI development, and the comparisons are not entirely wrong. The project employed 130,000 people across three secret cities (Los Alamos, Oak Ridge, Hanford), consumed resources equivalent to 0.4% of US GDP at its peak, and produced the single most consequential weapons technology in human history in under four years. [Brookings, Manhattan Project Costs](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-manhattan-project-cost/) It is always reassuring to compare oneself to the greatest achievement of a generation, provided one does not examine the differences too closely.

The analogies to AI are striking: both involve a small number of brilliant scientists working under extreme secrecy; both involve a "scaling" insight (for the Manhattan Project, it was the realization that enriched uranium could be produced in quantity; for AI, it is the scaling laws that connect compute, data, and model capability); and both experienced an inflection point that forced the rest of the world to respond — Trinity for nuclear weapons, ChatGPT's launch in November 2022 for AI.

But the differences are equally important. The Manhattan Project had a binary objective (build a bomb before Germany does), a single primary funder (the US government via the Army Corps of Engineers), and a clear termination point (the end of the war). The AI race has none of these. It is not binary — nobody wins the AI race; somebody might simply lose less badly. It is not single-funder — the $700 billion is being spent by at least four competing corporate entities, plus a dozen sovereign wealth funds, plus three frontier labs, plus the Chinese state. And it has no termination point — there is no equivalent of Hiroshima, no armistice, no moment at which everyone agrees to stop. The Manhattan Project ended with a weapon that could end the world. The AI race has no such elegant finish line.

The physicist J. Robert Oppenheimer, upon witnessing the Trinity test, quoted the Bhagavad Gita: "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds." In 2026, the founders of OpenAI, Anthropic, and DeepSeek are not quoting scripture. They are quoting scaling laws, compound annual growth rates, and market share projections. The difference in temperament is not trivial. One may wonder whether the men who built the bomb had the advantage of knowing they were building a bomb.

## III. The Space Race: Speed, Prestige, and the Role of the State

The space race offers a structurally tighter analogy. Like AI, it was a two-player competition (US versus USSR) that the public understood in binary terms (someone would get there first). Like AI, it involved massive capital expenditure on capital-intensive infrastructure (launch pads, tracking stations, spacecraft versus data centers, GPUs, fiber). And like AI, it prompted an enormous government response: the NDEA, the creation of NASA, and the single largest peacetime R&D budget in American history.

The phrase "AI is the new space race" has been used so relentlessly that it has become embedded in official policy documents. President Trump's America First AI Action Plan, released in July 2025, declared: "Just like we won the space race, America will win the AI race." [America's AI Action Plan, July 2025](https://www.whitehouse.gov/ai-action-plan/) The Stargate Project — a $500 billion joint venture announced at the White House in January 2025 by Sam Altman, Masayoshi Son, Larry Ellison, and President Trump — is explicitly modeled on the Apollo program in its rhetoric, if not in its ownership structure. [Stargate Announcement, Jan 2025](https://www.whitehouse.gov/stargate/) Nothing flatters a businessman more than being compared to a government program, unless it is being compared to a government program that actually worked.

And yet, the space race analogy breaks down in a crucial respect. The Soviet Union and the United States were building the same thing (rockets, capsules, lunar modules), using roughly comparable technology (both had access to von Braun's V-2 heritage, both developed liquid-fueled rocketry independently), and competing on a single measurable dimension (who gets to the moon first). The AI competition is not a race to a single destination. It is a multidimensional contest involving model performance, inference cost, talent retention, chip manufacturing, energy availability, regulatory frameworks, and national security policy — all evolving simultaneously, with no finish line in sight. A race with no finish line is not a race at all. It is a religion with quarterly earnings reports.

The space race was also expensive by the standards of its time — Apollo consumed 4% of the federal budget at its peak. But $48 billion per year (GDP-share adjusted) is less than one-tenth of what the hyperscalers plan to spend on AI infrastructure in 2026. The space race was a sprint; AI is a marathon funded like a sprint, which is a fine way to win a marathon if the marathon is only four hundred meters long.

## IV. The Nuclear Arms Race: Deterrence, Escalation, and the Military-Industrial Complex

The nuclear arms race — spanning roughly four decades from the first Soviet atomic test in 1949 to the dissolution of the Soviet Union — offers the darkest lens through which to view the AI competition. It also offers the most honest one.

The structural parallel is straightforward: two powers, each convinced that the other is gaining an advantage, locked in a cycle of investment and counter-investment that neither can unilaterally halt. The US defense budget averaged 7.5% of GNP between 1948 and 1989, peaking at approximately $320 billion (current-year dollars) in 1985 under the Reagan buildup. [CRS, Defense Spending, RL32510](https://sgp.fas.org/crs/natsec/RL32510.pdf) The Soviet Union, with a much smaller economy, attempted to match this spending, ultimately spending itself into economic collapse. There is a certain grim comedy in watching two adversaries race to see which can bankrupt itself first, and a certain tragedy in the fact that both often succeed.

In 2026, the US Department of Defense awarded AI-related contracts worth $90.7 billion — up from $269 million in 2022, a 33,600% increase in four years. [Brookings, DoD AI Contracts](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-pentagons-ai-spending/) The $90.7 billion is still small relative to the $700 billion hyperscaler capex, but it is growing at a rate that outstrips any other category of defense spending. The militarization of AI is proceeding even faster than the commercialization of AI, which is saying something. It seems the Pentagon has discovered that artificial intelligence, like most things, is more interesting when it might kill someone.

But the arms race analogy has a more troubling implication. In a nuclear arms race, the objective is parity — the point at which each side's destructive capability renders attack unthinkable. In the AI race, parity is not the goal; dominance is. Every hyperscaler CEO, every AI lab founder, and every national AI strategy document makes this explicit. "The risk of under-investing in AI infrastructure is far greater than the risk of over-investing," Sundar Pichai told Alphabet's investors in February 2026. [CNBC, Alphabet Earnings, Feb 2026](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/04/alphabet-googl-q4-2025-earnings.html) This is not the language of deterrence. It is the language of escalation — and escalation, historically, has never ended because someone decided they had enough.

President Dwight Eisenhower's farewell address in January 1961 warned against the "unwarranted influence" of the military-industrial complex — a phrase he coined to describe the emerging permanent nexus of defense contractors, military planners, and congressional appropriators. [Eisenhower Farewell Address, 1961](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/president-dwight-d-eisenhowers-farewell-address) In 2026, the AI equivalent — call it the compute-industrial complex — consists of hyperscalers, chip manufacturers (Nvidia alone supplies GPUs for 52% of all tracked AI infrastructure projects globally, per CNAS), frontier labs, sovereign wealth funds, and venture capital firms, all with aligned incentives to amplify the urgency of further investment. [CNAS Sovereign AI Index](https://interactives.cnas.org/reports/sovereign-ai-index/) No one in this ecosystem benefits from declaring that enough is enough. Eisenhower warned about a complex that might become too powerful. He did not foresee one that would be entirely self-funding.

## V. The Industrial Revolution: General-Purpose Technology and Structural Transformation

If the space race and the arms race are the wrong analogies because they are about competition rather than transformation, then the Industrial Revolution may be the right one. It is always more comfortable to compare oneself to a revolution than to an arms race, even when the evidence points toward the latter.

AI is what economic historians call a general-purpose technology (GPT): a technology that does not merely improve existing processes but reshapes the entire economy, much as the steam engine, electricity, and the internal combustion engine did in earlier eras. [Crafts, 2021, GPTs in Economic History](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/econometrics-and-economic-history/general-purpose-technologies) The steam engine enabled factories to locate anywhere, not just near water power. Electricity enabled continuous production and the modern assembly line. AI — like these predecessors — is not a single product but a capability layer that can be applied across virtually every sector. The difference is that the steam engine did not require its investors to spend the GDP of Switzerland before anyone knew what it was for.

The Industrial Revolution also saw the first large-scale private capital markets funding technological infrastructure. The railway boom of the 1860s–1890s was financed through a combination of private capital, government land grants, and speculative investment — and it produced the same pattern of overbuilding, financial panic, and eventual consolidation that the telecom bubble would replicate a century later. The US built more railway track in the 1880s than existed in all of Europe, much of it to nowhere in particular. By 1894, a quarter of US railroad mileage was in receivership. [Historical Statistics of the United States](https://www.census.gov/history/www/reference/publications/historical_statistics_of_the_united_states.html) The more things change, the more they resemble the same mistakes at larger denominations.

The AI parallel is uncomfortable. The hyperscalers are building data centers not because they have demonstrated demand for all that compute, but because they fear the cost of not building. Microsoft reported $80 billion in unfulfilled Azure orders in early 2026 — not because it lacked servers, but because it could not get enough electricity to the GPUs it already owned. [Introl, Feb 2026](https://introl.com/blog/hyperscaler-capex-690-billion-microsoft-azure-power-bottleneck-2026) Amazon's AWS backlog reached $364 billion. The demand is real. But the investment is outrunning the demand by a margin that the railway boom, the telecom bubble, and every other infrastructure overbuild in history would recognize. They would also recognize the feeling of being too far in to stop.

## VI. The Telecom Bubble: When the Build-Out Exceeded the Business Case

The telecom bubble of the late 1990s is the closest historical analogy to the current AI build-out, and it is the one that keeps the most people in the industry awake at night. It is also the analogy that no one making the investment wishes to discuss.

Between 1996 and 2002, telecommunications companies invested approximately $2 trillion in fiber-optic infrastructure, driven by the conviction that internet traffic would double every three months — a rate that, if sustained, would require continuous network expansion. The traffic did grow, but it did not grow at that rate. By 2002, an estimated 95% of installed fiber capacity was "dark" — laid in the ground but never lit. [Telegeography, 2002](https://www2.telegeography.com/dark-fiber) Major carriers including WorldCom, Global Crossing, and Qwest filed for bankruptcy. Investors lost hundreds of billions of dollars. And the infrastructure — the dark fiber itself — was eventually repurposed at a fraction of its construction cost, providing the backbone for the broadband internet that would materialize years later. The optimist calls this long-term thinking. The realist calls it a very expensive way to be right a decade too late.

The AI infrastructure build-out of 2025–2026 shares a structural feature with the telecom bubble: the investment is being driven by an assumption about demand that is untestable until the infrastructure exists. The hyperscalers are building data centers that will consume multiple gigawatts of electricity, based on a forecast that AI inference demand will grow by 10x to 100x over the next five years. That forecast may be correct. It may also be as wrong as the assumption that internet traffic would double every three months. The difference between a visionary and a speculator is, in the end, a matter of which forecast happens to be accurate.

There is one crucial difference. The telecom bubble was financed almost entirely through debt issued by companies with no cash flow. The 2026 AI build-out is being financed by companies with enormous cash flow — Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, and Meta collectively generate well over $400 billion in annual operating income. But the gap between that cash flow and the $700 billion in capex is being filled with debt: AI-related tech companies issued $141 billion in corporate bonds in 2025 alone. [Goldman Sachs, via Primary Ignition, May 2026](https://primaryignition.com/2026/05/08/why-microsofts-80-billion-ai-capital-expenditure-plan-is-the-most-important-corporate-finance-decision-of-the-decade/) The five hyperscalers are now spending approximately 100% of operating cash flow on capex, up from a historical average of 40%. If AI revenue does not materialize as forecast, the resulting asset impairment — physical GPUs that depreciate in 4–5 years and become technologically obsolete in 18 months — could be the largest in corporate history. The only thing worse than having no cash flow is having cash flow and believing it will last forever.

## VII. The Great Powers: US vs. China in the AI Arena

Every historical competition examined so far was either a US-only story or a US–USSR story. The AI race is the first technology competition in history in which the United States faces a comparable adversary: China. The Soviet Union was a military superpower with a peasant economy. China is a military superpower with an industrial one, which is a rather more serious proposition.

The Stanford AI Index 2026 provides the data frame. In 2025, the United States invested $285.9 billion in private AI funding. China invested $12.4 billion. The ratio is 23:1 in America's favor. And yet, the performance gap between the best US and Chinese AI models has collapsed from approximately 17.5–31.6 percentage points in May 2023 to just 2.7% in early 2026. [Stanford AI Index 2026, via The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/stanford-ai-index-2026-china-us-performance-gap)

This is the central paradox of the AI competition. The United States is spending 23 times more and achieving an advantage that is within the margin of measurement error. It is as though one spent a fortune on a racehorse only to discover that the other competitor had taught a mule to run at nearly the same speed, and the mule ate less.

The reasons are structural rather than circumstantial. China's comparative advantages in the AI race are not in capital; they are in talent, efficiency, and industrial policy. In 2019, 29% of NeurIPS authors began their careers in China. By 2025, half did. Nine of the top ten institutions producing NeurIPS authors by undergraduate degree were Chinese. Tsinghua University alone produced 4% of all authors; MIT, the top American institution, produced 1%. [The Economist, March 2026](https://www.economist.com/interactive/science-and-technology/2026/03/25/china-is-winning-the-ai-talent-race) The United States, it appears, has been winning the money race while China has been winning the talent race, and talent has the advantage of not having to be spent.

Even more critically, China is retaining that talent. In 2019, roughly one-third of Chinese-educated NeurIPS authors remained in China. By 2025, 68% stayed. AI talent migration to the United States has dropped 89% since 2017. [Stanford AI Index 2026](https://thenextweb.com/news/stanford-ai-index-2026-china-us-performance-gap) The Economist analysis found that none of the core contributors to DeepSeek R1 — the model that stunned Silicon Valley in January 2025 — held degrees from outside China. The country that produces the talent and keeps the talent is the country that will eventually build the better model, regardless of whose treasury is larger.

![The US–China AI Paradox: Spending vs. Performance](/api/blog/images/chart2_us_china_paradox.png)

China also dominates the patent and publication dimensions. According to WIPO's 2025 report on generative AI patents, China accounts for 69.7% of global AI patents — roughly six times the US share. In generative AI specifically, Chinese inventors filed 38,210 patents between 2014 and 2024, compared to 6,276 from the United States. [WIPO GenAI Patent Report, 2025](https://www.wipo.int/web-publications/wipo-publications/en/generative-ai-patent-landscape-2025.html) China produces 23.2% of all AI publications globally, compared to the US share of 12.6%. In industrial robotics, China operated 295,000 industrial robots in 2024, compared to 34,000 in the US. [OECD AI Policy Observatory, 2025](https://oecd.ai/en/) The numbers suggest that the United States is winning the battle of boardrooms while China is winning the battle of laboratories, and laboratories, in the long run, have a way of rendering boardrooms irrelevant.

These are not marginal differences. They represent a structural shift in the global geography of AI capability. The US advantage is in capital, but capital is a diminishing-returns game at these scales. China's advantages are in talent, efficiency, and the institutional capacity to execute industrial policy at a scale that no Western democracy can match. Democracies debate; autocracies build.

On the policy front, both sides have been explicit about their ambitions. In January 2025, President Trump signed Executive Order 14179, declaring that "the United States cannot and will not accept second place in AI." [EO 14179, Jan 2025](https://www.whitehouse.gov/executive-orders/ai-dominance/) The America First AI Action Plan of July 2025 went further, stating flatly: "Just like we won the space race, America will win the AI race." In December 2025, a second executive order, EO 14365, established new export controls and created a "National AI Infrastructure Coordination Office." [EO 14365, Dec 2025](https://www.whitehouse.gov/executive-orders/ai-infrastructure/) There is a certain pathos in watching a nation reassure itself through paperwork.

China's response has been equally systematic. In April 2025, Xi Jinping chaired a Politburo study session on AI, calling for "self-reliance and self-strengthening in AI" and the creation of a "new system for mobilizing resources nationwide" to achieve technological independence. [Xinhua, April 2025](https://www.xinhuanet.com/politics/2025-04/ai-study-session/) China's 15th Five-Year Plan (2026–2030) targets an AI industry worth 10 trillion yuan (approximately $1.4 trillion) by 2030, backed by a $70 billion subsidy package for domestic chip production announced in March 2026. [China AI Chip Subsidy, March 2026](https://awesomeagents.ai/news/china-70b-ai-chip-subsidy-self-sufficiency/) Where the United States issues executive orders, China issues five-year plans. It is the difference between a sprint and a marathon, or perhaps between a tantrum and a strategy.

The export control regime — originally imposed in October 2022 and progressively tightened through April 2025 — has constrained China's access to advanced Nvidia GPUs. But it has also accelerated China's domestic chip development. Huawei's Ascend 910C is approximately competitive with Nvidia's A100 (two generations behind Blackwell). The $70 billion subsidy aims to close that gap over a 3- to 5-year horizon. In the meantime, DeepSeek's models demonstrated that frontier-class performance can be achieved on export-restricted hardware through architectural innovation — mixture-of-experts, efficient attention mechanisms, and synthetic data generation. [Abhishek Gautam, 2026](https://www.abhs.in/blog/china-ai-manhattan-project-export-control-gaps-chip-supply-2026) The law of unintended consequences has never respected export controls.

## VIII. Efficiency, Compute, and the Diminishing Returns of Scale

The relationship between compute investment and model capability is the single most important analytical question in the AI race, and the data suggests that it is changing. It is always the questions that cannot be answered that determine the outcome of competitions.

Epoch AI's research on training compute scaling shows that the compute used to train frontier AI models has been doubling every 5.2 months since 2010 — a 10 billion-fold increase. Training costs have been growing at 2.4x per year, and Epoch projects that the largest training runs could cost $1 billion by 2027. [Epoch AI, Training Compute Trends, 2025](https://epochai.org/blog/training-compute-trends) The slope of the curve is terrifying for anyone who believes that Moore's Law was fast. It is also terrifying for anyone who believes that spending $1 billion on a single training run is a sensible business proposition.

![Training Cost Explosion: The Exponential Path to $1 Billion Models](/api/blog/images/chart3_training_cost_explosion.png)

And yet, the returns to this investment appear to be diminishing. The Stanford AI Index documents that the US–China model performance gap has shrunk from 20+ percentage points to 2.7% while the US spending advantage has grown from roughly 10:1 to 23:1. The marginal efficiency of capital in AI — the improvement in frontier model capability per billion dollars invested — is declining for the US and rising for China. This is the pattern that keeps the strategists up at night. It is also the pattern that suggests the strategists may be solving the wrong equation.

The explanation is not that US labs are spending wastefully. It is that Chinese labs are becoming more efficient at a faster rate than US labs can increase their absolute spending. DeepSeek reported training R1 for approximately $5.6 million — compared to estimates of $78 million for GPT-4. DeepSeek V4 was trained on a cluster of approximately 55,000 H800 GPUs, using a Mixture-of-Experts architecture that activates only a fraction of parameters per token, dramatically reducing compute requirements. [DeepSeek V4 Technical Report, April 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/deepseek-v4) When your competitor does in one day what takes you two weeks, your larger budget is not an advantage; it is an overhead.

This efficiency divergence has a direct implication for the geopolitical competition. If the relationship between compute input and model output is flattening at the frontier, then the value of the US compute advantage declines — and the value of China's efficiency, talent, and industrial-policy advantages rises. The race becomes less about who has the most GPUs and more about who uses them most effectively. The shift from "who has more" to "who does more with less" is the kind of change that leaves the leaders at a disadvantage.

## IX. The Stargate Bet, DeepSeek's Sputnik, and the $5.6 Million Question

Two events in 2025 crystallized the competing theories of how AI progress happens. One was a monument to capital; the other, a monument to the fact that capital is not everything.

The first was the announcement of the Stargate Project in January 2025 — a $500 billion joint venture between SoftBank, OpenAI, Oracle, and MGX (UAE), announced at the White House with the President in attendance. Stargate aims to build 20+ data centers across the United States, each consuming hundreds of megawatts. The architecture of the deal — sovereign capital (SoftBank/MGX) plus frontier lab (OpenAI) plus cloud provider (Oracle) — represents the theory that AI progress is primarily a function of capital deployment at scale. [Stargate Project, Jan 2025](https://www.whitehouse.gov/stargate/) It also represents the theory that if you build enough data centers, the intelligence will take care of itself.

The second was the release of DeepSeek R1 in January 2025 — a model that matched GPT-4 on key benchmarks while costing roughly one-fourteenth as much to train. Marc Andreessen called it "AI's Sputnik moment." [Andreessen on DeepSeek, Jan 2025](https://a16z.com/deepseek-sputnik/) The Sputnik analogy was apt not because DeepSeek was technologically superior (Sputnik was a very simple satellite; DeepSeek is a genuinely impressive model), but because it changed the perceived structure of the competition. Suddenly, it was not obvious that the entity with the most money would win. A Sputnik moment, one recalls, is what happens when the other side demonstrates that your theory of the competition is incomplete.

DeepSeek V4, released in late April 2026, extended the pattern. The model scored 87 on BenchLM.ai's unified leaderboard, within striking distance of the top Western proprietary models (Claude Mythos Preview at 99, Gemini 3.1 Pro at 93, GPT-5.4 at 92). And it did so at one-tenth the inference cost. [BenchLM.ai, April 2026](https://benchlm.ai/blog/posts/state-of-llm-benchmarks-2026) One-tenth the cost for nearly the same result is the kind of mathematics that tends to concentrate the mind.

The $5.6 million question — literal for DeepSeek R1's training cost — is whether the US capital-centric theory of AI progress is wrong, or merely incomplete. The data from 2025 and early 2026 suggests it is not wrong, but that its dominance is passing. Capital still matters. But the returns to capital are declining, and the returns to efficiency, talent, and institutional coordination are rising. It is a curious thing to spend $700 billion and discover that the most expensive strategy is not necessarily the winning one.

## X. Forecast: Four Scenarios for 2030

Any forecast in this space must be hedged; the rate of change is too high for certainty. But the data — historical and contemporary — supports four structurally distinct scenarios. What follows is less a prediction than a set of warnings.

**Scenario 1: Capital Wins (Probability: 30%)** — The US spending advantage proves decisive. The 23:1 investment ratio translates into a persistent model performance gap of 5–10 percentage points. Chinese chip development falls further behind; export controls continue to bite. The US maintains frontier model leadership through 2030, and the $700 billion annual capex is absorbed by AI revenue that materializes as forecast. This is the outcome that current US policy assumes. It is also the outcome that requires everything to go right.

**Scenario 2: Efficiency Wins (Probability: 35%)** — The Chinese efficiency advantage overwhelms the US spending advantage. By 2028, the best Chinese and American models are functionally indistinguishable on most benchmarks. China achieves de facto AI parity before it achieves parity in chip production. The US leads in revenue from AI (due to larger commercial ecosystems), but China leads in AI capability and lower-cost deployment. The competition resolves into a split: the US dominates the market for AI; China dominates the infrastructure for it. The split is not a tie, but it is not a win either.

**Scenario 3: The Overbuild Crisis (Probability: 20%)** — The telecom bubble scenario. AI revenue grows, but not fast enough to absorb $700 billion in annual capex. The hyperscalers begin writing down data center assets in 2028–2029. Frontier labs consolidate. One or more of the four hyperscalers either exits the AI race or merges. The resulting asset impairment exceeds $500 billion, triggering a broader technology sector correction. The long-term effect is similar to the telecom bust: the infrastructure is eventually utilized, but the investors who financed it lose most of their capital. Those who mistake a correction for a collapse are usually those who invested largest.

**Scenario 4: The Third Pole Emerges (Probability: 15%)** — Neither the US nor China dominates. The Gulf states (primarily UAE and Saudi Arabia) emerge as a "compute safe haven," hosting data centers that serve both American and Chinese AI companies. The EU becomes the dominant regulatory voice, setting global standards for AI safety that both US and Chinese companies follow. AI becomes multipolar — not because any single actor declines, but because the resource requirements (capital, energy, talent, chips) are too distributed for any single country to monopolize. The world, it turns out, does not organize itself according to the convenience of American strategists.

## XI. What History Actually Teaches

The historian will note that every great technology race in American history — from the transcontinental railroad to the Manhattan Project to the Apollo program to the internet — was initially viewed as a contest between rivals and eventually recognized as a transformation of everything. The space race was not really about beating the Soviet Union to the moon. It was about creating the institutional and technological infrastructure for satellite communications, Earth observation, and planetary science — capabilities that the Soviet Union could not match even after Apollo ended. The winners discovered that the race had never been about the destination.

The AI race will likely follow the same pattern. The framing of the competition — US versus China, capital versus efficiency, proprietary versus open — is a useful narrative but a misleading analytical framework. The real outcome of the $700 billion build-out will not be who "wins" AI. It will be what kind of AI infrastructure gets built in the process, who controls access to it, and what it enables that was previously impossible. The prize is not victory; the prize is the world the race leaves behind.

The data suggests that the United States has a narrowing window in which its capital advantage is meaningful — perhaps two to three years before Chinese efficiency gains and talent pipeline advantages close the capability gap. The question is not whether the US can maintain its lead indefinitely. The data says it cannot. The question is whether the US can use its remaining window of advantage to build the governance frameworks, safety infrastructure, and responsible deployment practices that will shape AI regardless of who builds the best models. The lead is not a prize; it is a responsibility, and one that has rarely been exercised well.

If it does not, then the $700 billion will have purchased the most expensive short-term advantage in history, while the long-term architecture of AI — who makes it, who controls it, who decides what it is allowed to do — is decided elsewhere. There is nothing more expensive than a short-term victory purchased at the cost of long-term influence.

The $700 billion question, in the end, is not about money. It is about whether the people spending it understand what they are actually trying to buy. And that, perhaps, is the most unsettling question of all.
