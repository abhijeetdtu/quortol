# The People's Garbage: Why Slop Has Always Been Democratic

---

On a Thursday afternoon in the spring of 2024, a medical student in India opened a dashboard and watched the numbers climb. He was not yet a doctor, nor a programmer, nor a filmmaker — though he had stumbled upon a truth that each of those professions guards carefully: that a convincing imitation of reality is often easier to produce than the real thing. He was the creator of "Emily Hart," an entirely synthetic American influencer whose image he generated using Midjourney, whose captions he wrote with ChatGPT, and whose audience consisted mostly of conservative American men who believed they were looking at a real woman. The posts featured MAGA rhetoric, scripture quotes, and carefully composed lifestyle shots that had never been photographed because the woman in them had never existed. The student was making thousands of dollars a month [404 Media, August 2024](https://www.404media.co/). The men were getting what they wanted. So was he. The only person who did not exist was the only one who mattered.

Half a world away, a Kenyan creator opened ChatGPT and typed in all caps: "WRITE ME 10 PROMPT PICTURE OF JESUS WHICH WILLING BRING HIGH ENGAGEMENT ON FACEBOOK." He fed the output into Midjourney, collected the images, and posted them to Facebook pages where older users commented "Amen" on pictures of a savior who was also a shrimp. "Shrimp Jesus" became, briefly, one of the most-viewed things on the internet [New York Magazine, September 2024](https://nymag.com/intelligencer/article/ai-slop.html). The faithful did not notice the absurdity. Or perhaps they noticed and did not care. Faith, after all, has always been a willingness to believe in what one cannot verify. The internet merely made the arrangement explicit.

The reaction from the commentariat was swift and dismissive. This was "AI slop" — defined by Merriam-Webster as "digital content of low quality that is produced usually in quantity by means of artificial intelligence" — and crowned the dictionary's 2025 Word of the Year [Merriam-Webster, December 2025](https://www.merriam-webster.com/word-of-the-year). Simon Willison, the developer who helped popularize the term, defined it more precisely: content that is "mindlessly generated and thrust upon someone who didn't ask for it" [Simon Willison, May 2024](https://simonwillison.net/2024/May/8/slop/). *The Atlantic* worried about cultural pollution. *The Guardian* ran headlines about algorithm collapse. YouTube's CEO Neal Mohan used the term himself in his 2026 annual letter, promising to crack down on "low-quality, aka AI slop" [YouTube CEO Letter, January 2026](https://news.slashdot.org/story/26/01/21/1422227/youtube-ceo-acknowledges-ai-slop-problem-says-platform-will-curb-low-quality-ai-content). Every generation invents a word for the garbage it did not grow up with. This generation's word rhymes with its panic.

The panic is understandable. The scale is unprecedented: Kapwing's analysis of YouTube found that 21% of videos recommended to new users are AI-generated slop, collectively earning an estimated $117 million annually [Kapwing, December 2025](https://the-decoder.com/one-in-five-youtube-shorts-shown-to-new-users-is-ai-generated-slop-study-finds/). On TikTok, the figure jumps to 59% of For You feed content [Kapwing, June 2026](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/tiktok-shows-3x-more-ai-slop-than-youtube-report-finds/579521/). The dead internet theory — the paranoid thought that the web is now mostly machines talking to machines — no longer sounds quite so paranoid. It sounds, more disturbingly, like a market projection.

But panic has a blind spot. It mistakes a change in volume for a change in kind. The truth is that slop — mass-produced, low-quality, formulaic content — is not a new phenomenon. It is as old as mass media itself. What changes is who gets to produce it, who gets to call it garbage, and who gets to decide what the garbage means. Every new media technology has produced a flood of what elites considered worthless dross. And every time, some of that dross turned out to be the soil from which something new grew. The gatekeepers cry deluge; history smiles and takes notes.

This is the history that the slop panic erases. It is worth recovering.

## The Original Slop Economy

The word "Grub Street" first appeared in English print in the 1630s, designating a real street in London's Moorfields district. By the time Samuel Johnson published his dictionary in 1755, it had already become a metaphor. Johnson defined it as a place "much inhabited by writers of small histories, dictionaries, and temporary poems; whence any mean production is called grubstreet" [Johnson's Dictionary, 1755](https://www.grubstreetproject.net/). Note the word "temporary." Even the lexicographer knew that most of what is written is meant to be forgotten.

These were the original slop merchants. They churned out pamphlets, satires, political tracts, sensational crime stories, and hagiographies of the recently deceased — whatever would sell. They wrote on deadline, often drunk, always desperate. Alexander Pope devoted his epic poem *The Dunciad* to mocking them, portraying Grub Street hacks as agents of cultural darkness, "dunces" whose proliferation threatened civilization itself [Pope, *The Dunciad*, 1728-1743](https://www.grubstreetproject.net/essays/grubstreetmyth/). Pope's complaint was aesthetic; his real grievance was the opening of the gates. When everyone can publish, the genius must share the street with the dunce. This has always been intolerable to geniuses, and the dunces have always outnumbered them.

But here is what the moral panic of Pope's era misses: those hacks were building something. They were the first fully professional writers in English history — freelancers who supported themselves entirely by their pens, beholden to no patron, accountable only to the market. They invented the periodical press, the political pamphlet, the serialized novel. Daniel Defoe, the author of *Robinson Crusoe*, began as a Grub Street journalist. Jonathan Swift, who mocked them alongside Pope, was himself a political pamphleteer. Eliza Haywood, one of the most prolific and commercially successful novelists of the 18th century, was a Grub Street veteran [Grub Street Project](https://www.grubstreetproject.net/). The dunces, it turned out, were inventing the future. The satirist and the satirized were made of the same ink.

The French historian Robert Darnton has documented a parallel world in pre-revolutionary Paris: a "Grub Street" of impoverished writers living in garrets, churning out libels, pornography, and seditious pamphlets. The literary establishment dismissed them as hack scribblers. Darnton argues that their relentless production of scandalous, anti-aristocratic material helped create the intellectual conditions for the French Revolution [Darnton, *The Literary Underground of the Old Regime*, 1982](https://paris.pias.science/article/grub-street-revisited-1). The hacks did not storm the Bastille. They simply made the Bastille look ridiculous enough that no one wished to defend it.

What looks like garbage from above looks like a living from below. What looks like cultural degradation to the gatekeeper looks like opportunity to the excluded. Perspective is the only thing that separates a hack from a pioneer, and perspective depends entirely on where one stands.

## The Assembly Line as Art School

In 1935, Warner Bros. decided that half of its entire film output would consist of B-movies. The studio had a dedicated B-unit headed by Bryan Foy, known as the "Keeper of the B's." These films were made on two-to-five-week schedules, on budgets as low as $30,000, with second-tier stars and rigidly formulaic plots. They were designed to fill the bottom half of double features — and to make money on flat rental fees that guaranteed profitability regardless of quality [Wikipedia, B Movies (Hollywood Golden Age)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B_movies_(Hollywood_Golden_Age)). The operative word was "regardless." Quality was not the point. It never is, at the bottom of the bill.

Critics dismissed them as assembly-line product. They were right about the assembly line. But the B-movie unit served functions that the critics did not see. It kept studios running during the Depression, when every dollar of stable income mattered. It trained directors, writers, and technicians who would go on to make A-pictures. It served as a laboratory for genre conventions — horror, film noir, the western — that would define American cinema for decades. The dregs taught the masters their trade.

The Harvard Film Archive, looking back at the era, argues that the B-film "needs to be recognized as a unique and quintessentially American art form, albeit one created largely by emigres and artists whose work and careers remained on the margins" [Harvard Film Archive, *The B-Film: Low-Budget Hollywood Cinema 1935-1959*](https://harvardfilmarchive.org/programs/the-b-film). The B-movie's low status was precisely what allowed for experimentation. No one was watching. No one cared. So directors could take risks that would never be permitted on a prestige picture. Neglect, it turns out, is a kind of freedom.

The great irony of cultural gatekeeping is that it often protects mediocrity as much as it protects quality. The B-movie unit produced vast quantities of forgettable dross. It also produced genuine art — not despite the formula, but through it. The formula was the vessel; the art was what sometimes filled it.

## The Masala Defense

No film industry in history has been dismissed by Western critics as thoroughly as Bollywood. The charges are familiar: formulaic, melodramatic, excessive, lacking psychological depth, stuffed with song-and-dance numbers that have nothing to do with the plot. The term "masala film" — referring to the mix of flavors in Indian cooking — was originally used by critics as a derogatory label for Bollywood's genre-blending, emotionally maximalist style. The implied insult was that the audience had the palate of a tourist.

But as the scholar Rosie Thomas has noted, these criticisms reveal more about the critic than the cinema. "What seems to be needed," Thomas wrote, "is an analysis which takes seriously both the films and the pleasures they offer" [Thomas, cited in scholarly analysis of Bollywood aesthetics](https://api.taylorfrancis.com/content/books/mono/download?identifierName=doi&identifierValue=10.4324%2F9780429293726&type=googlepdf). It is a remarkable demand: that one actually attend to what an audience enjoys rather than explain why they are wrong to enjoy it.

The masala formula, it turns out, is not a failure to achieve Western realism. It is rooted in the Natyashastra, an ancient Sanskrit treatise on dramaturgy that prescribes the evocation of multiple *rasas* (emotional essences) in a single work. Bollywood's blending of comedy, tragedy, romance, action, and music is not aesthetic chaos but a sophisticated inheritance — one that Western film theory is only beginning to appreciate [IAFOR Journal of Cultural Studies](https://iafor.org/archives/journals/iafor-journal-of-cultural-studies/10.22492.ijcs.10.1.07.pdf). The formula, in other words, has a pedigree the critics lack.

Arjun Appadurai, the anthropologist, has written about the "ready-made pleasures of déjà vu" in Bollywood — the way formula can produce genuine pleasure through repetition with variation [Appadurai, *Cambridge Journal of Postcolonial Literary Inquiry*, 2019](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-journal-of-postcolonial-literary-inquiry/article/abs/repetition-with-a-difference-a-response-to-arjun-appadurai/EBF766A85159C047119F6EAA0C210FE7). The critic who sees only formula misses what audiences actually experience: a rhythmic alternation between the familiar and the new, anchored by the reassurance of genre while surprised by moments of invention. The same could be said of a sonnet, a concerto, or a prayer. Repetition is not the enemy of meaning. It is often the condition of it.

The point is not that the formula is always good. It is that the formula is not always bad — and that the people who consume formulaic content are not passive dupes but active participants in a cultural system that works differently from the one elite critics prefer. Dismissing the masala film as Bollywood slop is itself a kind of provincialism. The provincial, as always, mistakes his own habits for universal standards.

## The Least Objectionable Program

The most honest theory of broadcast television was developed in the 1960s by Paul Klein, an executive at NBC. He called it the theory of the "Least Objectionable Program," or LOP. Klein argued that viewers do not choose specific programs so much as they choose to watch television itself. The audience is in a kind of partial trance. Programmers should therefore worry less about giving viewers something to like than about giving them nothing to dislike enough to change the channel [Wikipedia, Least Objectionable Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_objectionable_program). It is a theory that treats the audience with perfect honesty: you are not discerning, you are merely inert. And it was correct.

This is a remarkably cynical theory of cultural production — and it was remarkably accurate. The three-network era produced vast quantities of bland, formulaic, risk-averse programming explicitly designed not to offend. The creative community hated it. Critics hated it. But it worked: the same percentage of American homes had their televisions on at 9 PM on Thursday, year after year, regardless of what was actually airing. The content was irrelevant. The habit was everything.

And yet, from this wasteland, quality television emerged. The "rural purge" of the early 1970s — in which CBS canceled popular but older-skewing shows like *Hee Haw* and *Petticoat Junction* in favor of younger-skewing "relevant" comedies — was a direct response to Klein's demographic insights. *All in the Family*, *The Mary Tyler Moore Show*, *M\*A\*S\*H* — some of the most acclaimed television ever made — came from network executives trying to optimize for a 31 share instead of a 38 share, targeting the *Washington Post* audience rather than the *National Tattler* audience [*Western Humanities Review*, Fall 2016](https://www.westernhumanitiesreview.com/fall-2016-70-3/quality-tv-as-liberal-tv/). Great art, in this account, was a demographic accident. The executives did not want to make history. They wanted to sell soap.

The formulaic environment was not the enemy of quality. It was the precondition. Without the economic stability of LOP programming, the risk-taking of the 1970s would have been unaffordable. The bland subsidized the brilliant. The mediocre paid for the masterpiece. The system that seemed designed to prevent art was, in the end, the only system that could afford it.

## Churnalism and the 12% Rule

In 2008, the journalist Nick Davies published *Flat Earth News*, a devastating investigation of British journalism. His central finding: most news stories were not reported in any meaningful sense. They were recycled from press releases and wire services — "churnalism," a term coined by BBC journalist Waseem Zakir to describe the process of "processing stuff and maybe adding the odd local quote" [Wikipedia, Churnalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Churnalism). The definition is charming in its modesty; it describes the profession's central activity as a clerical task.

The most shocking number in Davies' book came from a Cardiff University study: only 12% of British news stories showed any evidence that the central factual claims had been independently checked [Nick Davies, *Flat Earth News*, 2008](https://pressgazette.co.uk/publishers/nationals/nick-davies-churnalism-has-taken-the-place-of-what-we-should-be-doing-telling-the-truth-40117/). The other 88% were, by any honest measure, a form of slop. The fourth estate was running on fumes.

This was the state of journalism before AI. The wires and PR machines were already producing the majority of what people read. The "authentic" journalism of daily newspapers was, to a shocking degree, already slop — produced under conditions of staffing cuts and output pressure that made original reporting a luxury few could afford. The noble profession was already a recycling operation. The only scandal was that anyone was surprised.

The response to Davies' book was not to fix the system but to accept it. The churnalism.com website, launched in 2011 by the Media Standards Trust, let users paste press releases and compare them to published articles. It found that 89% of a *Mail Online* story was cut-and-pasted from an Asda press release; 97% of another came from a OnePoll release [Press Gazette, February 2011](https://pressgazette.co.uk/publishers/nationals/mail-online-under-fire-from-new-churnalism-website/). The technology of exposure did not shame anyone into change. It merely confirmed what everyone already suspected and no one wished to discuss.

The point is not to condemn churnalism but to understand it. Local newspapers cannot afford to send reporters to city council meetings. Trade magazines cannot fact-check every product claim. The churn is not a failure of integrity; it is a structural response to economic pressure. Before generative AI, the information ecosystem was already running on automated, recycled, minimally-vetted content. The machines did not create the problem. They inherited it. As with so many inheritances, the heirs were blamed for what the ancestors left behind.

## The Platform Economy's Double Edge

Vimeo was founded in 2004 as a haven for filmmakers — a YouTube alternative that prioritized quality over quantity, creators over consumers, discretion over virality. Its Terms of Service banned commercial content, gaming videos, and anything that wasn't original. Staff Picks, its curated front page, became a career accelerant for a generation of independent directors. The promise was that purity would protect them.

By 2022, Vimeo had pivoted to enterprise SaaS. Its self-serve subscribers — the individual creators who built its reputation — collapsed from 1.5 million to approximately 53,500 by Q3 2024, a 96% decline [CineD, January 2026](https://www.cined.com/how-vimeo-betrayed-the-filmmakers-who-built-it/). The platform that had promised to protect filmmakers from the slop economy had become a business tool for the corporations those filmmakers worked for. The artists were not saved; they were evicted. The sanctuary became a trade show.

Vimeo's story is a cautionary tale, but not the one it appears to be. The platform that tried to wall itself off from slop discovered that purity is not a business model. YouTube, for all its algorithmic chaos, remained open to anyone. That openness produced the K-Pop fan armies, the educational channels, the independent news operations, and the millions of creators who make a living — modest, precarious, but real — from the same algorithmic firehose that now delivers AI slop to one in five new users. The open door lets in the storm. But it also lets in the light.

The slop is not separable from the opportunity. They come from the same infrastructure. The B-movie unit and the A-picture were produced by the same studio. The Grub Street hack and the Grub Street poet shared the same street. The algorithm that surfaces a bad AI video is the same algorithm that surfaces a good one. The question is not how to eliminate slop. The question is how to ensure that the system remains open enough that what comes next — whatever it is — is not locked out because it looked, at first, like garbage. The gatekeepers always want to close the gate. But the gate is where everything interesting happens.

## The Aesthetic Case for Slop

In 1936, Walter Benjamin published "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction," arguing that the technological reproducibility of art was destroying its "aura" — its unique presence in time and space — but also liberating art from its "parasitic subservience to ritual" [Benjamin, 1936](https://web.mit.edu/allanmc/www/benjamin.pdf). Mass reproduction, Benjamin thought, could be democratic. It could bring art to people who had never had access to it. The photograph replaced the painting; the film replaced the stage; the people, Benjamin hoped, would replace the priest.

Eight years later, Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer published "The Culture Industry: Enlightenment as Mass Deception," arguing the opposite: that mass culture was a system of standardized, assembly-line products designed to keep the masses docile. "Under monopoly all mass culture is identical," they wrote, "and the lines of its artificial framework begin to show through" [Adorno & Horkheimer, *Dialectic of Enlightenment*, 1944](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/adorno/1944/culture-industry.htm). The priests, in this account, had not been replaced. They had merely gone corporate.

Both were right. Benjamin saw the democratic potential. Adorno saw the industrial reality. The tension between these two visions has structured every subsequent debate about mass culture — and it structures the slop debate today. The question is not which philosopher was correct. It is whether democracy or industry will win the argument, because they have been arguing since the printing press and neither has yet prevailed.

The 2026 paper "Why Slop Matters," published on arXiv, makes an explicitly Benjaminian argument. Its authors identify three features of AI slop — "superficial competence, asymmetric effort, and mass producibility" — and argue that slop "has its own aesthetic value" and "offers a legitimate means of collective sense-making" [arXiv, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.06060). The paper draws a direct parallel to other "low" cultural forms that were initially dismissed by critics before being recognized as significant. Slop may not be art in the way we recognize art. But neither was the novel, at first. Neither was cinema. Neither was television. The category "art" has always been a waiting room. The door opens slowly, and only after the righteous have finished complaining.

*The Scientific American*, in a 2025 essay, traced this pattern across centuries. The biblical book of Ecclesiastes complains about the endless production of books. The 18th century's Grub Street produced "mean productions" that elites mocked. The 20th century's kitsch was deplored by modernists. "Mass-produced culture has a long, messy history," the essay concludes — and "trash and treasure have appeared in the same stream" [*Scientific American*, November 2025](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/ai-slop-how-every-media-revolution-breeds-rubbish-and-art/). The stream does not distinguish between trash and treasure. Only the critic does, and only in hindsight.

This is the democratic argument for slop. Not that it is good — much of it is genuinely bad. But that the people who produce it, and the people who consume it, are not making a mistake. They are participating in a cultural economy that works differently from the one elite critics prefer. The Kenyan creator generating Jesus images for Facebook is not a vandal of culture. He is a participant in it — operating at a scale and speed that previous generations could not have imagined, producing something that satisfies a demand that the gatekeepers did not know existed. The gatekeepers are always the last to know what people actually want. They are too busy telling them what they should want instead.

## The Slop That Remains

In the summer of 2024, when "Shrimp Jesus" was peaking, a Facebook user named Hazel Thayer shared screenshots of her feed on TikTok. "I don't think anybody is going 'Hallelujah' for our shrimp lord," she said [NBC News, March 2024](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/facebook-users-say-amen-bizarre-ai-generated-images-jesus-rcna143965). The clip went viral. The response was a mix of horror and delight — horror that this was happening, delight that it was so strange. The horror was from those who understood the implications. The delight was from those who understood the joke. Both were correct.

What happens next? The platforms are scrambling. YouTube has promised algorithmic demotion for AI slop. Meta has introduced labeling systems. TikTok has added user controls. None of these measures will fully work, because the economic incentives are too strong. The Kapwing study found that slop channels earn an estimated $4.25 million annually at the top end [TrendTechDaily, December 2025](https://trendtechdaily.com/report-unveils-how-youtubes-new-ai-slop-economy-generates-millions/). The medical student in India, the Kenyan prompt engineer, the content farmers in Pakistan and the Philippines — they are not producing slop because they hate culture. They are producing it because it pays better than the alternatives available to them. Morality is a luxury good. When survival is at stake, people produce whatever the algorithm rewards.

This is the democratic dimension that the panic obscures. The slop debate is not really about quality. It is about access. Who gets to produce culture? Who gets to distribute it? Who gets to decide what counts? The gatekeepers of each era — the Popes, the Adornos, the film critics, the journalism professors, the platform CEOs — have always had good reasons to fear the flood. And the flood has always come anyway. The dam is the story they tell themselves. The water is the story that history tells.

The Grub Street hacks that Pope mocked in 1728 are not remembered as dunces. They are remembered — if they are remembered at all — as the first professional writers, the inventors of the commercial press, the people who made it possible to live by the pen. The B-movie directors of the 1930s are not remembered as hacks. They are remembered — when they are remembered — as the craftsmen who taught themselves cinema by making it, quickly and cheaply and against expectations. The Bollywood "masala" films are not remembered as formulaic dross. They are remembered as the most globally beloved expression of Indian culture. The dunces of one century become the pioneers of the next. The hacks become the canon. It is the critics who are forgotten.

We do not know yet what will survive of the current slop. Most of it will not. That has always been true. But something will. Some form of expression, some new aesthetic, some way of using these tools to say something that matters — it will emerge from the same flood that terrifies the critics. It always does. The flood is not the enemy of the garden. The flood is what the garden drinks.

The panic is real. The disruption is real. The cheapening of attention, the degradation of discourse, the pollution of the cultural commons — these are genuine harms. But they are not the whole story. The rest of the story is about the thousands of people who now have access to tools of cultural production that were once reserved for professionals with capital, credentials, and connections. They are using those tools badly, mostly. But some of them will use them well. And some of what they make will outlast the panic. The mediocre is the price of the extraordinary. It always has been. The only difference is that now the price is visible.

The slop is democratic. That is its offense. That is also its promise.
