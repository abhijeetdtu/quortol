# The Prescription Pad and the Controller: What Clinical Science Has Learned About Video Games as Medicine

The first time a video game was prescribed as medicine, it looked exactly like a video game. This, perhaps, is the most honest thing about it. On June 15, 2020, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration cleared EndeavorRx — a tablet-based game — for children aged 8 to 12 with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder. It was the first time the agency had authorized a digital therapeutic delivered through a game interface for any medical condition. [FDA De Novo Classification, DEN200026](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/reviews/DEN200026.pdf)

This was not a gamified version of an existing therapy — not a point-scoring variant of cognitive behavioral therapy, not a meditation app with achievements attached. EndeavorRx was built from the ground up as a neurocognitive intervention. Its core mechanism, developed by neuroscientists at Akili Interactive, is a set of adaptive algorithms designed to target the neural systems underlying attention and cognitive control. Children play by navigating a character through obstacle courses, collecting targets and ignoring distractions, while the difficulty adjusts in real time to the child's performance — calibrating the cognitive load to stay precisely between boredom and frustration. That therapeutic window, it turns out, is where the brain changes.

The pivotal trial, published in *The Lancet Digital Health*, randomized 348 children with ADHD to either EndeavorRx or a digital control — a game that looked identical and rewarded identically but lacked the adaptive algorithms. After four weeks of treatment — 25 minutes per day, five days per week — the EndeavorRx group showed a statistically significant improvement on the Test of Variables of Attention Attention Performance Index (TOVA API), the study's primary endpoint: a mean change of 0.93 in the treatment group versus 0.03 in the control group (p = 0.006). The effects were not massive. They were not a cure. But they were measurable, replicable, and — crucially — achieved without the side effect profile of stimulant medication. One-third of children who completed treatment no longer showed a measurable attention deficit on at least one objective measure of attention. [Akili Interactive, FDA Clearance Announcement, 2020](https://www.akiliinteractive.com/news-collection/akili-announces-endeavortm-attention-treatment-is-now-available-for-children-with-attention-deficit-hyperactivity-disorder-adhd-al3pw)

Since that decision, the evidence has continued to accumulate. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis in *Pediatrics* found that game-based digital therapeutics improved inattention more than control conditions, with a standardized mean difference of 0.28 (95% CI 0.14-0.41) as rated by parents and teachers. A 2024 open-label trial in adolescents aged 13-17 showed similar benefits, with TOVA-ACS scores improving by a mean of 2.64 points (SD = 3.80) over four weeks. [PubMed: Effects of game-based DTx on ADHD, 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36862162/)

But perhaps the most telling data came from the question parents answer every day, often without data: did they see a difference? In the STARS-Adjunct open-label study, 56% of parents reported that EndeavorRx improved their child's attention. Nearly half saw a clinically meaningful change in their child's day-to-day impairments after one month. After two months, that figure rose to 68%. [Akili Interactive, STARS-Adjunct Data, 2020](https://www.akiliinteractive.com/news-collection/researchers-present-new-outcome-data-for-akili-digital-therapeutic-endeavorrx-in-pediatric-adhd)

EndeavorRx is the most prominent example, but it is far from the only one. Across the past decade, a growing body of clinical research has been quietly making the case that video games — the same medium parents have been worrying about since long before they stopped worrying about Pong — may offer a genuinely new approach to treating some of the most intractable problems in mental health. The medium that has been blamed for everything may, under the right conditions, be part of the treatment for everything.

## The Depression Game

In 2012, a team of researchers in New Zealand published a randomized controlled non-inferiority trial in *The BMJ* that compared a video game against standard counselling for adolescent depression. The game was called SPARX — an acronym for Smart, Positive, Active, Realistic, X-factor thoughts — and it was built around a disarmingly simple idea: what if you could deliver cognitive behavioral therapy through a fantasy game?

The trial enrolled 187 adolescents aged 12 to 19 who were seeking help for depression. Half received SPARX, delivered in seven modules over four to seven weeks. The other half received treatment as usual — primarily face-to-face counseling. The results surprised even the researchers. SPARX was not merely as effective as usual care; on some measures, it performed better. Remission rates — the proportion of participants who no longer met the threshold for clinical depression — were 43.7% in the SPARX group versus 26.4% in the treatment-as-usual group (difference 17.3%, 95% CI 1.6% to 31.8%, p = 0.030). Improvements were maintained at three-month follow-up. [Merry et al., BMJ 2012](https://www.bmj.com/content/344/bmj.e2598)

The mechanism was not magic. SPARX did not "cure" depression through gameplay alone. It taught cognitive behavioral therapy skills — identifying unhelpful thoughts, challenging them, replacing them with more balanced alternatives — through an interactive fantasy narrative. Players navigated a virtual world, completed quests, and defeated "Gloomy Negative Automatic Thoughts" (GNATs, in the game's own taxonomy, which is either whimsical or honest depending on how you feel about the creatures living in your own mind). The game was a delivery system for an evidence-based therapy, not a replacement for one. But it was a delivery system that adolescents actually wanted to use — a fact that, in the context of youth mental health treatment adherence, is almost comically significant. It is difficult to persuade a teenager to attend weekly therapy. It is considerably less difficult to persuade them to finish a quest.

Since then, the evidence for SPARX has accumulated across multiple populations and settings. A 2017 school-based randomized controlled trial in Australia, published in the *Journal of Medical Internet Research*, tested a version called SPARX-R as a preventive intervention for depression in 540 final-year secondary students facing the stress of end-of-school exams. The results showed significantly reduced depression symptoms relative to the control group at post-intervention (Cohen's d = 0.29) and at six-month follow-up (d = 0.21). [Perry et al., JMIR 2017](https://www.jmir.org/2017/11/e369) A 2023 pilot randomized controlled trial in Inuit youth in Nunavut found that SPARX reduced hopelessness (p = 0.02), self-blame (p = 0.03), rumination (p = 0.04), and catastrophizing (p = 0.03) — the cognitive patterns that fuel depression. [Bohr et al., JMIR Serious Games 2023](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/32171/) And in 2024, a UK-based feasibility trial launched to test SPARX in England with the support of an e-coach, aiming to address one of the remaining problems: engagement in real-world settings, where therapy must compete with life. [SPARX-UK Protocol, 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40814-024-01475-7)

SPARX is not alone. A 2023 randomized trial published in *Frontiers in Psychiatry* tested a different kind of gaming intervention for depression — Super Mario Odyssey on the Nintendo Switch — in 46 clinically depressed adults. Participants played for 45 minutes, three times per week, for six weeks. The results were striking: the only group that showed a significant decrease in the proportion of participants with clinical depression (as measured by the Beck Depression Inventory) was the 3D video gaming group. The active control group (CogPack, a computerized cognitive training program) and the treatment-as-usual group did not show the same effect. Training motivation was significantly higher in the video game group — participants wanted to play, because the activity was intrinsically rewarding. Nintendo, perhaps without intending to, appears to have produced an antidepressant.

The broader meta-analytic picture supports these findings. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis in *JAMA Pediatrics* examined 27 randomized controlled trials of gamified digital mental health interventions across 2,911 children and adolescents. The results showed modest but significant effects on ADHD (Hedges' g = 0.28, 95% CI 0.09 to 0.48) and depression (g = 0.28, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.47). The effect on anxiety was smaller and non-significant (g = 0.07, 95% CI -0.02 to 0.17). [JAMA Pediatrics 2024](https://www.ovid.com/journals/japed/pdf/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2024.3139) A separate meta-analysis in *BJPsych Open* found a stronger effect specifically for depression in young people aged 12-25, with a Hedge's g of −0.54 (95% CI −1.00 to −0.08) favoring gaming interventions over control conditions. [BJPsych Open 2024](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-open/article/effectiveness-of-gaming-interventions-for-depression-and-anxiety-in-young-people-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis/7206B6C2FCB40355582267D37A9B7231)

The pattern is consistent: video games are not the most powerful intervention for depression — they do not outperform the best medications or the most rigorously delivered psychotherapy. But they are consistently effective, and they solve a problem that those treatments do not: the problem of getting people to show up. The best therapy in the world achieves nothing if the patient does not attend.

## Rewiring the Schizophrenic Brain

If video games can help with ADHD and depression, the next question — the harder question — is whether they can help with the most severe mental illnesses.

In May 2024, a team of German researchers published a study in *Translational Psychiatry* that tested exactly this proposition. They recruited patients with schizophrenia — a disorder characterized by profound cognitive deficits, reduced neural plasticity, and some of the most disabling symptoms in all of medicine — and randomly assigned them to eight weeks of video game training or an active control condition (reading e-books). The video game was a platformer — a 2D or 3D game in which players navigated a character through obstacles, collected rewards, and advanced through increasingly difficult levels. Patients played for 30 minutes daily. [Becker et al., Translational Psychiatry 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41398-024-02945-5)

The results were remarkable by any standard of schizophrenia research. Both video game groups showed significant increases in sustained attention compared to the control condition. The improvement correlated with increased functional connectivity in a hippocampal-prefrontal network — the same brain circuit that is typically impaired in schizophrenia and that is critical for learning and memory. But the clinical improvements went beyond cognition. Patients who trained with video games showed significant improvements in negative symptoms — the constellation of flattened affect, social withdrawal, and loss of motivation that is often more disabling than hallucinations or delusions, and for which existing treatments are largely ineffective. They also showed improvements in general psychopathology and, most tellingly, in perceived mental health recovery — the patient's own sense that they were getting better. It is one thing to measure improvement on a scale. It is another for the patient to feel it.

The authors were measured in their conclusions, but the implication was clear: "Videogames, incorporating initiative, goal setting and gratification, offer a training approach closer to real life than current psychiatric treatments." A treatment that patients actually want to engage with — that activates the brain's reward systems rather than suppressing them — is not merely a convenience. It may be a fundamentally different therapeutic strategy from the pharmacological approaches that have dominated psychiatry for seventy years. The history of psychiatry is largely the history of finding ways to make patients comply. Games suggest a different approach: making the treatment something the patient does not need to be persuaded to do.

Subsequent studies have reinforced the finding. A 2024 study in the same journal tested a game-based cognitive training program called Komori Life in inpatients with schizophrenia and found improvements in attentional bias toward threatening stimuli — a core feature of the disorder's cognitive profile. [Wang et al., Translational Psychiatry 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41398-024-02920-0) A 2025 randomized controlled trial found that smartphone video games for one hour daily, five days per week for six weeks, significantly improved cognitive function in middle-aged and elderly patients with chronic schizophrenia (p < 0.001), with measurable changes in serum biomarkers including GDNF, Tau, MIP-1, and MIP-4 — suggesting that the cognitive improvements had a biological correlate. [Smartphone Video Games for Schizophrenia, PMC 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12008215/) And an immersive puzzle video game study using Angry Birds — three 50-minute sessions per week for 12 weeks — improved cognitive flexibility, postural control, and self-efficacy in patients with schizophrenia spectrum disorders, with medium to large effect sizes. [Chern et al., Games for Health Journal 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1089/g4h.2024.0173)

The schizophrenia research is still early. The sample sizes are small. The effects are modest. But the direction of the evidence is unmistakable: the same neural plasticity that video games can enhance in healthy brains appears to be accessible, to some degree, in brains that psychiatry has long considered resistant to repair. The brain that medicine had given up on may not have given up on itself.

## The Aging Brain

If video games can help repair the schizophrenic brain, a growing body of research suggests they may also help preserve the aging one.

A meta-analysis published in *JMIR Serious Games* in 2024 examined 14 randomized controlled trials involving 714 older adults with Alzheimer's disease and mild cognitive impairment. The results were striking: digital serious game therapy significantly improved cognitive ability as measured by the Mini-Mental State Examination (SMD 2.11, 95% CI 1.42 to 2.80, p < 0.001) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (SMD 2.75, 95% CI 1.98 to 3.51, p < 0.001). It also increased daily behavioral capacity (SMD 0.53, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.99, p = 0.03) and reduced depression (SMD −2.08, 95% CI −2.94 to −1.22, p < 0.001). [JMIR Serious Games 2024](https://games.jmir.org/2024/1/e55785/)

These are large effect sizes — larger than those seen in most pharmacological trials for cognitive decline. The caveat, which the authors note carefully, is that the quality of the evidence was limited by small sample sizes and variability in the games used. But the consistency of the signal across multiple independent trials suggests something real. Science progresses one underpowered study at a time, and sometimes the pattern becomes visible before the proof is complete.

A 2023 randomized controlled trial published in *JMIR Aging* tested a tablet-based puzzle game intervention in 12 healthy adults aged 46 to 75. The game used algorithm-based dynamic adaptation — the difficulty adjusted to the player's performance level, keeping the cognitive challenge in the therapeutic range. The results showed that the intervention improved attentional function, with the adaptive nature of the game allowing accommodations for persons with different cognitive abilities. [JMIR Aging 2023](https://aging.jmir.org/2023/1/e46177) A 2025 study in *Scientific Reports* tested a narrative mobile game-based cognitive intervention in 17 patients with mild cognitive impairment (mean age 72.8). After four weeks of gameplay, participants showed significant improvements in frontal function — particularly in processing speed (Digit Symbol Coding, p = 0.007) and verbal fluency (phonemic fluency, p = 0.024). The adherence rate was 122.35% — participants played more than they were asked to. [Choi et al., Scientific Reports 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-84086-9) In any clinical trial, this is the kind of problem one is grateful to have.

The principle is the same across all these studies, across all these conditions. The brain is plastic. It responds to stimulation. And video games — with their combination of adaptive difficulty, immediate feedback, goal-directed behavior, and intrinsic reward — are, for many people, a uniquely effective way to deliver that stimulation. The secret that the gaming industry has known for forty years is that the brain likes to be challenged, provided the challenge does not exceed the reward. Medicine is only now catching up to this insight.

## The Limits of the Medium

Two caveats are necessary. The first is that the benefits of video games are not unconditional. A comprehensive meta-analysis published in *PLOS ONE* in 2023 — synthesizing 63 randomized controlled trials involving 2,079 participants — found that video game training produced a moderate and significant overall effect on cognition (g = 0.25, p < 0.001), with significant transfer to attention and perception (g = 0.27) and higher-order cognition including executive function and working memory (g = 0.31). But the same analysis found no significant transfer to general memory or to psychosocial outcomes. The effect was real, but it was specific — and smaller than the claims made by the brain-training industry would suggest. [Smith & Basak, PLOS ONE 2023](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0285925)

Moreover, the effect depended on *which* game mechanics were used, not on the genre label. Traditional distinctions — "action game," "strategy game," "casual game" — did not predict cognitive outcomes. What mattered were specific gameplay features: allocentric movement, first-person perspective, combat interaction, multiple controllable objects. This means that not all games are therapeutically equivalent. A game is not a game is not a game. Designing a game for cognitive improvement requires understanding which neural circuits you are trying to target, and building the game mechanics to activate them. The genre is a marketing category, not a neurological one.

The second caveat is that the line between therapeutic gaming and problematic gaming is not a line at all — it is a spectrum. The same medium that can enhance attention and executive function can also, when used compulsively, impair them. This is not a contradiction. It is the nature of anything powerful enough to change the brain.

Adolescents with internet gaming disorder show measurable alterations in resting-state brain networks, particularly in the executive control network and the dorsal attention network — the same circuits that therapeutic games aim to strengthen. A 2024 study in *Frontiers in Psychiatry* found that the severity of gaming disorder was positively correlated with impulsivity (r = 0.6350, p < 0.001) and linked to impairments in cognitive control. [Zhao et al., Frontiers in Psychiatry 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2024.1404050/full) A 2025 study in *Communications Psychology* found that problematic online gaming partially mediated the association between ADHD and subsequent mental health issues in adolescents — depression (29.2% mediation), anxiety (12.3%), psychotic experiences (20.6%), and diminished well-being (22.1%). [Narita et al., Communications Psychology 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-025-00296-5)

But the crucial finding — and the one that often gets lost in the moral panic — is that the cognitive impairments are associated with gaming disorder, not with gaming itself. A 2025 study comparing non-gamers, recreational gamers, and individuals at risk of gaming disorder found that the disorder-risk group showed reduced working memory, while recreational gamers displayed *enhanced* attention-related performance. [medRxiv 2025](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.10.08.25334347v1) The dose, the context, and the individual's relationship to the activity appear to matter more than the activity itself. This is true of almost everything that has ever been called a vice.

This is, in some sense, the most important finding in the entire literature: the games are not the problem, and they are not the solution. They are a tool — unusually powerful, unusually engaging, unusually adaptable — but still a tool. Whether the tool heals or harms depends on how it is used. It always does.

## What Prescribable Games Mean

The implications of this research are just beginning to reach clinical practice. In the United States, EndeavorRx is covered by some insurance plans and prescribed by pediatricians. In the UK, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence has begun evaluating digital therapeutics for inclusion in the National Health Service. In Germany, the Digital Health Care Act passed in 2019 makes certain digital health applications — including game-based interventions — prescribable on the public health system. The countries that once worried most about what games were doing to their children are now the ones paying for them to play.

A 2024 systematic review of 59 empirical studies on digital technology for mental health in children and adolescents, published in *Frontiers in Psychology*, found that digital technology interventions have a moderate and significant overall effect (g = 0.43) across conditions. The authors found that the research is accelerating — most of the studies were published after 2020. [Frontiers in Psychology 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1356554/full) And a 2024 systematic review in *BMC Public Health* of 17 randomized controlled trials on active video games for college student mental health found that AVGs can effectively improve stress, anxiety, depression, happiness, psychological satisfaction, and sleep quality. [BMC Public Health 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12889-024-21011-9)

The trajectory is clear. The video game that your teenager is playing right now — if it is the right game, played in the right way, for the right duration — may be doing something their brain needs. The same medium that has been blamed for addiction, violence, and social isolation appears to have a therapeutic potential that psychiatry is only beginning to understand. Every moral panic contains within it the seed of its own reversal: the thing you feared may turn out to be, under the right conditions, the thing that helps.

The question is no longer whether video games can be therapeutic. The question is which games, for which patients, under which conditions. And that is a question that clinical science — with its randomized trials, its meta-analyses, its fMRI scans, and its serum biomarkers — is only now learning to ask. It is the right question, and it is about time someone asked it properly.
