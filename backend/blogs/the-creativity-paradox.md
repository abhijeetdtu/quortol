# The Creativity Paradox: AI Makes Us More Creative, One at a Time

**Lede**

In the summer of 2024, an online experiment unfolded that captured something strange about the human mind and its newest tool. Researchers at University College London and the University of Exeter recruited 293 people to write eight-sentence stories — micro-fiction, the kind that Hemingway supposedly mastered in six words — and gave some of them access to an AI that could generate story ideas. The results, published in *Science Advances*, were striking: writers who used AI ideas produced stories rated 8.1% more novel and 9% more useful by independent evaluators. Their stories were better written, more enjoyable, more likely to contain a plot twist. Reading them, you would say: these are more creative stories.

But there was a catch. The AI-assisted stories also looked more like each other. When the researchers measured the similarity between stories within each condition, they found that AI-enabled stories were 10.7% more similar to one another than human-only stories — a narrowing of collective novelty that the authors describe as a "social dilemma." Individual writers were better off with AI, but if everyone uses it, the scope of novel content contracts ([Doshi & Hauser, Science Advances, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290)).

This is the creativity paradox of the age of generative AI: the tools that make each of us more creative are, in aggregate, making our culture more alike. One might say that artificial intelligence has solved the problem of individual mediocrity only to discover that collective mediocrity was the real problem all along.

**Nut Graf**

A wave of rigorous, peer-reviewed evidence published over the past three years has converged on a consistent finding: generative AI tools substantially increase human creative productivity and the quality of individual creative output. From writing to visual art to software, professionals using AI are producing more, faster, and often better. But the same evidence reveals a second, less celebrated pattern: AI-assisted work converges toward a statistical mean. Outputs become more similar. The diversity of novel ideas shrinks. The boost to individual creativity comes with a cost to collective originality — a trade-off that researchers are only beginning to understand, and that policymakers, platform companies, and creative professionals will have to navigate in the years ahead.

**The Productivity Explosion**

The most straightforward finding in the literature is that AI makes people dramatically more productive at creative tasks. The first randomized controlled trial of generative AI in professional work, conducted by Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang at MIT and published in *Science* in 2023, gave 453 college-educated professionals occupation-specific writing tasks and randomly exposed half of them to ChatGPT. The result: a 40% reduction in task completion time and an 18% improvement in output quality. The AI didn't just make workers faster — it made them better ([Noy & Zhang, Science, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh2586)). Efficiency, it turns out, is the least interesting thing about being more efficient.

![Line chart showing monthly e-book releases on Amazon from 2020 to 2025, nearly tripling from ~100K to ~300K per month after ChatGPT's launch](/api/blog/images/the-creativity-paradox_book_explosion.png)

These gains have been replicated across domains. Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li, and Lindsey Raymond studied the staggered rollout of a generative AI conversational assistant to 5,179 customer support agents at a Fortune 500 company. Access to the tool increased productivity by 14% on average — and by 34% for novice and low-skilled workers. The AI effectively disseminated the best practices of top performers to everyone else, flattening the experience curve ([Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, NBER Working Paper 31161, 2023](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161)).

In creative work specifically, the effects are even more pronounced. Eric Zhou and Dokyun Lee at Boston University analyzed over 4 million artworks from more than 50,000 users on a major art-sharing platform, tracking 5,800 known adopters of text-to-image AI tools like Midjourney and Stable Diffusion. Their findings, published in *PNAS Nexus* in 2024, showed that AI adoption increased creative productivity by 25% and boosted the value of artworks — measured as favorites per view — by 50%. Artists who used AI didn't just produce more; their peers liked what they produced more ([Zhou & Lee, PNAS Nexus, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae052)). Popularity, of course, has never been a reliable signal of originality — but it is a reliable signal of popularity.

The numbers extend beyond experimental settings into the marketplace. Imke Reimers and Joel Waldfogel, economists at Cornell and the University of Minnesota, analyzed Amazon's e-book ecosystem and found that monthly new book releases nearly tripled between 2022 and late 2025, from roughly 100,000 to over 300,000. Using AI detection on 50,000 randomly selected titles, they found that detected AI use in books rose from essentially zero before 2022 to 30% in 2023, 45% in 2024, and over 60% during 2025. By any measure, LLMs triggered an explosion in creative output ([Reimers & Waldfogel, NBER Working Paper 34777, 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34777)). A publisher's dream and a critic's nightmare, all in one elegant statistical finding.

**The Great Equalizer**

One of the most striking patterns across these studies is that AI helps less skilled creators the most — and in some cases, it closes the gap entirely.

In the Doshi and Hauser short story experiment, writers were given a divergent association task (DAT) to measure their baseline creativity. Among the most inherently creative writers, AI made almost no difference — their stories were already good. But among less creative writers, access to five AI ideas produced improvements of 10.7% in novelty and 11.5% in usefulness. Their stories became better written, more enjoyable, and less boring — and the gap between them and the most creative writers essentially vanished. "Having access to generative AI effectively equalizes the evaluations of stories," the authors wrote, "removing any disadvantage or advantage based on the writers' inherent creativity" ([Doshi & Hauser, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290)). The machine is the great leveller: it makes everyone equally capable of being competent, which is both its triumph and its tragedy.

The same pattern appears in the customer service study: an agent with just two months of tenure, using AI, performed as well as an agent with six months of tenure working without it ([Brynjolfsson et al., 2023](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161)). And at the elite consulting firm BCG, where researchers from Harvard Business School ran a randomized experiment with 758 consultants, AI had its largest effect on the bottom half of performers — a 43% improvement in quality compared to 17% for the top half. The bottom performers, with AI, caught up ([Dell'Acqua et al., HBS Working Paper 24-013, 2023/2026](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700)).

The AI literature calls this "compression of the productivity distribution." What it means in human terms is that AI is a democratizing force — it lowers the barrier to producing work that others consider creative or useful. The person who could never write a publishable story, design a recognizable image, or craft a persuasive memo can now, with the right prompt, produce something that passes muster. That is not a small thing. It is also not the only thing.

**The Homogenization Problem**

But the same data that shows individual improvement also reveals collective narrowing.

Zhou and Lee found that while the peak of content novelty among AI-assisted artists increased over time — meaning someone, somewhere, was pushing boundaries — the *average* content novelty declined. The focal subjects and themes of AI-assisted artworks became progressively more alike. Visual novelty declined even more sharply: both average and peak visual novelty fell, suggesting that AI-assisted artists gravitated toward a narrow set of preferred visual styles. "Creative domains may be inundated with generic content as exploration of the creative space diminishes," they warned ([Zhou & Lee, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae052)). One might observe that when every artist has a paintbrush, the danger is not that they cannot paint — it is that they all paint the same thing.

Doshi and Hauser's story experiment found the same dynamic. The AI-assisted stories were not just more similar to each other — they were also 5.2% more similar to the AI-generated ideas that inspired them. Writers were being "anchored" by the AI suggestions, their own divergent thinking subtly constrained by the starting point the machine offered. "These results point to an increase in individual creativity at the risk of losing collective novelty," the authors wrote. "This dynamic resembles a social dilemma: With generative AI, writers are individually better off, but collectively a narrower scope of novel content is produced" ([Doshi & Hauser, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290)). The prisoner's dilemma has found its cultural analogue: we are each better off defecting to the machine, but when everyone does, the cell grows a little smaller.

In the book market, the pattern takes an economic form. Reimers and Waldfogel found that the average quality of new books — measured by reader ratings — declined in the LLM era. Books with detected AI were substantially worse than human-authored books. But the sheer volume of new releases meant that the number of modest-quality books increased significantly, and consumer welfare actually rose by about 5-10%, depending on the scenario. AI floods the market with more options, most of them mediocre, but enough of them decent that the average reader is better off. The economists call this a Pareto improvement. A literary critic might call it something else ([Reimers & Waldfogel, 2026](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34777)). The triumph of adequate abundance.

**What Gets Lost**

The risk that receives less attention in the productivity literature is what happens to the upper tail of creative achievement — the kind of work that rearranges a genre, invents a new visual language, or changes how people think.

Zorana Ivcevic and Mike Grandinetti, writing in the *Journal of Creativity* in 2024, distinguished between two levels at which AI affects creativity. For "small-c" creativity — everyday creative expression like writing an email, designing a presentation, or composing a social media post — AI tools are clearly productive. But for "Big-C" creativity — the paradigm-shifting work that redefines a field — the picture is murkier. They noted that LLMs outperform humans on divergent thinking tasks, producing more original ideas, but that this may come at the cost of the sustained, deep engagement that produces genuinely transformative work ([Ivcevic & Grandinetti, Journal of Creativity, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.yjoc.2024.100079)). The machine is brilliant at brainstorming; it has never spent a decade staring at a single canvas.

The concern is not that AI will replace creative genius — the studies consistently show that top performers are least affected by AI tools, gaining little or nothing from them. The concern is subtler: that the widespread adoption of AI will shift the entire creative ecosystem toward the center, making it harder for genuinely novel work to emerge, be noticed, and find an audience. If every romance novel is competently written by an LLM, how does a truly original voice break through? The answer, perhaps, is that it does not — it simply becomes indistinguishable from the competent noise.

The OECD addressed this question in its June 2025 working paper on generative AI's effects on productivity, innovation, and entrepreneurship. Its findings suggest that AI's effectiveness depends heavily on the user's experience and the nature of the task. Human-AI collaboration is key to maximizing potential — but the long-term effects on innovation remain unclear, particularly regarding whether AI will ultimately expand or contract the frontiers of knowledge ([OECD, 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/the-effects-of-generative-ai-on-productivity-innovation-and-entrepreneurship_b21df222-en.html)).

**The Path Forward**

The researchers who have documented the creativity paradox are not pessimists, overall. Zhou and Lee describe the emerging human-AI creative workflow as "generative synesthesia" — "the harmonious blending of human exploration and AI exploitation to discover new creative workflows." The artists who benefited most from AI tools were those who brought strong ideation skills and a refined artistic filter — who could evaluate and curate the machine's output rather than simply accept it. "Content is king in the text-to-image creative paradigm," they concluded. The human role shifts from execution to curation, from craft to discernment ([Zhou & Lee, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae052)). The artist becomes a critic — and criticism, as any aesthete knows, is itself a kind of creation.

Doshi and Hauser, for their part, noted that their experimental setup likely understates AI's potential. Their writers were given a single, generic prompt to generate ideas, with no opportunity for repeated interaction or fine-tuning. "If that is the case," they wrote, "our estimates are likely a lower bound of the potential that generative AI could offer to writers when they are given full control over the AI engine, or when real-time interactions are enabled" ([Doshi & Hauser, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290)). The machine's best work, in other words, may yet await a better human.

The challenge, then, is not to reject the tools — the productivity gains are too large and too broadly distributed to ignore — but to design the conditions under which they augment rather than homogenize. This means investing in the skills that AI cannot yet replace: the ability to ask original questions, to recognize which ideas are worth pursuing, and to have the taste and judgment to curate rather than merely generate. The machine can give you a thousand answers; only you can decide which question is worth asking.

**Closing**

In the summer of 2024, the same season the story experiment was published, a work created by the AI system Midjourney won an art competition, and an artist forfeited a photography prize after revealing his winning entry was AI-generated. These events provoked outrage and soul-searching in creative communities. The data now available suggests that both the outrage and the celebration miss the point.

AI is not going to replace human creativity. The studies are clear: the most creative people remain the most creative with or without AI. What AI does is raise the floor — it pulls the less skilled up, makes the average better, and floods the marketplace with competent work. The risk is not that the machine becomes a genius, but that a million competent outputs drown out the one that is genuinely new. The mediocre, it turns out, does not need to be bad to be dangerous — it only needs to be plentiful.

The creativity paradox is not a verdict. It is a design problem. How do we build tools that give everyone a voice without making every voice sound the same? The answer, if the research is any guide, will depend less on the capabilities of the machines than on the discernment of the humans who use them. Wilde once remarked that it is only the modern that ever becomes old-fashioned. One hopes the same may be said of the merely competent.
