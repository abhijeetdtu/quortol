# The Thread of Power: A 400-Year Story of Who Controls What You Wear

*In a garment factory on the outskirts of Dhaka, a woman stitches a shirt destined for a mall outside Chicago, where it will sell for $39.99. She earns roughly $3,000 a year. The brand that ordered the shirt will book more than that in revenue before its morning coffee has cooled. This arrangement is not an accident of commerce. It is the latest chapter in a 400-year struggle for control of the oldest, most globalized, and most volatile supply chain on earth: the one that clothes humanity. The players have been replaced. The architecture of power remains standing.*

---

**I. The Indian Century**

For most of recorded history, the world's finest cotton textiles came from the Indian subcontinent. The muslins of Bengal, the calicoes of the Coromandel Coast, the chintzes of Gujarat — these were never mere commodities. They were signals of status, traded across the Indian Ocean, through the Middle East, into the Mediterranean, and finally, inevitably, to London. Indian weavers practiced a level of refinement that European artisans could admire and envy in equal measure, but never match. As the economic historians Stephen Broadberry and Bishnupriya Gupta have documented, India was "the world's main producer of cotton textiles" throughout the early modern period, with a substantial export trade. [Broadberry & Gupta, "Cotton Textiles and the Great Divergence" (2005), CEPR Discussion Paper, via University of Warwick](https://wrap-test.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/1708/1/WRAP_Broadberry_CEPR-DP5183%5B1%5D.pdf)
![A Tantee (Indian weaver) in Dhaka, 1827. Etching from Sir Charles D'Oyly's Antiquities of Dacca, showing a Bengali weaver at his handloom.](/api/blog/images/the-thread-of-power_indian_weaver.jpg)
>*A Tantee (weaver) in Dhaka, 1827. Bengali muslin weavers like this one supplied the finest cotton cloth in the world — until Lancashire's mechanized mills destroyed their market. Source: Sir Charles D'Oyly / British Library (Public Domain). [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:A_Tantee_or_Indian_weavers.jpg)*

When the British East India Company began importing Indian cottons in serious volume during the late 17th century, the effect on British consumers was electrical. Here was fabric that was lightweight, washable, and brilliantly coloured — everything that domestic wool and linen were not, and everything that an empire in a hurry could desire. By 1720, Parliament had passed the Calico Acts, banning most Indian cotton imports to protect the domestic wool and silk interests. Britain wanted the product but not the producer. Here, already, was the pattern that would define the next three centuries: the consuming nation using political power to dictate the terms of the textile trade, and calling it protection.

The Acts did not extinguish the appetite for cotton. They merely redirected it. As the economic historian Robert Allen has argued, they helped create a "high wage economy" in Britain — a system of incentives that made mechanization not merely attractive but inevitable. British wages were roughly six times those of Indian weavers, a ratio confirmed by contemporary accounts cited in Chaudhuri's study of the trade. [Broadberry & Gupta, op. cit.](https://wrap-test.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/1708/1/WRAP_Broadberry_CEPR-DP5183%5B1%5D.pdf) If Britain was to have cotton, it would have to be spun and woven by machines. The human hand had priced itself out of the market.

**II. The Lancashire Revolution**

Between 1764 and 1830, a cascade of mechanical inventions transformed cotton production from a domestic craft into an industrial conquest. James Hargreaves' spinning jenny (1764) multiplied a single spinner's output eightfold. Richard Arkwright's water frame (1769) substituted water power for human muscle. Samuel Crompton's spinning mule (1779) combined the jenny's fineness with the water frame's strength. And Edmund Cartwright's power loom (1785) began the mechanization of weaving — a process that took decades longer to perfect, as weaving, it turned out, was less willing to be tamed.

The numbers invite disbelief. In 1700, Great Britain consumed roughly 2 million pounds of raw cotton. By 1830, it consumed 270 million pounds. [Baines, "History of the Cotton Manufacture in Great Britain" (1835), reprinted Cambridge University Press](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/history-of-the-cotton-manufacture-in-great-britain/0CF3C5233C9F06CE3CA287C9E91F3166) Between 1780 and 1800 alone, output grew at 10.8 percent per year; exports expanded at 14 percent per annum. [Broadberry & Gupta via Deane & Cole (1969) and Farnie (1979)](https://wrap-test.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/1708/1/WRAP_Broadberry_CEPR-DP5183%5B1%5D.pdf) By the 1830s, Britain had done what three centuries of trade had not: it was now exporting cotton textiles to India — the very nation that had taught the world how to make them.

The productivity gains were unprecedented. As C. Knick Harley's archival research into the account books of three Manchester-area firms shows, the price of even the coarsest cotton yarn fell to a third of its early-1780s level by 1815. [Harley, "Cotton Textile Prices and the Industrial Revolution" (1998), Oxford University Working Paper](http://www.nuff.ox.ac.uk/economics/history/Paper81/harley81.pdf) Andrew Ure, an admiring chronicler of the factory system, wrote in 1835 that the new machines had "achieved a mighty revolution not only in the structure of British society, but in the fortunes of the world at large." [Ure, "The Philosophy of Manufactures" (1835)](https://historyofeconomicthought.mcmaster.ca/ure/PhilosophyManufactures.pdf)

Ure was not wrong. He was merely selective — and the side he chose happened to be the one that owned the machines.

**III. The Spinners Who Paid the Price**

The human cost of the textile revolution was not distributed evenly. It fell, as such costs generally do, on women and children. The new evidence assembled by Jane Humphries and Benjamin Schneider overturns the long-held assumption that hand spinners were part of a "high wage economy." By combing through parish account books, spinning school records, farm ledgers, and workers' commonplace books, they reconstructed what female spinners actually earned from the late 16th to the early 19th century.

The picture that emerges is grim. In 1615, married spinners earned 1 shilling 4 pence per week — about 2.7 pence per day. [Humphries & Schneider, "Spinning the Industrial Revolution," *Economic History Review* 72(1), 2018](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ehr.12693) Productivity was low: a wool spinner could produce around 2.1 pounds per week. The mechanization of spinning did not liberate these women. It destroyed their livelihood. A single child operating a spinning jenny could produce what it took one hundred hand wheels to accomplish. Progress, as it was then called, had arrived.

A pamphlet from 1794 captures the desperation with an intimacy that no economic history can match:

> "To tell a poor woman with three, four or five children… to set her children to work, where no Wool is to be had is a mockery of misery… The scanty fare it enables them to eat when the day's work is done, with want of firing makes her at length prefer breaking a hedge for her own fuel."

[Fordham University Internet History Sourcebook, "Observations on the Loss of Woollen Spinning" (c. 1794)](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/1794woolens.asp)

The mills that replaced the cottages were not sanctuaries. They had their own particular horrors. Children as young as five worked 14-hour days in rooms "many stories high" with no woman presiding, the pamphlet's anonymous author noted with alarm. The Factory Act of 1833 — restricting child labour — was the first legislative acknowledgment that the textile industry's hunger for power had a moral limit, however faint a one. By then, of course, the wheel — literal and figurative — had already turned. Textile power had migrated from the weavers of Bengal to the mill owners of Manchester, and it was not inclined to return.

**IV. The Cotton Empire and American Slavery**

The Lancashire mills did not run on British cotton. They ran on American cotton, grown by enslaved hands in the American South — a fact the industry preferred to treat as a logistical detail rather than a moral accusation. Eli Whitney's cotton gin (1793) made short-staple cotton profitable to process, and the plantation system expanded violently across the Deep South. By 1860, the United States was producing two-thirds of the world's cotton, and the British textile industry was utterly dependent on slave-grown fiber. [USDA Economic Research Service, Cotton and Wool Yearbook (historical data)](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/cotton-wool-and-textile-data/cotton-and-wool-yearbook)

This was the second great transfer of power in the textile story: first from Indian artisans to British machines, then from British mills to American fields. The American Civil War and the abolition of slavery disrupted the supply chain — the "Lancashire Cotton Famine" of 1861–1865 threw hundreds of thousands of British mill workers out of work — but the underlying structure held. Cotton production in the American South recovered under sharecropping and tenant farming, a system that had the decency to rename what it continued to practice. The textile industry has never found itself at a loss for a captive labour supply.

**V. The Great Asian Reversal**

The 20th century witnessed the slow unraveling of Western dominance. The Japanese textile industry modernized in the Meiji era and by the 1930s was competitive on the global stage. After World War II, the centre of gravity shifted again: first to Japan, then to Hong Kong, South Korea, and Taiwan, then to China — each move a reminder that the industry's loyalty lies with the cheapest labour, not the most grateful nation.

The mechanism of control was the Multi-Fibre Arrangement (MFA), established in 1974, which imposed quotas on textile exports from developing countries to rich ones. The MFA was, in essence, a coordinated attempt by the United States and Europe to stretch the decline of their domestic textile industries across decades rather than admit it in a season. It did not prevent the decline. It merely orchestrated its migration. When the MFA was finally dismantled in 2005 under the WTO's Agreement on Textiles and Clothing, production consolidated swiftly around the most efficient producer available — which is to say, China. [WTO, "Global Value Chain Sectoral Profiles: Textiles and Clothing" (2024)](https://www.wto.org/english/res_e/statis_e/miwi_e/gvc_sectoral_profiles_textiles_clothing24_e.pdf)

Today, Asia accounts for 70.6 percent of world textile and clothing exports, up from 59.4 percent in 2017. China alone accounts for more than 40 percent of the value-added in global textile exports. Its domestic supply chains cover every stage — from fiber creation to finished garment — giving it an integration that no other country matches. India has similarly high domestic content (83.2 percent). Bangladesh and Vietnam concentrate in labor-intensive assembly, relying on imported raw materials. [WTO TiVA analysis, op. cit.](https://www.wto.org/english/res_e/statis_e/miwi_e/gvc_sectoral_profiles_textiles_clothing24_e.pdf)

The geography of control is now unmistakably Asian. But the raw materials tell an entirely different story.

**VI. The Synthetic Shift**

In 1996, natural fibres still held more than half of the global fibre market. By 2023, the situation had reversed itself with an efficiency that would impress a fashion editor. Synthetic fibres — overwhelmingly polyester, made from fossil fuels — now account for 72.7 percent of global fibre production. Natural fibres, led by cotton, have fallen to about 27 percent. [UNCTAD, "Trends in Natural and Man-made Fibres Trade" (2025)](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/ditccom2025d2_en.pdf)

The numbers are extraordinary. Global fibre production has more than doubled since 2000, from 58 million tonnes to a record 132 million tonnes in 2024. Polyester alone reached 78 million tonnes in 2024 — 59 percent of all fibre produced. [Textile Exchange, "Materials Market Report 2025"](https://textileexchange.org/knowledge-center/reports/materials-market-report-2025/) Production is on track to hit 169 million tonnes by 2030 if current trends hold.

This shift represents a quiet but decisive transfer of power from the cotton farmers of the United States, India, China, and Brazil to the petrochemical companies of China. The control of what we wear has passed from agriculture to industry, and ultimately to the fossil fuel sector — an irony that would be devastating if it were not so profitable. The implications for geopolitics and the environment are, as one might expect, profound.

**VII. The Environmental Reckoning**

The modern textile industry is among the most accomplished polluters on earth. Total greenhouse gas emissions from textile production reached 1.2 billion tonnes of CO₂ equivalent in 2015 — more than the emissions of all international flights and maritime shipping combined. [Ellen MacArthur Foundation, "A New Textiles Economy" (2017)](https://content.ellenmacarthurfoundation.org/m/6d5071bb8a5f05a2/original/A-New-Textiles-Economy-Redesigning-fashions-future.pdf) Production uses roughly 93 billion cubic meters of water annually, and 20 percent of industrial water pollution globally comes from textile dyeing and treatment. [UNEP, "Sustainability and Circularity in the Textile Value Chain" (2020)](https://wedocs.unep.org/handle/20.500.11822/34184)

The waste is, by any reasonable standard, obscene. Every second, the equivalent of one garbage truck of textiles is landfilled or burned. Less than one percent of material used to produce clothing is recycled into new clothing — a loss of more than $100 billion in materials each year, which the industry has learned to describe as the cost of doing business. [Ellen MacArthur Foundation, op. cit.](https://content.ellenmacarthurfoundation.org/m/6d5071bb8a5f05a2/original/A-New-Textiles-Economy-Redesigning-fashions-future.pdf) The Circularity Gap Report found that of all materials the textile industry uses, just 0.3 percent come from recycled sources. Over 99 percent come from virgin sources. [Circularity Gap Report: Textiles (2024)](https://circularity-gap.world/textiles)

![Environmental footprint of the textile industry, showing GHG emissions, water use, and waste across the value chain.](/api/blog/images/the-thread-of-power_environmental_footprint.png)
>*The textile value chain's environmental footprint spans climate, water, and waste. Fiber production and wet processing are the hottest hotspots. Source: Ellen MacArthur Foundation (2017); UNEP (2020); Circularity Gap Report (2024).*

The cheapness of synthetic fibres is the root cause, as cheapness so often is. Polyester is cheaper to produce than cotton, and virgin polyester is cheaper than recycled polyester. The economic incentive runs directly against the environmental imperative, and in the textile industry, the economic incentive has rarely lost a contest. The structure of power — with production concentrated among a small number of giant brands and fibre manufacturers — makes systemic change a matter of will, and the industry has never been particularly willing.

"The same creativity that defines the industry is what will transform it," the Ellen MacArthur Foundation argues. [Ellen MacArthur Foundation, "Circular Fashion" overview](https://www.ellenmacarthurfoundation.org/topics/fashion/overview) One hopes they are right. But creativity operates within constraints, and the primary constraint is the profit margin of a system built on volume. The industry's annual 2–8 percent share of global greenhouse gas emissions is projected to rise. Sector CO₂ emissions could hit 2.7 billion tons per year by 2030. [UNEP, "Sustainability and Circularity in the Textile Value Chain: Global Roadmap" (2023)](https://circulareconomy.europa.eu/platform/sites/default/files/2023-12/Full%20Report%20-%20UNEP%20Sustainability%20and%20Circularity%20in%20the%20Textile%20Value%20Chain%20A%20Global%20Roadmap_0.pdf)

**VIII. The Next Thread**

The future of textiles is dividing into two roads, as futures so often do when the present has become untenable. On one track lies the continuation of the synthetic, linear model — ever-cheaper polyester, ever-faster fashion, ever more waste. On the other lie two alternative visions: the circular economy and smart textiles.

The circular economy vision — championed by the Ellen MacArthur Foundation, UNEP, and a growing number of brands — imagines a system in which clothes are designed to last, to be repaired, remade, and eventually reborn as new garments. It is a vision that sounds almost quaint in an industry built on making things that fall apart just after they cease to be fashionable. Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) policies are being introduced in France, the Netherlands, and other European countries, requiring brands to fund the collection and recycling of the textiles they sell. [Ellen MacArthur Foundation, "Pushing the boundaries of EPR policy for textiles" (2024)](https://www.ellenmacarthurfoundation.org/epr-policy-for-textiles) Six scenarios identified by the Circularity Gap Report could triple the industry's circularity while reducing environmental impacts by up to 50 percent. [Circularity Gap Report, op. cit.](https://circularity-gap.world/textiles)

The smart textiles vision is something else entirely, and considerably more seductive to the sort of person who invests in futures. Valued at roughly $2.4 billion in 2025, the smart fabrics market is projected to reach between $5.6 billion and $21.2 billion by 2030, depending on the estimate — growth rates range from 18 to 31 percent annually. [MarketsandMarkets, "Smart Textiles Market Report 2025–2030"](https://www.marketsandmarkets.com/Market-Reports/smart-textiles-market-13764132.html); [Grand View Research, "Smart Fabrics Market 2030"](https://www.grandviewresearch.com/industry-analysis/smart-textiles-industry) These are fabrics that can sense, react, and adapt: graphene-coated fibres that monitor heart rate, carbon-nanotube threads that harvest energy from movement, AI-enabled textiles that function as human-machine interfaces.

A recent review in the *Journal of Electronic Materials* describes the field as "a fundamental materials design problem rather than a simple integration problem." [Hossain et al., "Nanomaterial-Enabled Smart Textiles," *J. Electron. Mater.* 55, 4899–4925 (2026)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11664-026-12806-0) The technological frontier is real — but so is the question of who will control it. Today's smart textile patents are concentrated in the United States, Japan, and South Korea. The next generation of textile power may belong not to the country with the cheapest labour or the largest mills, but to the one that masters the materials science — and the old pattern of control will simply have found a new disguise.

**Closing**

The woman stitching shirts in Dhaka has never heard of graphene, nor the Multi-Fibre Arrangement. She knows the piece rate for a finished garment, the cost of the bus ride to the factory, the rent for the room she shares with three other women. Her great-grandmother might have spun cotton by hand in a village in Bengal, before the British mills destroyed that market. Her granddaughter may work in a factory automated by robots and monitored by smart fabrics. The thread that connects them across centuries is not made of cotton or polyester or graphene. It is made of power.

Whoever controls the thread controls more than cloth. They control the terms on which billions of people are clothed, the conditions under which millions work, the environmental burden the planet bears, and the direction of technological change. For four hundred years, that power has moved — from weavers to mill owners to plantation owners to petrochemical companies — always promising liberation, always delivering a new master. Where it goes next — toward circularity or deeper into the linear economy, toward smart materials that serve human needs or toward still more concentrated control — is not a question of technology. It is a question of power. And as the history of textiles makes abundantly clear, power is never surrendered. It is only taken. Or lost.
