# The House That New England Built: Why the Region with the Most Historic Housing Stock Is the Least Affordable in America

## At the Place Where Home Became a Luxury

According to the Federal Highway Administration's 2021 National Household Travel Survey, the average one-way commute in the United States is 41 miles for long-distance workers and 14 miles for local commuters. In greater Boston, both numbers are rising — not because employment has drifted further from housing, but because housing has drifted further from affordability. The [U.S. Census Bureau's 2020–2024 American Community Survey](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/MA/HSG445224) puts Massachusetts' median home value at $562,100. To afford a median-priced home with a 10 percent down payment at prevailing 2025 mortgage rates, a household in the state would need an annual income of roughly $175,000. The median household income: about $99,000. The arithmetic does not work, and it has stopped pretending that it might.

This arithmetic is not unique to Massachusetts. Across New England — Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, and Connecticut — the gap between what people earn and what a home costs has become the region's defining economic fact. The [Federal Housing Finance Agency's House Price Index](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CNEWSTHPI) for the New England Census Division stands at 1,056.68 as of Q1 2026 (1980:Q1 = 100), having risen 47.7 percent over five years. That growth rate [ranks fourth among all U.S. census divisions](https://www.fhfa.gov/data/hpi/datasets), trailing only the East North Central, Middle Atlantic, and West North Central divisions.

But that ranking undersells the story. A February 2025 [policy report from the Federal Reserve Bank of Boston](https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/2025/neppcpr-2501.pdf) by economist Sam Shampine found that single-family home prices in New England rose roughly **50 percent** from the first quarter of 2020 to the first quarter of 2024 alone. Across the Northeast more broadly, the median price of existing homes rose 44.7 percent over the same period. Nationally, [CoreLogic reported in January 2025](https://www.cotality.com/press-releases/corelogic-northeastern-new-england-states-continue-to-lead-us-for-annual-home-price-growth-in-november) that New England states claimed the top five spots for year-over-year home price appreciation in November 2024.

New Hampshire led the nation: its median home price jumped [67.3 percent](https://constructioncoverage.com/research/cities-with-highest-home-price-to-income-ratios) in five years. Maine followed at 66.4 percent. Connecticut: 58.8. Rhode Island: 57.8. Even Vermont, the region's most rural state, recorded a 44.4 percent increase. Massachusetts, the most expensive state in the region at a median of $562,100, posted a 47.5 percent rise.

What makes these numbers particularly striking is that New England is not suffering from a housing-supply collapse. According to the [Census Bureau's Building Permits Survey](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CNEWBPPRIV), the New England Census Division authorized 2,833 new private housing units in April 2026 alone. Shampine's Boston Fed report notes that the number of newly issued building permits in New England **relative to population changes has reached its highest level in four decades**.

And yet demand has overwhelmed every effort to meet it — a reminder that building more is not the same as building enough.

## The Supply Paradox

The Boston Fed report traces the housing imbalance across two key metrics: building permits per capita and rental vacancy rates. The permit data reveals a cyclical pattern — a boom in the 1980s, a collapse in the early 1990s, a recovery through the 2000s, and a sharp post-2008 decline before the recent uptick. The vacancy data, however, tells a more ominous story, and one that begins well before the pandemic.

Rental vacancy rates across New England's metropolitan areas have been declining since 2005, long before the pandemic-era price surge. "Therefore, the recent tightness in the rental market, expressed as vacancy rates, represents an intensification of a preexisting trend," Shampine writes. A crisis, in other words, that was already in progress before anyone thought to name it.

The [Census Bureau's Housing Vacancy Survey](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/RRVRNEQ156N) confirms this. The rental vacancy rate for the Northeast Census Region stood at 5.9 percent in Q1 2026, up slightly from the 5.1 percent recorded a year earlier but still historically low. Compare that to the 9 to 10 percent range that prevailed in the late 2000s and early 2010s — a period that, in retrospect, looks less like a normal market and more like a generous one we failed to appreciate.

The supply-demand imbalance is visible in every state. The [U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development's Quarterly Market Report for the third quarter of 2024](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/periodicals/USHMC/reg/NewEngland-rr-3Q2024.pdf) classified rental market conditions across New England as "Tight" in Rhode Island and Vermont, "Slightly Tight" in Maine and New Hampshire, and "Balanced" only in Connecticut and Massachusetts — though even those "balanced" labels paper over local extremes.

Providence, Rhode Island, recorded the region's tightest rental market at a 3.0 percent vacancy rate, with rents rising 5 percent year over year. Burlington, Vermont, was nearly as tight at 3.3 percent. The Boston-Cambridge-Newton metro area, despite its size, posted a 5.0 percent vacancy rate and an average monthly rent of $2,891 — the **fifth highest among all metropolitan areas in the United States**, according to HUD. A city can call itself a rental market, but at these levels it begins to resemble an auction.

## The Rent That Eats Your Paycheck

Boston's $2,891 average rent is far from the region's only punishing statistic. Across the HUD report's eight tracked metropolitan areas, average monthly rents ranged from $1,706 in Maine to $2,891 in Boston. Every metro area in New England except Maine and Hartford posted rents above the national average of $1,734.

The rent growth is relentless. HUD recorded year-over-year rent increases of 3 percent in Maine, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, and Vermont; 4 percent in Connecticut and Rhode Island; and 5 percent in Hartford and Providence. Nationwide, the average increase was 1 percent. The region is inflating at three to five times the national rate, and it shows no sign of embarrassment.

The [MassINC Gateway Cities Housing Monitor, published in September 2025](https://massinc.org/research/2025-gateway-cities-housing-monitor-chapter-2-housing-affordability/), paints an even starker picture for Massachusetts' mid-sized industrial cities — places like Worcester, Springfield, Fall River, and New Bedford. The typical renter household in a Gateway City earns about $54,000 per year. The minimum annual income needed to afford average asking rents in mid-2025 was **$94,000** — a $40,000 shortfall, up from $38,000 in 2024. The gap is not merely widening; it is becoming a permanent feature of economic life.

The gap varies wildly by location. In Leominster, the shortfall is $20,000; in Barnstable, it reaches $82,000, where households would need $129,000 to cover asking rents without financial strain. One can live in Barnstable on a modest income, provided one does not also require a place to sleep.

Homeownership is even further out of reach. After a decade of 5 percent average annual gains, inflation-adjusted home values in Gateway Cities rose less than 1 percent from 2024 to 2025. But they remain at historic highs: the average home in a Gateway City was valued at $515,000 in June 2025 — **23 percent higher** than the peak of the 2005 housing bubble, after adjusting for inflation. On average, just **20 percent of Gateway City households** can afford to purchase the typical home in their community. The other 80 percent are left to rent, commute, or leave.

## The National Context: Five Times Income

The New England crisis is a regional amplification of a national one. The [Joint Center for Housing Studies at Harvard University reported in October 2025](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/blog/home-prices-surge-five-times-median-income-nearing-historic-highs) that the national median single-family home price grew to **five times the median household income** in 2024 — nearly matching the record highs set during the pandemic peak. In 2019, the multiple was 4.1. Throughout the 1990s, it was 3.2. A generation ago, a home cost a little more than three years of labor; now it costs five, and the trajectory suggests six is not far off.

Nationally, median single-family home prices rose 48 percent between 2019 and 2024, while median income rose only 22 percent. Home prices grew at more than double the rate of wages. That is not a market; it is a divergence dressed as one.

In New England, that ratio is worse. Using the Census Bureau's 2020–2024 American Community Survey data, the median home value across the six states is approximately $432,000 (weighted average of state medians), while the median household income is roughly $87,000. That yields a price-to-income ratio of approximately 5.0, matching the national figure. But in Massachusetts, the ratio is closer to 5.7 — and in Greater Boston, it exceeds 7.0. For a household earning the median income in Boston, a home costs more than seven years of everything they make. The city is not merely expensive; it has become a proposition that mathematics declines to endorse.

[Census data](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/table/PST045216/50,44,33,23,25,09) on homeownership rates tells a story of exclusion: Massachusetts, at 62.5 percent, and Rhode Island, at 63.6 percent, both fall below the national rate of about 65.2 percent. Vermont (73.2 percent), New Hampshire (72.8 percent), and Maine (74.3 percent) sit comfortably above it — but those states are also the ones seeing the fastest price growth, as remote workers and retirees from the southern New England states flee northward. The escape hatch, too, is filling up.

## Migration and the Shifting Landscape

The Boston Fed analysis identifies migration as the single strongest demand-side correlate of price increases. "Prices have risen fastest in places where migration increases have been the largest," Shampine writes. The pandemic triggered a wave of relocations from expensive coastal metros to interior and northern New England — a trend that pushed prices upward in places like Burlington, Portland, Manchester, and even rural Vermont and New Hampshire.

This migration-driven demand interacts with housing supply in a way that favors price growth over new construction. Even though building permits have reached a 40-year high relative to population, the new units are concentrated in multi-family developments in already-dense urban areas. Single-family zoning, local opposition to new development, and the high cost of construction labor in the region all constrain the supply of the kind of housing that migrants and first-time buyers seek. The machinery of permission grinds slowly, and the market does not wait.

The [FHFA's division-level data](https://www.fhfa.gov/data/hpi/datasets) shows the New England Division with a 47.70 percent increase over five years as of 2025Q4 — nearly 9 percentage points higher than the national average of 38.87 percent. Only the Mountain Division (496.57 since 1991) has a higher cumulative index since 1991, reflecting the long-term structural demand for housing in the West and New England alike.

## The Policy Response: Too Small, Too Late?

State governments across New England have begun to respond. Massachusetts passed the Affordable Homes Act in 2024, authorizing $4 billion in spending on housing production, preservation, and rehabilitation. The act includes provisions for accessory dwelling unit (ADU) reform and the creation of a HousingWorks infrastructure program. Connecticut has implemented its own "Work, Live, Ride" initiative, tying transit-oriented development to housing production goals.

But the scale of the response is dwarfed by the scale of the problem — a gap so large that even well-intentioned policy begins to look like a gesture. The Boston Fed report notes that the "supply and demand factors described earlier are important for understanding housing affordability in New England" but adds that "the full range of causal influences that determine housing prices (for example, location-specific zoning practices, mortgage interest rates, etc.) is well beyond the scope of this report." In other words, even the region's central bank researchers cannot fully untangle the knot of causes — zoning, interest rates, construction costs, migration patterns, local politics, and the enduring appeal of New England's small cities and towns. When the Federal Reserve admits the problem exceeds its analytical grasp, the situation has passed beyond complexity and into the realm of intractability.

## The Cost of Staying

What gets lost in the indices and vacancy rates is the human dimension. The MassINC report found that for every percentage point the rent gap widens, more households double up, more young workers leave the state, and more of the region's essential workforce — teachers, nurses, firefighters, restaurant workers — accepts commutes that erode their quality of life. The cost of staying, in other words, is not only financial. It is measured in hours lost, relationships strained, and the slow realization that the place you serve for a living no longer has room for you.

A 2025 analysis by [Construction Coverage](https://constructioncoverage.com/research/cities-with-highest-home-price-to-income-ratios), using Zillow Home Value Index data and Census income figures, ranked U.S. cities by price-to-income ratio. While no New England city cracked the top 10 for largest cities, the region's smaller cities — Portland, Maine; Manchester, New Hampshire; Burlington, Vermont — all posted ratios well above the national average, driven by the influx of higher-income remote workers from Boston and New York. The wealthy have discovered the charms of small New England cities, and the people who used to live there are discovering the economics of displacement.

In the Boston metro area, [Census Bureau QuickFacts](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/portlandcitymaine,bostoncitymassachusetts,providencecityrhodeisland,newportcityrhodeisland,newyorkcitynewyork,US/HSG495222) data shows that only 35.7 percent of housing units are owner-occupied — among the lowest rates for any major U.S. city. The median home value is $731,700. The median selected monthly owner cost with a mortgage: $2,986. Rents in Boston are not far behind. The city has become a place where homeownership is effectively limited to dual-income professional households, inherited wealth, or both. The middle class, in Boston, is not disappearing; it is being redefined as anyone who can still afford the rent.

## Closing: The House That New England Built

New England has always been a region of houses — saltbox colonials, cape cods, Victorian triple-deckers, mill-town tenements, and farmhouses tucked into the White Mountains and the Green Mountains. The region's identity is bound up in its domestic architecture, its town commons, its village centers, its coastal enclaves.

But a house becomes something other than a home when it costs five times what its inhabitants earn. And a region cannot call itself livable when the people who staff its hospitals, teach its children, and fight its fires cannot afford to live within its borders. The FHFA index will keep ticking upward. The building permits will keep coming. But the question that matters — who gets to live here, and at what cost — remains unanswered. New England built itself on the idea that a house was a foundation, not a luxury. That idea is now up for revision.
