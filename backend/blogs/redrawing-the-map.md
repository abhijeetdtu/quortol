# The Map Is Being Redrawn: How Geopolitics, Climate, and a Quiet Revolution in Trade Are Reshaping Global Shipping

## 1. The Ship That Took the Long Way

Nothing ages faster than the ordinary. On a June morning in 2014, the container ship *LICA MAERSK* steamed east across the Pacific, rounded the southern tip of South America, and disappeared into the gray Atlantic on a voyage from New Zealand to Europe — 265 meters of Danish steel carrying 63,400 tons of cargo, photographed from a beach near Christchurch as it passed. Eleven years later, that photograph has become a kind of historical document — not because the ship is unusual, but because the route it traveled would now be considered almost quaint.

The shortest route is no longer the safest. In 2023, a container vessel departing Shanghai for Rotterdam would have passed through the Strait of Malacca, crossed the Indian Ocean, slipped through the Bab-el-Mandeb, and transited the Suez Canal in approximately 20 days. In 2026, that same vessel — if its operator is prudent — rounds the Cape of Good Hope instead, adding roughly 30 percent to the voyage length and a week or more to the journey [UNCTAD Ch.1](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025ch1_en.pdf). The fuel bill is higher. The emissions are greater. The cargo arrives late. This is not a temporary disruption — it has become, for the foreseeable future, the structure of global trade.

The Suez Canal, as of mid-2025, was operating at roughly 70 percent below its average tonnage transit levels of 2023 [UNCTAD RMT 2025 Overview](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025overview_en.pdf). The Panama Canal, choked by drought, limited daily transits through much of 2024 [World Bank CPPI 2025](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2025/09/22/port-performance-varies-across-the-globe-amid-continuing-shocks). And the Strait of Hormuz — a passage for roughly 34 percent of global seaborne oil exports — saw risks escalate through 2025, with UNCTAD warning that "the potential for increased shipping costs, delays and insurance premiums cannot be excluded" [UNCTAD press release](https://unctad.org/news/maritime-trade-under-pressure-growth-set-stall-2025).

The map of global shipping is being redrawn in real time — not by cartographers, but by geopolitics, climate change, and an accelerating rebalancing of economic power that is shifting, almost without notice, the center of gravity of world trade.

## 2. The Distance Problem

The central paradox of modern maritime trade is this: in 2024, the volume of goods carried by sea grew by a respectable 2.2 percent, reaching 12,720 million tons — a figure above the 2013–2023 average [UNCTAD RMT 2025](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025_en.pdf). But the distance those goods traveled grew far more. Measured in ton-miles — the standard metric that multiplies weight by distance — global seaborne trade surged by 5.9 percent in 2024, nearly three times the volume growth [UNCTAD RMT 2025 Overview](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025overview_en.pdf).

The average voyage haul for a ship has increased from 4,831 miles in 2018 to 5,245 miles in 2024 [UNCTAD RMT 2025 Overview](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025overview_en.pdf). That extra 414 miles per voyage — detours around the Cape of Good Hope, longer Pacific crossings to avoid the Panama Canal, re-routed tankers skirting the Arabian Sea — is the physical signature of a world pulling apart. As UNCTAD puts it in its 2025 Review of Maritime Transport: "Distance is no longer geography; it is geoeconomics" [UNCTAD RMT 2025 Overview](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025overview_en.pdf).

The 5.9 percent ton-mile surge was driven overwhelmingly by the Red Sea crisis. Houthi attacks on commercial shipping forced carriers to abandon the Suez route, sending vessels around southern Africa instead. Containerized trade from East Asia to Europe — the arterial route of global supply chains — saw vessel detours add approximately 30 percent to voyage lengths, contributing to an estimated 11 percent increase in overall container TEU-mile demand [UNCTAD Ch.1](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025ch1_en.pdf).

## 3. Fragile Growth, Uncertain Forecasts

The global economy has not made it easy for the ships that serve it. The 2.2 percent volume growth of 2024, while decent, lagged the 20-year average of 2.9 percent and fell well short of the 2003–2023 average of 2.9 percent [UNCTAD RMT 2025](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025_en.pdf). Structural factors — a weakening of the link between trade and GDP, a slowdown in global value-chain expansion, rising trade barriers, and geopolitical fragmentation — have been compressing maritime trade growth for years [UNCTAD RMT 2025](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025_en.pdf).

The outlook for 2025 is worse. In February 2025, analysts projected 1.4 percent growth in seaborne trade volumes. By May, that forecast had been revised to a contraction of 0.1 percent [UNCTAD Ch.1](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025ch1_en.pdf). UNCTAD's official projection now stands at 0.5 percent growth for 2025, with containerized trade growing a slightly more robust 1.4 percent. From 2026 to 2030, UNCTAD projects total seaborne trade growth of about 2 percent annually, with containerized trade at 2.3 percent [UNCTAD RMT 2025 Overview](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025overview_en.pdf).

Those forecasts carry an unusual degree of uncertainty. "In early 2025 (specifically, in February and March), global seaborne trade volumes were projected to grow by 1.4 per cent," the UNCTAD report notes. "By May 2025, they were projected to contract marginally by 0.1 per cent. This notable reduction points to the increasingly complex and uncertain global trade environment" [UNCTAD Ch.1](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025ch1_en.pdf).

Not all trade lanes are equal. The main East–West corridors — East Asia to North America, East Asia to Europe, and Europe to North America — remain the backbone of global container flows, accounting for 36.8 percent of all containerized trade in 2024. These routes recorded their strongest expansion in years: East Asia–North America up 14.7 percent, East Asia–Europe up 10.2 percent, transatlantic up 5.2 percent [UNCTAD Ch.1](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025ch1_en.pdf). But the fastest-growing segment is South–South trade — between developing nations — which expanded 8 percent in 2024 [UNCTAD Ch.1](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025ch1_en.pdf).

## 4. The Price of Passage

Maritime freight rates have become one of the most volatile economic indicators in the world. The Baltic Dry Index, which tracks the cost of shipping dry bulk commodities like iron ore, coal, and grain, stood at approximately 3,124 points on June 3, 2026 — up 110 percent year-on-year [Trading Economics](https://tradingeconomics.com/commodity/baltic). The capesize segment, which moves 150,000-ton loads of iron ore, saw its time-charter average hit $46,000 per day — a 170 percent increase from a year earlier [Hellenic Shipping News](https://www.hellenicshippingnews.com/weekly-dry-market-monitor-spotlight-on-capesize-freight-and-growth-of-tonne-miles-to-india/). The Containerized Freight Index reached 2,572 points, up 24 percent from a year earlier and surging 35 percent in the past month alone as peak-season demand collided with constrained capacity [Trading Economics](https://tradingeconomics.com/commodity/containerized-freight-index).

On the transpacific routes, container spot rates hit approximately $3,200 per forty-foot equivalent unit (FEU) to the West Coast and $5,000 to the East Coast. Asia–Europe rates stood at $3,000 per FEU to Northern Europe and $4,400 to the Mediterranean [Freightos](https://container-news.com/freightos-weekly-update-container-rates-starting-to-spike-on-peak-season-rush/). These are not pandemic-era peaks, but they are high enough to concern shippers and economists alike — especially because they are driven not by demand but by disruption and capacity management.

The supply side tells a contradictory story. Since 2021, carriers have poured the record profits of the pandemic years into an unprecedented wave of new vessel orders. An estimated 10 million TEU of container ship capacity — equivalent to roughly one-third of the current active fleet — is now on order and will be delivered over the next few years [Freightos 2026 Forecast](https://www.freightos.com/freight-industry-updates/market-updates/ocean-and-air-freight-forecast-2026-what-to-expect/). The conventional wisdom holds that this will trigger a container market downcycle, with persistent downward pressure on rates, reduced carrier revenues, and even losses. But in the short term, the Red Sea disruption has absorbed so much excess capacity — through longer voyages, extra vessels needed to maintain schedules — that rates have stayed elevated.

"Expect a cyclical pattern of sharp rate dips followed by periods of recovery through capacity management in the near term," the Freightos 2026 Forecast advises. "Though overall rate levels will likely trend lower than 2025 through the downcycle" [Freightos 2026 Forecast](https://www.freightos.com/freight-industry-updates/market-updates/ocean-and-air-freight-forecast-2026-what-to-expect/).

## 5. The Fleet and What It Burns

As of January 1, 2025, the world's merchant fleet consisted of 112,500 commercial vessels, carrying a total capacity of 2.44 billion deadweight tons [UNCTAD Ch.2](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025ch2_en.pdf). The fleet grew 3.4 percent in the past year — faster than maritime trade growth, slower than the 20-year average of 5.1 percent [UNCTAD RMT 2025 Overview](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025overview_en.pdf). Greece remains the largest ship-owning nation, controlling 16.4 percent of global fleet capacity, followed by China and Japan; together, the top three own 40.7 percent of the world's ships by deadweight tonnage. The top three flag states — Liberia, Panama, and the Marshall Islands — account for 45.1 percent of carrying capacity, which is to say that a ship's nationality is usually a matter of regulatory convenience [UNCTAD RMT 2025 Overview](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025overview_en.pdf).

But the most significant number in the fleet profile is this: 8 percent of active fleet tonnage is designed to run on alternative fuels. On the orderbook, the share is 53 percent [UNCTAD RMT 2025 Overview](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025overview_en.pdf). "Progress in 'greening' the fleet is underway but incremental," UNCTAD reports. The gap between the active fleet and the vessels being built defines the central challenge of maritime decarbonization: the industry must rebuild itself almost entirely in the next two decades, while continuing to move 80 percent of the world's trade by volume.

## 6. The Emissions That Follow

In 2023, global shipping emitted 911 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent greenhouse gases (tank-to-wake, using 100-year global warming potentials). If counted as a country, shipping would rank as the ninth-largest CO₂e-emitting nation on earth, with a share of approximately 1.7 percent of anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions [ICCT 2025](https://theicct.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/ID-332-%E2%80%93-Global-shipping_report_final.pdf). By CO₂ alone, shipping accounted for an estimated 2.3 percent of global emissions in 2023, up from 2.2 percent in 2016 [ICCT 2025](https://theicct.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/ID-332-%E2%80%93-Global-shipping_report_final.pdf).

The numbers for 2024 are higher. The OECD's experimental maritime transport emissions database puts global CO₂ emissions from shipping at 972.8 million tonnes in 2024 — an increase of 9.3 percent from 2019 [OECD 2025](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/12/analysing-recent-trends-and-drivers-of-maritime-transport-co-emission_4463d5fe/6bb2bd69-en.pdf). The European Commission's Monitoring, Reporting, and Verification (MRV) system, which tracks ships calling at European Economic Area ports, recorded 144.9 million tonnes of CO₂ in 2024 — the highest since the system began, and 12.9 percent higher than 2023 [EU Commission MRV 2025](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-8722-2026-ADD-1/en/pdf).

The longer routes forced by geopolitical disruption are directly inflating emissions. The carbon intensity of shipping — emissions per unit of transport work — has improved by about 10.3 percent since 2016 [ICCT 2025](https://theicct.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/ID-332-%E2%80%93-Global-shipping_report_final.pdf). But total emissions keep rising because vessels are steaming farther. "The substantial growth in ton-miles was primarily due to extended voyage distances caused by logistical disruptions, particularly the Red Sea rerouting," UNCTAD explains [UNCTAD RMT 2025](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025_en.pdf). Ships are becoming more efficient per mile — but there are more miles.

The regulatory response has been slow and contested. In April 2025, the International Maritime Organization approved the IMO Net-Zero Framework, the world's first sector-wide combination of mandatory emissions limits and GHG pricing [IMO](https://www.imo.org/en/mediacentre/pressbriefings/pages/imo-approves-netzero-regulations.aspx). The framework would have been formally adopted in October 2025, with entry into force expected in 2027. But the extraordinary MEPC session in October 2025 voted to adjourn for one year, postponing adoption until at least 2026 [IMO MEPC 84](https://www.classnk.com/hp/pdf/info_service/imo_and_iacs/MEPC84_sumE.pdf). Meanwhile, the European Union's Emissions Trading System — the first cap-and-trade system to include shipping — came into effect for CO₂ in 2024 and will extend to methane and nitrous oxide in 2026 [EU Commission](https://data.consilium.europa.eu/doc/document/ST-8722-2026-INIT/en/pdf). The gap between ambition and action, between regions and globally, has rarely been wider.

## 7. The Developing World's Maritime Moment

One of the most consequential shifts in global trade is happening below the headlines. In 2000, developing countries accounted for 38 percent of global maritime freight. By 2023, their share had risen to 54 percent [UNCTAD press](https://unctad.org/news/shipping-data-unctad-releases-new-seaborne-trade-statistics). The surge was led by Asia, with China driving much of the growth. South–South trade — between developing nations — expanded 8 percent in 2024, faster than any other non-mainlane segment [UNCTAD Ch.1](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025ch1_en.pdf).

The composition of trade is shifting too. Crude oil's share of seaborne trade fell from 29 percent in 2000 to 18 percent in 2023, while dry bulk commodities like iron ore, coal, and grain rose from 27 to 36 percent [UNCTAD press](https://unctad.org/news/shipping-data-unctad-releases-new-seaborne-trade-statistics). New trade flows are emerging: iron ore shipments from major loading hubs to Indian ports, for instance, grew more than four-fold between Q1 2024 and Q1 2026 [Hellenic Shipping News](https://www.hellenicshippingnews.com/weekly-dry-market-monitor-spotlight-on-capesize-freight-and-growth-of-tonne-miles-to-india/). This is not merely a shift in volume — it is a reconfiguration of the global map of supply and demand.

The performance of ports, however, has not kept pace. The World Bank's Container Port Performance Index, which benchmarked 403 ports using 175,000 vessel calls and 247 million container moves, found that global port efficiency declined between 2020 and 2024. East Asian and South Asian ports showed improvement. But developing-country ports, particularly in Africa and Latin America, continued to struggle with yard management, berth availability, and hinterland connectivity [World Bank CPPI 2025](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2025/09/22/port-performance-varies-across-the-globe-amid-continuing-shocks). The ports that handle the fastest-growing share of global trade are often the least equipped to do so.

## 8. The Map Ahead

The convergence of forces bearing down on global shipping is historically unusual — not because any single factor is unprecedented, but because so many are arriving at once. Geopolitical fragmentation is lengthening routes and raising costs. Climate change is choking canals and forcing a reckoning with the industry's own emissions. A trade rebalancing is shifting the center of gravity of global commerce toward the developing world. And a regulatory revolution — the IMO Net-Zero Framework, the EU ETS — promises to transform how ships are built, fueled, and operated, even as its adoption stalls.

UNCTAD's latest Review of Maritime Transport captures the stakes in a single sentence: "Maritime transport has weathered storms before. But never have so many transitions converged so quickly. The sector will adapt. The question is whether adaptation will be managed or chaotic, inclusive or divisive, sustainable or merely survivable" [UNCTAD RMT 2025 Overview](https://unctad.org/system/files/official-document/rmt2025overview_en.pdf).

For the rest of us — the people who do not own ships or operate ports, who simply buy things that arrived on one — the stakes are less abstract. Every container of electronics, every shipment of grain, every barrel of oil that crosses an ocean carries with it a freight cost, a carbon cost, and a geopolitical story. The map is being redrawn. The question is whether we will read it in time.
