# The Unknown Continent: What Lies Beneath the World's Oceans

*The ocean covers 70 percent of the planet's surface and contains 90 percent of its living space. Its average depth is 12,080 feet. We have visually explored less than 0.001 percent of it. The deep ocean is not merely a feature of our planet. It is our planet, and we have treated it like a stranger at our own table.*

---

It begins with a descent into darkness.

At 200 meters, sunlight surrenders. The blue fades to indigo, then to a black so absolute that the human eye cannot distinguish up from down. At 1,000 meters — the bathypelagic zone — the pressure reaches 100 atmospheres. At 4,000 meters, the abyssal plain stretches across 70 percent of the ocean floor: a landscape of sediment and silence, interrupted only by seamounts the size of the Alps and trenches deeper than Everest is tall. At 6,000 meters, the hadal zone begins — a region of crushing pressure, perpetual cold, and life that has evolved to treat eight tons per square inch as routine.

The descent takes about two and a half hours in a submersible. It is a journey from the familiar to the alien, from the mapped to the imagined, from the world we know to the one we have only begun to suspect exists.

And the unsettling truth is this: we have better maps of the surface of Mars than we do of the ocean floor. Humanity has always found the distant more compelling than the immediate. Mars is a far more romantic subject than mud.

---

## I. The Atlas of Ignorance

As of April 2026, exactly 28.7 percent of the global seafloor has been mapped to modern high-resolution standards — an area of approximately 104 million square kilometers, roughly two-thirds of Earth's land surface [Seabed 2030, April 2026](https://seabed2030.org/2026/04/20/global-seabed-mapping-reaches-new-milestone-as-five-million-square-kilometres-added-in-a-year/). This represents a considerable acceleration: in 2017, when the Nippon Foundation-GEBCO Seabed 2030 Project launched, only about 6 percent of the seafloor had been mapped at comparable resolution. To put it another way: in less than a decade, we have nearly quintupled our knowledge of the shape of our own planet's floor — and it is still less than a third.

The remaining 71.3 percent — roughly 260 million square kilometers — exists on maps derived from satellite altimetry, a technique that infers seafloor topography from subtle variations in the height of the ocean surface. These maps reveal the broad outlines of the seabed: the mid-ocean ridges, the major fracture zones, the largest trenches. But they miss almost everything smaller than a few kilometers. Seamounts a thousand meters tall go undetected. Submarine canyons vanish into the noise. Shipwrecks, hydrothermal vent fields, and the fine-scale habitat structure that determines where life can survive — none of it appears [NOAA Ocean Exploration](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/explored/). It is the cartographic equivalent of knowing a person's silhouette and nothing else.

The Seabed 2030 Project — a collaboration between The Nippon Foundation and GEBCO, itself a joint program of the IHO and the UNESCO Intergovernmental Oceanographic Commission — intends to close this gap by the end of the decade. In the 2025–2026 reporting year alone, nearly five million square kilometers of new bathymetric data were added to the GEBCO grid — an area roughly the size of the European Union. Two hundred twenty organizations from 37 countries contributed data, including first-time submissions from Malaysia, Morocco, Papua New Guinea, and Saudi Arabia [IOC-UNESCO, April 2026](https://www.ioc.unesco.org/en/articles/seabed-mapping-global-public-good).

The pace has been remarkable, as these things go. Global mapping coverage grew from 6 percent in 2017 to 26.1 percent in 2024, 27.3 percent in 2025, and 28.7 percent in 2026 — an average of roughly 3.2 percent of total seafloor area added per year [Frontiers in Marine Science, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmars.2025.1543885/full). At this rate, the entire seafloor could be mapped by approximately 2047 — well beyond the Seabed 2030 target, but still a revolution in human knowledge, assuming one does not dwell on the irony that "revolution" here means finally discovering the shape of one's own home.

Yet even a complete map is not exploration. A map tells you where things are, not what they are.

![Global seafloor mapping progress, 2017–2026: from 6% to 28.7%](/api/blog/images/ocean_mapping_progress.png)

"Explorers have seen less than 0.001 percent of the deep ocean seafloor," NOAA notes — roughly the area of Rhode Island [NOAA Ocean Exploration](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/explored/). The vast majority of what lies beneath the ocean surface has never been observed by any human eye, instrument, or remotely operated vehicle. We inhabit a planet whose primary geographical feature — the deep seabed — is almost entirely unvisited. A stranger living in your house would know more about your attic.

## II. The Architecture of the Abyss

The deep ocean floor is not a featureless plain. It is a landscape of extreme topography, shaped by tectonic forces that operate on scales both vast and minute.

The mid-ocean ridge system is the most extensive chain of mountains on Earth, stretching nearly 65,000 kilometers — long enough to wrap around the planet one and a half times. More than 90 percent of it lies underwater, rising from the abyssal plain to an average depth of 2,500 meters below the surface. Along the divergent plate boundaries that form the ridge system, new oceanic crust is created continuously as magma rises from the mantle. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads at 2 to 5 centimeters per year — slow enough to produce a steep, rugged rift valley the depth and width of the Grand Canyon. The East Pacific Rise spreads at 6 to 16 centimeters per year, producing a smooth volcanic summit without a rift valley at all [NOAA Ocean Exploration](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/mid-ocean-ridge).

![The vertical structure of the ocean: depth zones, features, and the deepest points of each ocean basin](/api/blog/images/ocean_depth_zones.png)

Rising from the seafloor are seamounts — underwater volcanoes that never reached the surface. Data from satellite altimetry and ship-based sonar suggests the number of seamounts at least 1,000 meters tall exceeds 100,000. Despite their abundance, fewer than one-tenth of a percent have been explored [NOAA Ocean Exploration](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/education/seamounts/). Each seamount is a potential island of biodiversity: currents flow around them, concentrating nutrients and creating habitats for corals, sponges, and fish that may exist nowhere else. On a single expedition in January 2024, Schmidt Ocean Institute discovered four previously unknown seamounts in international waters off Central America, the tallest rising 2,681 meters from the seafloor — higher than any mountain in the eastern United States [Schmidt Ocean Institute, February 2024](https://schmidtocean.org/four-new-seamounts-discovered-in-the-high-seas/). Four mountains, hidden in plain sight, on a planet we have the audacity to call explored.

The ocean's trenches are the reverse image of its mountains: long, narrow canyons where one tectonic plate plunges beneath another, reaching depths that invert the scale of terrestrial elevation. The Mariana Trench's Challenger Deep, at approximately 10,935 meters below sea level, is the deepest point on Earth — deeper than Mount Everest is tall [NOAA Ocean Exploration](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/ocean-depth/). Only a handful of people have ever visited it. The first descent was made in 1960 by Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh in the bathyscaphe *Trieste*. No one returned until James Cameron's solo dive in 2012. The Five Deeps Expedition, led by explorer Victor Vescovo in the submersible *Limiting Factor*, conducted the first systematic survey of all five of the world's ocean deepest points between 2018 and 2019, discovering at least 40 new species and 30 previously unmapped underwater features in the process [Five Deeps Expedition](https://fivedeeps.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/FDE-Challenger-Release-FINAL-5132019.pdf); [Smithsonian Magazine, September 2019](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/american-becomes-first-to-visit-five-deepest-spots-worlds-oceans-180973094/). The deepest places on Earth have been visited fewer times than the surface of the Moon.

## III. The Life That Should Not Exist

The hadal zone — the region of ocean trenches from 6,000 to 11,000 meters — was once considered virtually sterile. The pressure exceeds 1,000 atmospheres. No sunlight penetrates. Temperatures hover just above freezing. Nutrients are scarce. It was assumed that very little could survive.

That assumption has been demolished.

In 2025, the Mariana Trench Environment and Ecology Research (MEER) project published a trio of papers in *Cell* that revealed an ecosystem of staggering complexity. Metagenomic sequencing of 1,194 sediment samples generated a 92-terabase-pair dataset — the largest of its kind — and identified 7,564 microbial species, 89.4 percent of which had never been reported. The study found that environmental selection, not random drift, drives microbial community assembly in these extreme depths, and that hadal microbes operate under evolutionary pressures distinct from those in shallower waters [Cell, 2025](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674(24)01436-3); [Science, March 2025](https://www.science.org/content/article/oceans-deepest-trenches-are-home-incredible-diversity).

That same year, researchers aboard the Chinese manned submersible *Fendouzhe* discovered flourishing chemosynthetic communities at 9,533 meters in the Kuril-Kamchatka Trench — the deepest and most extensive chemosynthesis-based ecosystems known on Earth. Fields of tubeworms up to 30 centimeters long, bivalves, and other invertebrates stretched across a 2,500-kilometer zone along the trench floor, sustained by methane produced by microbes rather than by sunlight [Nature, July 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09317-z); [Phys.org, July 2025](https://phys.org/news/2025-07-deepest-animal-communities-km-sea.html). They do not need the sun. They never did.

A 2026 study published in *Science* described a previously unknown hard-substrate fauna at depths from 9,000 to 10,898 meters in the Kermadec and Mariana trenches. The researchers identified 32 species across six phyla — most of them new to science — dominated by heterotrophic foraminifera that feed on terrestrial pine pollen that has fallen to the deepest ocean floor. The study concluded that similar protistan-dominated communities likely thrive in hadal regions across Oceania, representing unrecognized carbon hotspots in the deepest parts of the global ocean [Science, May 2026](https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.aea7086). Life in the most remote places on Earth, sustained by pine pollen carried from continents above. The planet is more connected than we ever imagined, and it has nothing to do with us.

Nor is life confined to the trenches. In January 2025, an iceberg the size of Chicago — designated A-84 — calved from the George VI Ice Shelf in Antarctica, exposing 510 square kilometers of seafloor that had been covered by 150-meter-thick ice for centuries. Within days, a Schmidt Ocean Institute expedition deployed the remotely operated vehicle *SuBastian* and found flourishing ecosystems at depths up to 1,300 meters: large corals, sponges, icefish, giant sea spiders, and octopus. "The science team was originally in this remote region to study the seafloor and ecosystem at the interface between ice and sea," said Dr. Jyotika Virmani, Schmidt Ocean Institute's Executive Director. "Being right there when this iceberg calved from the ice shelf presented a rare scientific opportunity" [Schmidt Ocean Institute, March 2025](https://schmidtocean.org/thriving-antarctic-ecosystems-found-in-wake-of-recently-detached-iceberg/). The ice retreated, and life was already there — as though it had been waiting.

## IV. A Census of the Unseen

The scale of marine biodiversity — and our ignorance of it — is one of the great scientific stories of our time. It is also a rather good comedy of manners, if one has the detachment to see it that way.

The Census of Marine Life, completed in 2010 after a decade of international collaboration, documented approximately 250,000 known marine species (excluding microbes). But the Census estimated that the true total could exceed one million — meaning roughly 75 percent of marine species have yet to be formally described [Census of Marine Life](http://www.coml.org/media-resources/frequently-asked-questions-faq.html). We have lived on this planet for the entirety of human history and have named barely a quarter of its co-inhabitants.

The Ocean Census, launched in 2023 by The Nippon Foundation and Nekton, aims to accelerate this process dramatically. In its Year 2 Impact Report (April 2024 — March 2025), the program documented over 900 new species — increasing the global annual marine discovery rate by 38 percent [Ocean Census Year 2 Impact Report](https://oceancensus.org/ocean-census-year-2-impact-report/). By Year 3, the pace had accelerated further: 1,121 new species in a single year, a 54 percent increase over the baseline discovery rate [Discover Magazine, May 2026](https://www.discovermagazine.com/ocean-census-uncovers-1-121-new-marine-species-from-sea-caves-to-deep-trenches-49127).

These discoveries span the full range of ocean depths. A newly identified chimaera — a "ghost shark" that split from sharks and rays nearly 400 million years ago — was found in Australia's Coral Sea Marine Park. A carnivorous sponge nicknamed the "death ball," covered in microscopic hook-like structures that trap crustaceans, was discovered at 3,600 meters near the South Sandwich Islands. A bristle worm living inside the translucent silica skeleton of a glass sponge was found near volcanic seamounts off Japan at 800 meters. Even shallow Mediterranean sea caves off Marseille yielded an orange-striped shrimp new to science [Ocean Census, 2026](https://oceancensus.org/our-top-12-highlights-from-the-ocean-census-in-2025/). Every place we look closely enough, we find something that has never been seen.

The Schmidt Ocean Institute, operating the research vessel *Falkor (too)*, has been one of the most prolific contributors to this wave of discovery. Three expeditions to the Nazca and Salas y Gómez Ridges in 2024 documented over 150 previously unknown species, bringing the known species count in this portion of the Pacific from approximately 1,000 to over 1,300. The expeditions also captured the first-ever footage of a live *Promachoteuthis* squid — a genus so elusive that most known specimens were collected in the late 1800s — and documented a Casper octopus in the Southern Pacific for the first time [Schmidt Ocean Institute, August 2024](https://schmidtocean.org/expeditions-chapter_8-2024/); [Ocean Decade, August 2024](https://oceandecade.org/news/20-suspected-new-marine-species-arise-from-sois-nazca-ridge-expedition).

Off Argentina, a 2026 expedition along the entire continental shelf documented the largest-known *Bathelia candida* coral reef — covering at least 0.4 square kilometers, nearly the size of Vatican City — along with 28 suspected new species and the first deep-water whale fall observed in Argentine waters [Schmidt Ocean Institute, February 2026](https://schmidtocean.org/argentinas-deep-sea-is-more-biodiverse-than-scientists-thought/). Off Uruguay, an expedition discovered thriving cold-water coral reefs built by *Desmophyllum pertusum*, a species recently listed as vulnerable to extinction, along with at least 30 potential new species [Schmidt Ocean Institute, September 2025](https://schmidtocean.org/vibrant-coral-reefs-discovered-in-historic-exploration-of-uruguays-deep-sea/).

These are not isolated findings. They are the first systematic glimpses of ecosystems that cover 70 percent of the planet. The Ocean Census now involves more than 1,000 scientists across 400 institutions. The Ocean Biodiversity Information System (OBIS) — the global repository for marine species observations — has surpassed 177 million occurrence records for 204,000 species, drawn from 7,201 datasets [OBIS](https://obis.org/). The database grows by millions of records each year, contributed by scientists in 99 countries.

And still the estimate holds: roughly 90 percent of marine species remain unknown.

## V. The Vessels That Take Us There

The tools for exploring this hidden world are as remarkable as the world itself — which is fortunate, because the world itself is remarkably unforgiving.

The human-occupied submersible *Alvin*, operated by the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, has been the workhorse of American deep-sea research for six decades. As of December 31, 2025, *Alvin* had completed 5,340 dives, totaling more than 11 million meters of vertical descent — equivalent to descending from sea level to the bottom of the ocean and back more than 500 times. Over its 60-year career, the submersible has carried 16,020 people to the seafloor, supported the publication of thousands of peer-reviewed studies, and spent a cumulative 37,290 hours submerged [WHOI Alvin Dive Statistics](https://www.whoi.edu/what-we-do/explore/underwater-vehicles/hov-alvin/dive-statistics/).

The submersible has been upgraded repeatedly. Its most recent overhaul, completed in 2022, extended its depth range from 4,500 to 6,500 meters — putting 99 percent of the ocean floor within reach. The new titanium personnel sphere, improved imaging systems, and enhanced manipulator arms represent more than a decade of engineering. "Alvin's recent upgrade opens new possibilities for deep-sea research," wrote the science verification team, noting that the sub can now access the abyssal and upper hadal zones — some of the least-understood regions on Earth [Phys.org, February 2025](https://phys.org/news/2025-02-alvin-ocean-depths.pdf); [WHOI, Alvin 60](https://www.whoi.edu/alvin60).

In the Pacific, the Chinese manned submersible *Fendouzhe* — "Striver" — has pushed even deeper. Capable of reaching the deepest parts of the ocean at nearly 11,000 meters, *Fendouzhe* conducted 33 dives in the Mariana Trench and neighboring regions between August and November 2021, collecting 1,648 sediment samples and generating the most comprehensive hadal ecological dataset ever assembled. The submersible's 2024 dives in the Kuril-Kamchatka and Aleutian trenches revealed the deepest chemosynthetic communities ever documented, at depths exceeding 9,500 meters [Cell, 2025](https://www.cell.com/cell/fulltext/S0092-8674(24)01436-3); [Nature, July 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09317-z).

Meanwhile, a new generation of autonomous and remotely operated vehicles is expanding the reach of ocean exploration. The Schmidt Ocean Institute's *ROV SuBastian*, deployed from the research vessel *Falkor (too)*, can operate at depths of 4,500 meters and has conducted hundreds of dives across the Pacific, Atlantic, and Southern oceans, streaming live video to shore-based scientists and the public. NOAA's *Okeanos Explorer* has mapped hundreds of thousands of square kilometers of seafloor, discovering deep-sea coral habitats and hydrothermal vent fields. Environmental DNA (eDNA) sampling, integrated into regular operations aboard *Okeanos Explorer* in 2021, has opened a new window into deep-sea biodiversity by detecting genetic traces of organisms that conventional sampling methods miss entirely [NOAA Ocean Exploration, May 2026](https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/news/from-seawater-to-sequences-exploring-noaas-new-deep-sea-environmental-dna-dataset/). The water itself gives away its secrets.

At the deepest extremes, NASA and WHOI have developed the Orpheus autonomous underwater vehicles — small, pressure-tolerant robots designed to explore the hadal zone in swarms, capable of navigating the narrow, rocky terrain of ocean trenches autonomously [WHOI Hadal Zone](https://www.whoi.edu/ocean-learning-hub/ocean-topics/how-the-ocean-works/ocean-zones/hadal-zone/).

## VI. The Lure of the Seabed

The deep ocean is not only a site of scientific discovery. It is also a repository of mineral wealth — and a new frontier for geopolitical competition. One might almost call it a gold rush, if gold rushes were conducted through intergovernmental organizations with 170 member states.

Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), the international seabed beyond national jurisdiction — known as "the Area" — belongs to no single country or corporation. It is managed by the International Seabed Authority (ISA), an autonomous intergovernmental organization established in 1994, with 170 member states, charged with regulating mineral-related activities "for the benefit of all humankind" [ISA, 2024 Annual Report](https://isa.org.jm/secretary-general-annual-report-2024/). One admires the idealism, while noting that "all humankind" has never been a phrase that survived first contact with valuable resources.

The resources in question are extraordinary. Polymetallic nodules — potato-sized concretions rich in manganese, nickel, cobalt, and copper — cover vast areas of the abyssal plain, particularly in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone (CCZ) of the central Pacific. The ISA has estimated that some 5.4 billion tons of metal-bearing nodules exist in reserved areas alone, though data quality varies significantly across the survey regions [ISA Policy Brief, July 2024](https://www.isa.org.jm/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Policy_brief_Current_status_of_reserved_areas_with_ISA.pdf). Cobalt-rich ferromanganese crusts coat the slopes of seamounts in the western Pacific. Polymetallic sulfides — deposits rich in copper, zinc, gold, and silver — form around hydrothermal vents along mid-ocean ridges.

As of 2024, the ISA has issued 30 exploration contracts to 21 contractors, covering polymetallic nodules (19 contracts), polymetallic sulfides (8 contracts), and cobalt-rich crusts (5 contracts). Contractors include national agencies from China, India, Russia, South Korea, France, Germany, Japan, and Poland, as well as private companies and entities from developing states and small island nations including Nauru, Tonga, Kiribati, Cook Islands, and Jamaica [ISA Exploration Contracts](https://isa.org.jm/exploration-contracts/). These contracts grant exclusive exploration rights for 15-year periods, during which contractors are required to collect environmental baseline data, train personnel from developing states, and relinquish portions of their exploration areas at periodic intervals.

The tension between mineral extraction and environmental protection has intensified as scientific understanding of deep-sea ecosystems has grown. The 2025 discovery that chemosynthetic communities thrive across a 2,500-kilometer swath of the northwestern Pacific trench system — far more widespread than previously recognized — raises urgent questions about the potential impact of mining on ecosystems that may prove to be both fragile and extensive [Nature, 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09317-z). Contractors have invested an estimated $414 million in environmental baseline studies as of 2024, and the ISA is developing regional environmental management plans for the CCZ, the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, and the Indian Ocean [ISA Environmental Baseline Policy Brief](https://isa.org.jm/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/ISA-Environmental-Baseline-Policy-Brief-SSKI_2025.pdf). The irony is almost too neat: we are discovering what we would destroy in the same motion.

In June 2025, the ISA launched the Deep-Sea Biobank Initiative (DBI) on the margins of the UN Ocean Conference in Nice, establishing a global repository of biological samples and genetic data from the international seabed area, designed to promote equitable access to deep-sea research, particularly for developing states [UN Geneva, July 2025](https://www.ungeneva.org/en/news-media/news/2025/07/108752/deep-dive-international-seabed-authority-why-it-matters-now).

## VII. What Remains

After two and a half centuries of systematic exploration — from the *HMS Challenger* expedition of 1872–1876, which first discovered polymetallic nodules in the Pacific, to the 2025 discoveries of thriving ecosystems beneath Antarctic ice shelves — the deep ocean remains, by any honest measure, largely unknown.

The ocean's average depth is 3,682 meters, yet our knowledge of what lives below 200 meters is fragmentary. The global ocean has a total volume of approximately 1.332 billion cubic kilometers [NOAA NCEI World Ocean Volumes](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/sites/g/files/anmtlf171/files/2023-01/World%20Ocean%20Volumes.pdf). The volume that has been biologically sampled — with nets, cores, ROVs, or submersibles — is minuscule. The fraction that has been visually observed is infinitesimal. We are like a person who, having glanced at a single page of a library, declares himself well-read.

"We have only assessed under 30 percent of the samples collected from this expedition," said Dr. Michelle Taylor, head of science at Ocean Census, after a single expedition to the South Sandwich Islands that yielded 30 new species, speaking of the backlog that every deep-sea biologist knows: the rate of sample collection far exceeds the rate of analysis [ABC News, October 2025](https://abcnews.com/International/dozens-new-species-including-carnivorous-death-ball-sponge/story?id=126986681). The average time between collecting a specimen and formally describing it in the scientific literature is 13.5 years. There are an estimated 175,000 undocumented new species sitting in museum collections and institutional sample banks — already collected, already preserved, already known to exist, and waiting. The discoveries have already been made. They are simply waiting to be noticed.

"The deep sea may have served as an 'ecological refuge' during environmental changes caused by dramatic temperature and oxygen fluctuations," the MEER project researchers noted — a suggestion that the hadal zone has functioned as a sanctuary for life during past mass extinctions [Science, March 2025](https://www.science.org/content/article/oceans-deepest-trenches-are-home-incredible-diversity). If that is true, then the deep ocean is not merely a place of discovery. It is a library of survival — a record of how life persists through planetary crisis.

And we have read almost none of it.

---

The descent ends, eventually, with an ascent.

The submersible releases its ballast weights and begins to rise. The pressure decreases. The black fades to indigo, then to blue, then to the dazzling white of sunlight on the surface. The hatch opens. The crew blinks in the light. The voyage that took two and a half hours to descend lasted perhaps ten hours on the bottom, but it will take years — or a lifetime — to interpret everything they saw.

There are more seamounts on Earth than there are people who have visited them. There are more undiscovered species in the ocean than discovered ones. We have mapped less than a third of the seafloor, and seen less than a thousandth of it. The planet has a hidden continent, and we are still learning where its boundaries lie. The greatest age of discovery is not behind us. It is beneath us.
