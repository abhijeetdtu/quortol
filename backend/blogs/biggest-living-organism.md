# The Biggest Thing Alive: Five Organisms, Five Measuring Sticks, and One Unresolved Riddle

*Ask any schoolchild what the largest living thing on Earth is, and they will answer with the serene confidence of the partly informed: the blue whale. They are not wrong — though to be merely not wrong is a far cry from being right. The answer depends, as these answers always do, on what one means by largest, what one means by living, and, most treacherously of all, what one means by thing. There exist at least five organisms — one of them almost entirely underground, one of them spanning the breadth of two hemispheres of ocean, and one of them a single plant stretching 180 kilometers across the Australian seafloor — each of which is, by a different and equally defensible logic, the biggest thing alive.* [NOAA Fisheries, "Blue Whale"](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/blue-whale); [Guinness World Records, "Largest Living Organism"](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/606952-largest-living-organism)

---

## I. The Whale (Length)

The blue whale (*Balaenoptera musculus*) is the largest animal ever to have existed on this planet — larger than any dinosaur, any prehistoric reptile, any living creature organized around a backbone and a central nervous system. The heaviest reliably measured specimen, a female taken from the Southern Ocean in 1947, weighed 190 tonnes and measured 27.6 meters. The longest confirmed specimen, hauled ashore in 1909 at the whaling station at Grytviken, South Georgia, stretched 33.57 meters — roughly the length of a Boeing 737 — and, had anyone thought to weigh her, would almost certainly have surpassed even that extraordinary figure. [Guinness World Records, "Heaviest Animal Ever"](https://guinnessworldrecords.es/world-records/757349-heaviest-animal-ever); [NOAA Fisheries, "Blue Whale"](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/blue-whale)

A blue whale's heart weighs roughly 180 kilograms — about the mass of two grown human beings — and can be heard beating from several kilometers away through a hydrophone. Its tongue alone weighs as much as an elephant. Its calls reach 188 decibels, louder than a jet engine, and travel hundreds of kilometers through the ocean, as though the sea itself were merely a telephone line. [National Geographic, "Blue Whale"](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/mammals/facts/blue-whale); [World Wildlife Fund, "Blue Whale"](https://www.worldwildlife.org/species/blue-whale)

![A blue whale swimming in the ocean, the largest animal ever to have lived on Earth.](/api/blog/images/biggest-living-organism_blue_whale.jpg)
*A blue whale swimming in the ocean. Credit: NOAA Fisheries / Southwest Fisheries Science Center (public domain).*


But here is the first surprise, and it is a pleasant one: the blue whale is the *smallest* organism on this list. It is the biggest animal. The question of the largest living thing turns out to require a vocabulary that animality alone cannot supply.

## II. The Sequoia (Volume)

The General Sherman Tree stands in the Giant Forest of Sequoia National Park, California — a giant sequoia (*Sequoiadendron giganteum*), and by volume the largest single-stem tree on Earth. Its trunk alone contains 52,500 cubic feet (1,486.6 cubic meters) of wood — enough, the National Park Service helpfully calculates, to build 120 average-sized houses. The trunk weighs nearly 1,400 tons, which is roughly equivalent to 15 adult blue whales, 10 diesel-electric train locomotives, or 25 military battle tanks — comparisons that grow only more absurd the longer one contemplates them. [NPS, "The General Sherman Tree"](https://www.nps.gov/seki/learn/nature/sherman.htm)

![The General Sherman Tree in Sequoia National Park, the largest single-stem tree by volume on Earth.](/api/blog/images/biggest-living-organism_general_sherman.jpg)
*The General Sherman Tree, Sequoia National Park, California. Credit: Tichnor Brothers / Boston Public Library (public domain).*


There are taller trees — the coastal redwoods of California can reach nearly 116 meters, a full 32 meters taller than Sherman. There are wider trees — the giant sequoia known as General Grant, also in Sequoia National Park, has a base circumference of 107.6 feet compared to Sherman's 102.6 feet. But no tree on Earth packs more sheer wood volume into a single trunk. [NPS, "The Largest Trees in the World"](https://www.nps.gov/seki/learn/nature/largest-trees-in-world.htm)

The age of General Sherman is estimated at between 2,200 and 2,700 years. It was already a thousand years old when the Roman Empire fell. It was already two thousand years old when the first European set foot in California. An NPS historical brochure from the 1930s described it as "the largest, and perhaps the oldest living thing" — a claim that could be made with some confidence in that innocent era, before the discovery of clonal organisms, before the aspen and the fungus and the seagrass arrived to complicate the very idea of what a "living thing" might mean. [NPS History, "General Sherman and General Grant Brochure"](https://npshistory.com/publications/seki/brochures/gen-sherman-grant.pdf)

## III. The Fungus (Terrestrial Area)

In the Malheur National Forest of eastern Oregon, roughly eleven miles east of the town of Prairie City, a single organism hides beneath the soil. It is *Armillaria ostoyae*, the honey fungus, and it covers 2,385 acres — about 3.7 square miles, or 1,665 American football fields. It weighs an estimated 35,000 tons. It may be 8,650 years old. And almost all of it is invisible — which is precisely the sort of fact that ought to make one reconsider what one means by "it." [USDA Forest Service, "The Malheur National Forest: Location of the World's Largest Living Organism"](https://docslib.org/doc/4367100/the-malheur-national-forest-location-of-the-worlds-largest-living-organism-the-humongous-fungus); [Oregon Encyclopedia, "Humongous Fungus"](https://www.oregonencyclopedia.org/articles/humongous-fungus-armillaria-ostoyae/)

This organism was not discovered so much as mapped. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, a team led by USDA Forest Service pathologist Catherine Parks collected fungal samples from dead and dying conifers across the forest, grew them together in laboratory Petri dishes, and watched them merge. When two samples from different locations belong to the same genetic individual, they do not react with hostility — they fuse, like old friends greeting each other after a long separation. Samples collected from across 9.65 square kilometers fused without resistance. They were the same organism. [Ferguson et al. (2003), Canadian Journal of Forest Research](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/20135); [Scientific American, "Strange but True: The Largest Organism on Earth Is a Fungus" (Casselman, 2007)](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/strange-but-true-largest-organism-is-fungus/)

![Armillaria ostoyae mushrooms (honey fungus) at the base of an infected grand fir in Malheur National Forest, Oregon.](/api/blog/images/biggest-living-organism_armillaria_fungus.jpg)
*Armillaria ostoyae mushrooms at the base of an infected grand fir in Malheur National Forest, Oregon. Credit: Craig L. Schmitt, Michael L. Tatum / USDA Forest Service (public domain).*


*A. ostoyae* is a pathogen. It spreads through the soil via rhizomorphs — black, shoestring-like cords that bridge gaps of up to 18 meters between one food source and another. It infects living conifers, kills them, and uses their root systems as highways to reach the next tree. The disease centers it creates are visible from the air: patches of dead and dying trees radiating outward from a point of origin, the oldest mortality at the center, the newest at the edges. The fungus has no central body, no head, no heart, no visible distinction between itself and its surroundings. It simply extends, year after year, consuming and growing, at a rate of between 0.7 and 3.3 feet per year. At that pace, the largest genet in Malheur has been expanding for between 1,900 and 8,650 years. [USDA Forest Service, "Armillaria Root Disease"](https://apps.fs.usda.gov/r6_decaid/views/armillaria_root_disease.html); [Ferguson et al. (2003)](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/20135)

What the public sees of this organism — the honey-colored mushrooms that appear in autumn after the first rains — are merely its fruiting bodies. They are the fungal equivalent of the tip of an iceberg: the visible fraction of a whole that exists almost entirely out of sight, which is a metaphor the fungus would surely appreciate if it had a sense of irony. [Scientific American (Casselman, 2007)](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/strange-but-true-largest-organism-is-fungus/)

## IV. The Aspen (Mass)

In south-central Utah, on the Sevier Plateau within the Fishlake National Forest, lives Pando — from the Latin *pando*, meaning "I spread." It is a clonal colony of quaking aspen (*Populus tremuloides*), and to walk through it is to walk through a forest that is, in a literal and biologically verified sense, a single tree. [USFS Fishlake National Forest, "Pando"](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r04/fishlake/recreation/explore-forest/pando)

![Quaking aspen trees in the Pando clone, Fishlake National Forest, Utah, in autumn color.](/api/blog/images/biggest-living-organism_pando_aspen.jpg)
*Fall color in the Pando aspen clone, Fishlake National Forest, Utah. Credit: J. Zapell / USDA Forest Service (public domain).*


Pando consists of approximately 47,000 genetically identical stems (called ramets), each appearing to be an individual tree, all connected by a single underground root system that spans 42.8 hectares (106 acres). The clone weighs an estimated 6,000 metric tons — 13 million pounds. By dry-weight mass, it is the largest known organism on Earth, and by a generous margin: roughly 15 times heavier than the Oregon fungus and nearly 3 times heavier than General Sherman. The claim was first advanced in a 1992 letter to the journal *Nature* by three University of Colorado professors — Michael Grant, Jeffrey Mitton, and Yan Linhart — who had noticed a 1975 paper by University of Michigan botanist Burton Barnes describing an unusually large aspen stand. [DeWoody et al. (2008), Western North American Naturalist](https://bioone.org/journals/western-north-american-naturalist/volume-68/issue-4/1527-0904-68.4.493/Pando-Lives--Molecular-Genetic-Evidence-of-a-Giant-Aspen/10.3398/1527-0904-68.4.493.full); [USU Digital Commons, "Pando Clone History and Evaluation"](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=8701&context=aspen_bib)

In 2008, a team of scientists from the USDA Forest Service and Utah State University confirmed Pando's singularity using modern microsatellite DNA markers. They sampled 209 stems across the putative clone boundary and identified a single genetic individual coexistent with the morphological boundary described by Barnes. The results were published in the *Western North American Naturalist* under the delightful title "Pando Lives" — a paper whose title alone contains more joy than most scientific research permits itself. [DeWoody et al. (2008)](https://bioone.org/journals/western-north-american-naturalist/volume-68/issue-4/1527-0904-68.4.493/Pando-Lives--Molecular-Genetic-Evidence-of-a-Giant-Aspen/10.3398/1527-0904-68.4.493.full)

In 2024, a team led by Rozenn Pineau published a preprint on bioRxiv (since deposited in the NSF Public Access Repository) that sequenced more than 500 samples from Pando and neighboring clones — roots, leaves, bark. Using phylogenetic models of somatic mutation accumulation, they estimated Pando's age at between 16,000 and 80,000 years. If the upper bound holds, Pando was already tens of thousands of years old when *Homo sapiens* first left Africa — which is to say that this organism was ancient before the species that named it was even young. [Pineau et al. (2024), NSF Public Access Repository](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10584991-mosaic-somatic-mutations-earths-oldest-living-organism-pando); [ScienceAlert, "Largest Organism in The World Could Officially Be The Oldest Living Thing"](https://www.sciencealert.com/largest-organism-in-the-world-could-officially-be-the-oldest-living-thing)

But Pando is in trouble. A 2018 study in *PLOS One* by Paul Rogers and Darren McAvoy of Utah State University found that the clone is "clearly losing ground in the past half-century." The cause is chronic herbivory by mule deer (*Odocoileus hemionus*), whose browsing of young aspen suckers has prevented successful regeneration across most of the clone. Aerial photo sequences spanning seven decades show a steady thinning of the canopy, a forest slowly converting to a non-forest state. Only a single fenced exclosure — amounting to roughly 14 percent of the clone — has shown adequate regeneration. Rogers and colleagues have described the situation as "breaking up" due to herbivory. [Rogers & McAvoy (2018), PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0203619); [Rogers (2022), Conservation Science and Practice](https://conbio.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/csp2.12804)

Pando has survived ice ages, megadroughts, and millennia of changing climates. What it may not survive is the combined pressure of deer, cattle, and a paved state highway that bisects the clone, carrying tourists to a popular resort area at Fish Lake. [Rogers & McAvoy (2018)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0203619)

## V. The Seagrass (Area — Global)

In June 2022, an international research team published a paper in the *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* that quietly detonated everything we thought we knew about the largest organism on Earth. They had been studying the seagrass *Posidonia australis* — Poseidon's ribbon weed — in the shallow, sun-drenched waters of Shark Bay, Western Australia, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. They had collected shoot samples from ten meadows across the bay, expecting to find multiple distinct plants. Instead, they found one. [Edgeloe et al. (2022), Proceedings of the Royal Society B](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2022.0538)

![Posidonia australis seagrass meadow in the shallow waters of Shark Bay, Western Australia.](/api/blog/images/biggest-living-organism_posidonia_seagrass.jpg)
*Posidonia australis (Poseidon's ribbon weed) seagrass meadow in Shark Bay, Western Australia. Credit: Rachel Austin, The University of Western Australia.*


A single clone of *P. australis* spans at least 180 kilometers — from White Island in the western gulf to the Faure Sill near L'Haridon Bight in the east. It covers approximately 200 square kilometers (77 square miles), making it the largest known clone in any environment on Earth. It is a polyploid — it carries 40 chromosomes instead of the usual 20 — and appears to be a hybrid between two closely related but genetically distinct seagrass species. This genomic duplication, the researchers propose, may have given the plant the tools to outcompete its diploid progenitors and colonize the extreme conditions of Shark Bay, where salinity can reach nearly twice that of the open ocean and summer water temperatures exceed 30 degrees Celsius. [Edgeloe et al. (2022), Proc. R. Soc. B](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2022.0538); [The Conversation (Breed, 2022)](https://theconversation.com/meet-the-worlds-largest-plant-a-single-seagrass-clone-stretching-180-km-in-western-australias-shark-bay-184056)

The seagrass clone is estimated to be around 4,500 years old, in agreement with the timing of the Holocene high stand — the period when rising sea levels inundated what is now Shark Bay, creating the shallow marine environment the seagrass would come to dominate. The clone expanded via horizontal rhizome growth, fragmenting over time into physically disconnected patches that nonetheless remain genetically identical — separated by sandy gaps but still, in every meaningful biological sense, the same plant. [Edgeloe et al. (2022)](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.2022.0538)

Guinness World Records awarded the Shark Bay seagrass the record for "Largest Living Organism" in 2022. [Guinness World Records, "Largest Living Organism"](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/606952-largest-living-organism); [Guinness World Records, "Largest Plant by Area"](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/706618-largest-plant-by-area)

## VI. What Is an Individual?

Each of these five organisms — the whale, the sequoia, the fungus, the aspen, the seagrass — has held, at some point, the informal title of "world's largest living thing." Each still holds it by some defensible metric: the whale by length, the sequoia by trunk volume, the fungus by terrestrial area, the aspen by dry mass, the seagrass by total area. But the more interesting discovery, in comparing them, is that the phrase "living thing" turns out to be doing most of the conceptual work — and it is not at all clear what it means.

Pando is 47,000 trees. Are they one organism or 47,000? The roots connect them, but each stem has its own leaves, its own bark, its own eventual death. The fungus is 2,385 acres of thread and mat — but when you pick a honey mushroom in Malheur National Forest, are you harming the whole or merely plucking a fruit? The seagrass clone has fragmented into disconnected patches across 180 kilometers; the rhizomes that once connected them have rotted away. Genetic identity persists; physical continuity does not. Are they still one plant? [Discover Magazine (Dapcevich, 2025)](https://www.discovermagazine.com/the-worlds-largest-organism-is-a-35-000-ton-fungus-at-least-for-now-47733)

The philosopher and the biologist will give different answers. The philosopher might ask whether an organism requires a central nervous system, a unified consciousness, a distinguishable boundary between self and world. The biologist might note that genetic identity is a spectrum, that immunological compatibility varies, that the concept of the "individual" is itself a human convenience imposed on a living world that does not respect our categories. Some biologists have argued that clonal colonies like Pando and the fungus should be considered "superorganisms" — collections of genetically identical parts that function as a coordinated whole. Others have pushed back, noting that the individual stems of Pando compete with each other for light and nutrients, that the separate patches of the Shark Bay seagrass do not cooperate, and that calling a fragmented meadow a single organism may be more poetic than precise. [Wikipedia, "Largest Organisms"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_organism); [Montreal Science Centre, "The Two Largest Living Organisms on Earth"](https://www.montrealsciencecentre.com/blog/the-two-largest-living-organisms-on-earth)

The question resists resolution because it was never a scientific question to begin with. It is a question about language — about what we want the word "organism" to do. Do we want it to describe genetic continuity? Physiological integration? Functional coordination? Evolutionary individuality? Each answer yields a different champion, and the prize goes to whichever definition we find most flattering to our own sense of what life ought to be.

## VII. The View From Above

None of these organisms knows it is big. The blue whale does not know it is the largest animal that has ever lived; it knows only hunger, migration, the pressure of the deep. The General Sherman Tree does not know its trunk could build 120 houses; it grows one cubic meter of new wood per year, year after year, for twenty centuries, as though arithmetic were beneath its notice. The fungus does not know it covers 2,385 acres; it extends rhizomorphs into new soil, one millimeter at a time, sensing only the presence or absence of tree roots.

Pando's leaves tremble in the wind — which is why they are called "quaking" aspen — and the sound of 47,000 genetically identical trees shivering in the breeze is the sound of a single organism breathing. The seagrass in Shark Bay waves in the current, 200 square kilometers of one plant, a clone so vast that it has its own ecosystem, its own weather, its own microclimate — a republic of seagrass, governing itself without a constitution.

The question "What is the biggest living thing on Earth?" turns out to have no single answer, and that is precisely the point. Life does not fit neatly into categories of size and identity. It spreads — through roots, through rhizomorphs, through rhizomes, through seeds, through spores, through the deep ocean currents that carry whale song hundreds of kilometers — and in its spreading, it keeps revising the boundaries of what we thought a living thing could be.
