# The Long Pour

*On a warm evening in October 1978, President Jimmy Carter — a Southern Baptist who rarely drank — signed a bill that had nothing to do with beer. H.R. 1337 was a transportation tax measure. But tucked inside it was a single paragraph destined to alter American culture more than any highway: an amendment legalizing homebrewing for the first time since Prohibition. Nearly half a century later, that quiet stroke of the pen has produced nearly ten thousand breweries, a $71.8 billion economic engine, and an industry now confronting the hardest question of its existence: what happens when the party stops growing?*

---

**I.**

The American brewing industry in 1965 was a landscape of almost heroic uniformity. Eighty percent of the beer sold in the United States came from just six companies [Smithsonian NMAH, Anchor Brewing Company Records](https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/jimmy-carter-american-homebrew-hero). The light lager — pale, carbonated, engineered to offend no one — reigned unchallenged. Regional breweries were dying or being consumed. The number of operating breweries had fallen from more than 4,000 in the 1870s to barely 200. It looked like the inevitable end point of industrial efficiency, and the terminal point of pleasure.

That August, a 27-year-old Stanford graduate student in Japanese studies walked into a San Francisco bar and ordered an Anchor Steam. The bartender told him the brewery was about to close. The next day, Fritz Maytag bought a 51 percent stake for what he would later describe as "less than the price of a used car" [All About Beer, "Drafting a Revolution," 2013](https://allaboutbeer.com/article/drafting-a-revolution/). Maytag, heir to the appliance fortune, taught himself to brew. He brought back porter — the first dark beer brewed in America after Prohibition — and introduced Liberty Ale, considered by many the first modern IPA [CNBC, "How Anchor Brewing Changed the Beer Business," 2015](https://www.cnbc.com/2015/07/31/how-anchor-brewing-changed-the-beer-business.html). He did not know it yet, but he had started a revolution without a manifesto.

Thirteen years later, the legal bottleneck broke. On October 14, 1978, President Carter signed H.R. 1337, decriminalizing homebrewing at the federal level [National Museum of American History, "Jimmy Carter: American Homebrew Hero"](https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/jimmy-carter-american-homebrew-hero). The law took effect on February 1, 1979 — the same month Charlie Papazian and Charlie Matzen launched the American Homebrewers Association and published the first issue of *Zymurgy* magazine [American Homebrewers Association, "35th Anniversary of Homebrew Legalization"](https://www.homebrewersassociation.org/news/35th-anniversary-of-homebrew-legalization/). At the time, fewer than 100 breweries operated in the United States [Brewers Association, "U.S. Craft Brewery Count Reaches 2000," 2012](https://www.brewersassociation.org/brewing-industry-updates/u-s-craft-brewery-count-reaches-2000/). The same year the law passed, a young homebrewer from Chico, California, named Ken Grossman opened Sierra Nevada Brewing Company with a partner and a hand-built brewhouse [Oregon State University, "Craft Beer in the United States: History, Numbers, and Geography"](https://liberalarts.oregonstate.edu/sites/liberalarts.oregonstate.edu/files/2025-07/tremblay_c_-_vol.10-issue03-craft-beer-in-the-united-states-history-numbers-and-geography-by-kenneth-g.-elzinga3.pdf). In 1984, Jim Koch — a Harvard MBA who came from a long line of brewers — launched the Boston Beer Company, brewing his Samuel Adams lager under contract at a Pittsburgh facility [PUNCH, "The Coronation of Craft Beer," 2021](https://punchdrink.com/articles/coronation-of-craft-beer-boom-history-sam-adams-boston-lager/).

The craft brewery count crept upward with the patience of a slow fermentation: 124 in 1988, 500 in 1994, 1,000 in 1996 [BA, "U.S. Craft Brewery Count Reaches 2000"](https://www.brewersassociation.org/brewing-industry-updates/u-s-craft-brewery-count-reaches-2000/). The first wave crested around 1999, with roughly 1,500 craft breweries. Then came a shakeout. The count slipped back to 1,394 by 2005, as overexpansion and mediocre quality winnowed the field. But the survivors were stronger, and the base of committed homebrewers — now estimated at 1.1 million — kept feeding the pipeline of new talent [Brewers Association, "U.S. Passes 4,000 Breweries," 2015](https://www.brewersassociation.org/insights/4000-breweries/).

**II.**

What happened next has few parallels in American small business history.

From 2005 to 2015, craft brewers posted double-digit volume growth in eight out of ten years [BA, "Small and Independent Brewers Continue to Grow Double Digits," 2016](https://www.craftbeer.com/news/craftbeer-com-news/small-independent-brewers-continue-grow-double-digits). The brewery count began to accelerate at a rate that seemed almost exponential. The Brewers Association counted 1,759 operating breweries in 2010 [BA, "11 Percent Volume Growth for U.S. Craft Brewers in 2010"](https://www.brewbound.com/news/brewers-association-reports-11-percent-volume-growth-for-u-s-craft-brewers-in-2010/). By 2012, the number had hit 2,051. Then 3,040 by mid-2014 [BA, "U.S. Brewery Count Tops 3,000," 2014](https://www.brewersassociation.org/insights/us-brewery-count-tops-3000/). In 2015, America passed 4,000 breweries — more than at any point since the 1870s [BA, "U.S. Passes 4,000 Breweries," 2015](https://www.brewersassociation.org/insights/4000-breweries/).

The Bureau of Labor Statistics captured the same explosion from a different angle. In 2015, the BLS counted 2,190 brewery establishments under NAICS 31212. By 2019, that number had more than doubled to 4,733. By 2024, it reached 6,044 [BLS, Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, NAICS 31212 Breweries](https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/ENUUS00020531212).

The growth was not just in numbers but in cultural reach. Craft beer's share of the total beer market climbed from about 5 percent in the early 2000s to 12 percent by 2015 and 13.3 percent by 2024 [BA, National Beer Stats](https://www.brewersassociation.org/statistics-and-data/national-beer-stats/). Retail dollar value hit $22.3 billion in 2015 [BA, "Small and Independent Brewers Continue to Grow Double Digits," 2016](https://www.craftbeer.com/news/craftbeer-com-news/small-independent-brewers-continue-grow-double-digits). Production volume peaked at 26.3 million barrels in 2019 [BA, 2020 Annual Production Infographic](https://cdn.brewersassociation.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/05150445/2020_BA_growth-infographic.pdf). Breweries opened at a rate of more than two per day, every day, for years [Foundation for Economic Education, "America's Amazing Beer Renaissance," 2018](https://fee.org/articles/americas-amazing-beer-revealed-in-a-single-chart/).

A new business model emerged that would reshape the industry: the taproom brewery. Unlike brewpubs (which run restaurants) or microbreweries (which distribute packaged beer), taprooms sold beer directly to customers for on-site consumption without a full kitchen. It was a low-capital, high-margin model — the industry had discovered that people would pay more for the experience of watching their beer be made than for the beer itself. It proliferated wildly. By 2020, there were 3,471 taproom breweries — the largest single category of craft brewer [BA, 2020 Annual Production Infographic](https://cdn.brewersassociation.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/05150445/2020_BA_growth-infographic.pdf).

The industry had become an economic force. In 2024, the craft brewing industry contributed $77.1 billion to the U.S. economy and supported nearly 460,000 jobs [Brewers Association, "The 2024 Year in Beer"](https://www.brewersassociation.org/association-news/the-2024-year-in-beer/). But beneath the surface, the mathematics were already turning.

**III.**

The first sign of trouble came in 2020, when the pandemic slammed the hospitality economy with methodical brutality. Craft production fell 9 percent to 23.1 million barrels [BA, 2020 Annual Production Infographic](https://cdn.brewersassociation.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/05150445/2020_BA_growth-infographic.pdf). Taprooms shuttered. Distribution seized up. But 2021 brought a sharp rebound — 8 percent growth — as Americans rushed back to their local breweries with the enthusiasm of the newly paroled [BeverageDaily, "US Craft Beer: How Is the Industry Faring?" 2025](https://www.beveragedaily.com/Article/2025/04/23/us-craft-beer-how-is-the-industry-faring/).

The respite was temporary. In 2022, craft volume slipped 2 percent. In 2023, it slipped another 1 percent. In 2024, the bottom dropped further: production fell 4 percent to 23.1 million barrels — the largest decline since 2020 [BA, "Annual Craft Brewing Industry Production Report," 2025](https://www.brewersassociation.org/association-news/brewers-association-reports-2024-u-s-craft-brewing-industry-figures/). For the first time since 2005, brewery closings outnumbered openings: 529 closed against 430 that opened. The total number of craft breweries fell to 9,796, down from 9,761 the year before.

The broader beer market was contracting too — overall U.S. beer volume declined 1.2 percent in 2024 — but craft was contracting faster. Craft's market share slipped to 13.3 percent. Among brewery types, the distribution-focused microbreweries were hit hardest, losing 7.5 percent of volume [The New Brewer, "May/June 2025 Industry Review Issue"](https://www.brewersassociation.org/the-new-brewer/may-june-2025/).

Matt Gacioch, the Brewers Association's staff economist, offered a characteristically measured assessment: "In a mature market, not every year is going to be defined by substantial growth" [BA, 2024 Annual Production Report](https://www.brewersassociation.org/association-news/brewers-association-reports-2024-u-s-craft-brewing-industry-figures/).

Then came 2025. Craft production fell another 5 percent to 21.86 million barrels. Sixty percent of breweries reported volume declines. The number of operating craft breweries dropped 2.9 percent to 9,578. New brewery openings plummeted to 300 — down from 518 the year before and a distant echo of the 800-plus annual openings of the peak years [BA, "A Year of Correction for Craft Beer," 2026](https://www.brewersassociation.org/association-news/a-year-of-correction-for-craft-beer-with-early-signals-of-recovery/). Retail dollar value fell 3.6 percent to $27.8 billion.

But look closer, and the picture is not uniformly bleak. Nearly 40 percent of breweries *grew* in 2025. Craft beer's share of the total beer market *inched up* from 13.2 percent to 13.3 percent, because the overall beer category declined even faster — 5.7 percent. And the smallest breweries — taprooms and brewpubs producing fewer than 1,000 barrels annually — showed the most resilience. Among that group, 50 percent reported growth in the first half of 2025 [BA, "2025 Midyear Report"](https://www.brewersassociation.org/association-news/2025-midyear-report/).

The headline numbers hide a bifurcation that will likely define the next decade of craft beer. Local, hospitality-driven models are holding their own. Distribution-reliant, middle-sized breweries are getting squeezed from both directions — by smaller taprooms with better margins and by larger players with better scale. The middle, as always in America, is the first place the pressure shows.

**IV.**

If there is a single bright spot bright enough to call a trend, it is the rise of non-alcoholic beer.

Over the past four years, NA beer has experienced what the BA's Matt Gacioch calls a "remarkable" surge. From 2021 to 2025, volume sales lifted 111 percent. Dollar sales exploded 159 percent [BA, "The State of Non-Alc," 2026](https://www.brewersassociation.org/insights/the-state-of-non-alc/). The number of NA brands tracked by NIQ jumped from 173 in 2021 to 484 in 2025 — an increase of 180 percent. The number of companies producing them rose from 91 to 213.

Craft brewers are disproportionately driving this growth. In 2025, craft made up 31 percent of NA sales volume and 36 percent of NA dollar sales — roughly double craft's share of the overall beer market. The fastest growth came in the Mountain Census Division (Arizona, Colorado, Idaho, Montana, New Mexico, Nevada, Utah, Wyoming), where craft NA dollar sales rose 27 percent year over year.

The phenomenon is being driven by a genuine shift in consumer behavior. The BA's 2025 Consumer Survey, conducted by Harris Poll, found that 14 percent of regular craft beer drinkers are consuming less craft specifically because they are drinking more NA. A majority of American adults reported an intention to cut back on alcohol consumption. Within the industry, 40 percent of BA newsletter survey respondents felt that craft NA has genuine staying power [BA, "The State of Non-Alc," 2026](https://www.brewersassociation.org/insights/the-state-of-non-alc/).

This is not a fad. It is a structural change in how Americans think about alcohol — and it may be the craft beer industry's most important adaptation opportunity. The Brewers Association's 2024 Year in Beer report noted that NA beer scan dollars were up 30 percent year-over-year from January through October 2024 [BA, "The 2024 Year in Beer"](https://www.brewersassociation.org/association-news/the-2024-year-in-beer/). The IWSR projects that NA beer will overtake ale as the second-largest beer category worldwide by volume in 2025 [Benzinga, "Non-Alcoholic Beer Sales Up 9% in 2024," 2025](https://www.benzinga.com/personal-finance/management/25/06/45828187/non-alcoholic-beer-sales-up-9-in-2024-experts-predict-it-will-be-the-second-largest-beer-category-worldwide-in-2025).

Meanwhile, the core economic engine of craft beer remains substantial — if slightly smaller than its peak. In 2025, the craft brewing industry contributed $71.8 billion to the U.S. economy and supported more than 415,000 total jobs, with over 189,000 jobs directly at breweries and brewpubs [BA, "Economic Impact of the Craft Brewing Industry," 2025](https://www.brewersassociation.org/statistics-and-data/economic-impact-data/). Top states include California ($8.5 billion), Pennsylvania ($4.9 billion), and Texas ($4.2 billion). Per capita, Vermont leads at $1,052 per adult 21 and older.

But the industry has reached an inflection point. The Brewers Association's 2025 midyear survey distilled it plainly: "Overall, the results of this midyear survey indicate that for many breweries, the challenges they've faced over the past few years have either continued or accelerated" [BA, "2025 Midyear Report"](https://www.brewersassociation.org/association-news/2025-midyear-report/).

Gacioch, looking ahead from the 2025 annual report, offered cautious optimism: "While it's probably premature to declare a 'new normal,' there are many signs we're heading in that direction. What's nearly guaranteed is that future success will depend on creating something meaningful and memorable for consumers" [BA, "A Year of Correction for Craft Beer," 2026](https://www.brewersassociation.org/association-news/a-year-of-correction-for-craft-beer-with-early-signals-of-recovery/).

---

There is a scene that plays out every week in a thousand American towns. A couple walks into a taproom on a Friday evening. The room smells of malt and citrus. A chalkboard lists twelve beers, half of them styles that did not exist in American commerce twenty years ago. The bartender — often the owner — pours a taster of something hazy and aromatic. The couple sips, considers, orders a pint. They stay for an hour, maybe two. They buy a four-pack to take home. They tell a friend about it.

That transaction — direct, local, human — is the unit of survival for craft beer in its next chapter. The era of breakneck expansion is over. What remains is something harder and perhaps more durable: an industry that must earn every pint, one customer at a time. It was built by homebrewers who refused to accept that American beer had to be bland. It will be sustained by the same stubborn conviction.

President Carter, who died in December 2024 at age 100, lived long enough to see what his signature had wrought. The American Homebrewers Association issued a statement the day he passed: "Cheers, and thank you to an American homebrew hero, President Jimmy Carter" [American Homebrewers Association, 2024](https://www.homebrewersassociation.org/news/35th-anniversary-of-homebrew-legalization/). Nearly 10,000 breweries, 415,000 jobs, and a permanent change in the American palate are the tribute — an impressive afterlife for a transportation tax bill signed by a man who barely touched the stuff.
