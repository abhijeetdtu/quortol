# The Last Refuge: How the Pacific Northwest Became a Living Museum of Evolution

*From 2.5-million-year-old rainforests to a marmot found nowhere else, the Pacific Northwest is an evolutionary island unlike anything on the continent — and the data tells us exactly how much is at stake, with a precision that borders on the unkind.*

---

On a talus slope in the Olympic Mountains, at an elevation where the air tastes of granite and snowmelt, a marmot emerges from its burrow and settles onto a sun-warmed boulder. It whistles — a sharp, rising note that carries across the alpine basin as if announcing the mountain to itself. Below it, the forest begins its descent toward the Pacific: first subalpine fir, then mountain hemlock, then the great green cathedral of old-growth western hemlock and Sitka spruce. The marmot can see all of this from its perch. What it cannot see is how remarkable its own existence is — which is, perhaps, the first condition of being remarkable at all.

*Marmota olympus* — the Olympic marmot — is one of twenty-three species found on the Olympic Peninsula and nowhere else on Earth [NPS Endemic Animals](https://www.nps.gov/olym/learn/nature/endemic-animals.htm). It evolved here, on a peninsula isolated by Pleistocene glaciers, its ancestors stranded when the ice retreated roughly 15,000 years ago. In that isolation, something happened: the marmot ceased to be merely a marmot. Its coat lightened. Its call shifted. Its chromosome count diverged. Over thousands of generations, it became a creature that could live nowhere else — a triumph of specialization, and, as evolution would have it, a gamble. [NPS Olympic Marmot](https://www.nps.gov/olym/learn/nature/olympic-marmot.htm)

The Olympic marmot is not alone in its solitude. It shares the peninsula with the Olympic torrent salamander, the Olympic snow mole, the Olympic mudminnow, the Beardslee rainbow trout, the Lake Crescent cutthroat trout, the Olympic pocket gopher, and sixteen other endemic animals and eight endemic plants — species that occur here and nowhere else on the planet. [NPS General Management Plan](https://parkplanning.nps.gov/showFile.cfm?projectID=10233&sfid=24640) The peninsula's isolation has created what biologists call an "evolutionary island" — a place where the normal gene flow between populations was severed by ice, allowing evolution to take its own eccentric course. The result is a living museum of endemism that rivals the Galápagos in its biological distinctiveness, if not in its fame — which, as with most things of genuine value, is no measure of worth.

But the isolation that created these species is now being breached by something more powerful than any glacier, and considerably less patient.

## I. The Architecture of Isolation

The Pacific Northwest's biodiversity is not an accident. It is the product of 2.5 million years of geological and climatic theater — mountain-building, volcanic eruption, glaciation, and the slow choreography of ice sheets advancing and retreating across the landscape.

To understand how this region became a biodiversity refuge, one must first understand the forces that shaped it — and accept that in geology, as in society, the most interesting results come from isolation. The Cascade Range, a chain of volcanic peaks stretching from northern California into British Columbia, acts as a massive weather barrier. Moisture-laden air from the Pacific Ocean rises against the western slopes and drops its load: the Hoh Rainforest receives an average of 140 inches of precipitation per year, making it one of the wettest places in the contiguous United States [NPS Visiting the Hoh](https://www.nps.gov/olym/planyourvisit/visiting-the-hoh.htm). To the east of the Cascades, a rain shadow creates an arid landscape, separating the coastal forest from an inland temperate rainforest by more than 160 kilometers of dry shrubland. [NOAA Climate of Washington](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/data/climate-normals-deprecated/access/clim60/states/Clim_WA_01.pdf)

This separation is the key to the region's evolutionary story. The disjunct inland temperate rainforest of northern Idaho and eastern British Columbia — separated from its coastal counterpart by that dry barrier — is home to populations of western redcedar (*Thuja plicata*) and western hemlock (*Tsuga heterophylla*) that diverged from their coastal relatives approximately 2.5 million years ago, at the dawn of the Pleistocene. [Genomic evidence, Molecular Ecology 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/mec.16431)

This is not a guess. Researchers collected genomic data from both species across their ranges and tested multiple demographic models, using machine learning to infer population history. The results showed that inland populations persisted through multiple glacial cycles in refugia — pockets of suitable habitat in deeply incised river canyons of northern Idaho, known as the Clearwater Refugium — expanding when the climate warmed and contracting when the ice advanced. [PMC9322681](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9322681/)

At least 156 plant and animal species share this coastal-disjunct inland pattern, their populations split by the Cascade rain shadow. For some, like the tailed frog and giant salamander, the divergence happened before the Pleistocene, resulting in separate species that have not interbred for millions of years. For others, like the dusky willow and water vole, the inland populations were founded by recent post-glacial migrants from the coast. [Diversity and Distributions 2009](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1472-4642.2009.00597.x) The pattern that emerges is of a landscape that has been both a refuge and a sieve — preserving ancient lineages while also channeling new dispersers — creating a mosaic of biodiversity that is stratigraphically deep in evolutionary time, which is to say, as deep as any living thing can afford to be.

## II. The Cathedral of Trees

No account of the Pacific Northwest's biological uniqueness can ignore the trees. They are, quite literally, the largest living things in the temperate world — and they have the decency to stand still while being measured.

The coastal temperate rainforest of the Pacific Northwest is the most massive ecosystem on the planet by biomass. According to the World Wildlife Fund, the biomass on the best sites exceeds that of any comparable area in the tropics by a factor of at least four. [WWF Pacific Temperate Rainforests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_temperate_rainforests_(WWF_ecoregion)) This is not an exaggeration — it is a direct consequence of the region's unique combination of abundant rainfall, moderate temperatures, and the rarity of catastrophic fire. In the absence of regular disturbance, living and decaying material accumulates to levels found nowhere else on Earth — a reminder that nature, left to its own devices, does not believe in moderation.

The individual trees themselves approach mythic proportions. Coast Douglas-fir (*Pseudotsuga menziesii*) can live more than 1,000 years; the oldest authenticated specimen stood near Mount Vernon, Washington, and was slightly more than 1,400 years old when it was cut. [USDA Forest Service Silvics Manual](https://dendro.cnre.vt.edu/dendrology/USDAFSSilvics/105.pdf) Western redcedar regularly reaches 800 to 1,000 years; one specimen in Washington measures 592 centimeters in diameter at breast height. [USDA FS Thuja plicata](https://srs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/misc/ag_654/volume_1/thuja/plicata.htm) Western hemlock has been documented at 1,238 years, Pacific silver fir at 900 years, mountain hemlock at 924 years, and yellow cedar at 1,693 years. [WA DNR Old-Growth Guide](https://dnr.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2025-03/lm_hcp_west_oldgrowth_guide_full_lowres.pdf)

These numbers are more than curiosities — though they are that, too, and there is no shame in being a curiosity when the alternative is being forgotten. Each ancient tree represents centuries of undisturbed ecological process — a microhabitat for epiphytic lichens, mosses, and the invertebrates that depend on them. The old-growth forests of the Pacific Northwest host a Survey and Manage list of 298 species (189 fungi, 40 lichens, 36 mollusks, 15 bryophytes, 12 vascular plants, 4 amphibians, and more) that are associated with late-successional and old-growth conditions. [USFS PNW-GTR-966](https://www.fs.usda.gov/pnw/pnw/pubs/pnw_gtr966.pdf) These species are not charismatic megafauna; they are the overlooked infrastructure of the ecosystem — the lichens that fix nitrogen, the fungi that shuttle nutrients through the soil, the slugs that break down organic matter — and they depend on forests that have been left alone for centuries to do their work, a patience the modern world rarely shares.

And yet those ancient forests have been dramatically diminished. The earliest forest inventories in California, Oregon, and Washington, completed between 1933 and 1945, found 32.8 million acres of old-growth on productive forest land — roughly 49 percent of total forest cover. By 1992, that figure had fallen to 10.3 million acres, or just 18 percent. [USFS Old-Growth CA-OR-WA](https://www.fs.usda.gov/r5/rsl/publications/oldgrowth/old-growth-ca-or-wa.pdf) Washington alone went from approximately 8.7 million acres of old-growth to about 2.8 million — a loss of roughly two-thirds in five decades, which is what happens when a resource is treated as a commodity rather than an inheritance.

The Northwest Forest Plan, enacted in 1994, was designed to halt these losses and recover older forests across 24 million acres of federal land. The results have been mixed: maps show a slight 0.3 percent net gain in late-successional forests between 1993 and 2017, driven by forest succession outpacing losses from wildfire and harvest. But losses from wildfire on federal lands between 1993 and 2017 reached 6.2 percent of older forests, while the invasive barred owl has accelerated the decline of the northern spotted owl — the species that became the symbol of the old-growth conflict, and therefore, as symbols will, received more attention than most of what it represented. [USFS PNW-GTR-1004](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/65070)

## III. The Waters That Connect

The forests, for all their grandeur, are only half the story. The Pacific Northwest's biodiversity is fundamentally shaped by the movement of water — from the glaciers to the rivers to the Pacific — and by the species that migrate through all of them, which is nature's way of reminding us that the most interesting things happen at the boundaries.

Olympic National Park protects 12 major river basins, more than 3,500 miles of rivers and streams, and more than 300 high mountain lakes. Within these waters live 29 species of native freshwater fish and more than 70 unique stocks of Pacific salmon and steelhead. [NPS GMP](https://parkplanning.nps.gov/showFile.cfm?projectID=10233&sfid=24640) Salmon, in particular, are the keystone that links the marine and terrestrial ecosystems. They return from the ocean to spawn in the forest streams, dying after reproduction and leaving their nutrient-rich carcasses to be absorbed by the surrounding soil — fertilizing the very trees that shade their spawning grounds, in a cycle so efficient it could pass for design. An estimated 761,682 sockeye salmon returned to the Columbia River in 2024, nearly double the 401,700 forecast. But other runs fared worse: Upper Columbia spring Chinook returned at just 77 percent of forecast. [WDFW Columbia River 2024 Returns](https://wdfw.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2024-12/cr-2024-results-2025-expectations-v122024.pdf)

At the apex of this aquatic food web, the Southern Resident killer whales (*Orcinus orca*) patrol the inland waters of Washington and British Columbia. These orcas are fish-eaters, relying primarily on Chinook salmon — the largest and most oil-rich of the Pacific salmon species. As of the 2024 census, the Southern Resident population stands at just 73 individuals, down from a peak of 99 in 1995. [NOAA 2024 Stock Assessment](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/s3/2026-04/final_2024-mmsar_killer-whale.pdf) The 2025 census improved slightly to 74 whales — 27 in J pod, just 14 in K pod (the lowest since surveys began), and 33 in L pod. [Puget Sound Partnership](https://www.pugetsoundinfo.wa.gov/Indicator/Detail/32)

The population has failed to recover despite being listed as endangered under the Endangered Species Act in 2005 [NOAA Fisheries SRKW](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/west-coast/endangered-species-conservation/southern-resident-killer-whale-orcinus-orca) — a failure that suggests legal protection, however well-intentioned, is not the same as effective protection. A 2021 five-year review found that while some downlisting criteria had been met, the population continues to shrink. Genetic analysis reveals that inbreeding depression is limiting population growth, and without intervention — increasing Chinook availability, reducing vessel noise, lowering PCB contamination — the baseline model predicts "gradual reduction followed by an accelerating decline." [NOAA 5-Year Review 2021](https://media.fisheries.noaa.gov/2022-01/srkw-5-year-review-2021.pdf)

![Southern Resident killer whale population from 1974 to 2025 — peak of 99 in 1995, declining to 73 in 2024.](/api/blog/images/pacific-northwest-biodiversity-refuge_orca_population.png)

The Southern Residents tell a story that echoes across the region's biodiversity: abundance is not permanent. The same waters that once teemed with salmon and echoed with whale song are measurably quieter, the runs thinner, the pods smaller — a diminishment that has the quality of a slowly closing door.

## IV. The Ice That Defines

Above the forests and beyond the rivers, the mountains themselves are changing. Mount Rainier, the most glaciated peak in the contiguous United States, supports 28 named glaciers that cover roughly 30 square miles. These glaciers are the headwaters of five major river systems, their meltwater sustaining salmon habitat, irrigating agriculture, and supplying drinking water to millions — a reminder that even ice, which seems so permanent, is really only water that has agreed to wait. [NPS Mount Rainier Glaciers](https://www.nps.gov/mora/learn/nature/mount-rainier-glaciers.htm)

In 1896, those 28 glaciers covered 129.3 square kilometers. By 2021, that area had fallen to 75.5 square kilometers — a loss of 41.6 percent in 125 years. The volume loss is even steeper: from 7.26 cubic kilometers to 3.52 cubic kilometers, a decline of 51.6 percent. [NPS NRR 2023](https://npshistory.com/publications/mora/nrr-2023-2524.pdf)

The rate of loss, critically, is accelerating. Between 2015 and 2021, glacier area shrank at an average rate of 0.544 square kilometers per year — 2.23 times the rate measured between 2009 and 2015. The south-facing glaciers are being hit hardest: Pyramid Glacier lost 32.9 percent of its area and 42.0 percent of its volume in just six years. Van Trump Glacier lost 33.6 percent of its area and 42.9 percent of its volume. The Stevens Glacier has been officially removed from the inventory — it no longer shows features indicating flow and has been reclassified as a perennial snowfield, which is the bureaucratic equivalent of a demotion. [NPS morageology Glacier Data](https://www.morageology.com/glacier_2021.php)

"Continued losses will quickly lead to the demise of these glaciers in the coming decades," the 2023 NPS report concludes, noting that the south face of the mountain is "rapidly shrinking" and that retreating glaciers are "exposing large areas of loose sediment that can be mobilized to proglacial rivers during rainstorms, outburst floods, and debris flows." [NPS NRR 2023](https://npshistory.com/publications/mora/nrr-2023-2524.pdf)

For species that depend on cold, glacier-fed streams — the bull trout, the Olympic torrent salamander, the tailed frog — this is not an abstract concern. It is a slow-motion habitat collapse, measured in decades and degrees Celsius, which is how the sublime becomes the statistical.

## V. The Refuge in Numbers

What, then, does the Pacific Northwest still protect? The numbers, when taken together, tell a story of extraordinary biological wealth under accumulating pressure — a portfolio of natural capital that no sane investor would deplete.

Olympic National Park was designated a World Heritage Site in 1981. The UNESCO evaluation described it in terms that border on the rhapsodic: "Olympic National Park is the best natural area in the entire Pacific Northwest, with a spectacular coastline, scenic lakes, majestic mountains and glaciers, and magnificent temperate rain forest; these are outstanding examples of on-going evolution and superlative natural phenomena. It is unmatched in the world." [NPS GMP](https://parkplanning.nps.gov/showFile.cfm?projectID=10233&sfid=24640)

The park contains 922,651 acres of three distinct ecosystems — glacier-capped mountains, over 70 miles of wild Pacific coastline, and old-growth temperate rainforest. It provides habitat for more than 1,100 species of native plants, 300 species of birds, and 70 species of mammals. And it protects at least 23 endemic species — a concentration of endemism unmatched by any other national park in the continental United States. [NPS GMP](https://parkplanning.nps.gov/showFile.cfm?projectID=10233&sfid=24640)

In 2024, nearly 3.7 million people visited Olympic National Park, spending $380 million and supporting 2,880 jobs in gateway communities [The Daily World](https://www.thedailyworld.com/2026/01/16/olympic-national-park-had-more-than-3-6m-visitors-in-25/). Mount Rainier drew 2.5 million visitors [NPS MORA Visitation](https://www.nps.gov/mora/learn/management/annual-visitation.htm). North Cascades National Park, the least visited national park in the lower 48 states after Gates of the Arctic, saw just 16,485 — a solitude that is itself a form of preservation, and a reminder that obscurity, too, has its privileges. [NPS 2025 Ranking](https://npshistory.com/publications/visitation/annual-park-ranking/2025.pdf)

Across Washington state, outdoor recreation generated $25.2 billion in spending in 2024, supported 237,000 jobs (5.4 percent of state employment), and contributed $20.5 billion to state GDP. The average wage in recreation-related employment is $42,600 per year; every $1 million of recreation spending sustains nine jobs. [WA RCO 2025 Report](https://rco.wa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/EconomicReportOutdoorRecreation2025.pdf)

These are not marginal figures. The outdoor recreation economy of Washington alone is larger than the GDP of several small nations — and it is entirely dependent on the health of the ecosystems that this article describes, which is to say that the region's prosperity rests on a foundation most people never see.

## VI. What Endures

The Olympic marmot does not know it is endangered. It does not know that its habitat is fragmented, that its population has declined, that the glaciers that shaped its mountain home are retreating at an accelerating rate. It knows only what a marmot has always known: where to find the best alpine sedges, which rocks offer the warmest basking spots, how to whistle a warning when a predator approaches. Ignorance, for the marmot, is a form of happiness.

But the data knows. The U.S. Geological Survey, the National Park Service, the Forest Service, NOAA Fisheries, the Center for Whale Research, and others have been gathering it for decades. The glacier extents have been measured since 1896. The Southern Resident orcas have been counted annually since 1974. The old-growth has been mapped from satellite imagery, its change detected, its loss quantified. The genetic divergence between coastal and inland western redcedar has been read from thousands of loci across the genome. We have, in short, no excuse for ignorance — only for inaction.

The picture these data paint is not one of imminent collapse. It is something more subtle and more instructive: a landscape of extraordinary biological richness, shaped by deep time and geographic isolation, that is being slowly, measurably diminished by the cumulative effect of human activity — climate change, habitat loss, salmon depletion, pollution, noise. It is not a crisis that arrives as a single event, but as a thousand small surrenders.

The Olympic Peninsula's 23 endemic species did not evolve here to be counted and catalogued before they disappear. The 2.5-million-year-old inland temperate rainforest did not persist through the Pleistocene to be logged in a century. The 73 Southern Resident orcas are not a statistic; they are individuals with names — J pod, K pod, L pod — each with known genealogies, life histories, and documented deaths. Data, at its best, is a form of testimony.

What endures, for now, is the opportunity to pay attention. The marmot is still on its boulder, whistling across the alpine basin. The western redcedar still stands in groves that were old when the Magna Carta was signed. The glaciers, though shrinking, still flow. The salmon, though diminished, still return. Nature has a habit of continuing until it cannot.

The question — and it is a question that the data alone cannot answer — is whether we will choose to keep it that way. Data can measure a decline, but it cannot reverse one.
