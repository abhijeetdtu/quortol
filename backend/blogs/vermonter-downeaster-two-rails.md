# The Two New England Rails: Vermonter and Downeaster, a Tale of Two Trains

The winter sun has not yet cleared the ridge of Mount Mansfield when the Vermonter's engineer sounds the horn at St. Albans station — a small brick depot at the northern edge of a small city at the northern edge of a small state, as though New England itself were running out of territory. The train — a string of Amfleet cars in Amtrak's silver-and-blue, pulled by a diesel locomotive — has 611 miles ahead of it, a journey that will carry it down the Connecticut River Valley, through the Knowledge Corridor of western Massachusetts, across the Hell Gate Bridge into New York, and south along the Northeast Corridor to Washington, D.C. The 7:00 AM departure is daily, year-round, and typically carries fewer than 200 passengers out of Vermont. By the time it reaches New Haven, it will have crossed three state lines, switched from diesel to electric power, and accumulated enough riders to fill the café car for the run into Manhattan.

Three hundred miles to the east, beneath the gray-metal train shed at Boston's North Station, the Downeaster is boarding for Brunswick, Maine. Its cars are also Amfleet, its locomotive also diesel, but the scene could hardly be more different: commuters with briefcases, students with backpacks heading to the University of New Hampshire at Durham, retirees with tote bags bound for the LL Bean flagship in Freeport, skiers in winter, beachgoers in summer. Five daily round trips. Three hours, twenty minutes end to end. At its quietest, the train is rarely more than half empty; on summer weekends, it sells out. Since December 2001, when the Downeaster rolled out of North Station for the first time — restoring passenger rail to Maine after a 36-year absence — it has carried more than 10.6 million passengers [NNEPRA Statistics](https://www.nnepra.com/reports-stats/statistics/).

Two trains. Two corridors. Two very different trajectories.

## I. The Vermonter: From Sleeping Car to State-Supported Striver

The track the morning Vermonter glides over leaving St. Albans was laid for a different kind of train. From 1924 until 1995, the overnight Montrealer carried passengers from Washington, D.C., to Montreal's Central Station — a joint service operated by five railroads (the Pennsylvania, the New Haven, the Boston & Maine, the Central Vermont, and the Canadian National) whose cooperation remains a feat of infrastructure seldom repeated [Wikipedia — Vermonter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermonter_(train)). The Montrealer had Pullman sleeping cars, a diner, a lounge; passengers boarded in the evening, woke up in another country, and crossed the border without ever leaving their seats.

The Montrealer was suspended from 1987 to 1989 for track repairs and killed outright by Amtrak budget cuts on April 1, 1995. The next day, the daytime Vermonter began operating between St. Albans and Washington — but not to Montreal. Vermont declined to pay the high labor and terminal costs of extending service across the border. For a time, a connecting bus met the train at St. Albans; when Amtrak moved the schedule two hours earlier, requiring a southbound departure before 5:00 AM, ridership plunged. The bus was dropped in 2005 [Wikipedia — Vermonter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermonter_(train)).

For its first two decades, the Vermonter followed a circuitous routing: from Springfield, Massachusetts, it ran east to Palmer on CSX tracks, then executed an awkward backup move onto the New England Central Railroad, reversing direction to head north through Amherst. The detour added an hour. It was the best option available, because the more direct route — the Connecticut River Line, the historic Boston & Maine mainline through Northampton and Greenfield — had fallen into such disrepair that no passenger train could use it.

Massachusetts changed that. Beginning in 2009, the state poured $73 million in federal American Recovery and Reinvestment Act money into rebuilding the 49-mile Connecticut River Line — known officially as the Knowledge Corridor, a name chosen to reflect the 32 colleges and universities along its route [MassDOT Knowledge Corridor](https://web.archive.org/web/20160523230208/www.massdot.state.ma.us/KnowledgeCorridor/index.html), [Trains in the Valley — Connecticut River Main Line](https://trainsinthevalley.org/connecticut-river-main-line/). Track was replaced with continuously welded rail. Signals were upgraded. Stations in Northampton and Greenfield were built or restored. Holyoke received an infill stop in 2015. On December 29, 2014, the Vermonter rolled into Northampton for the first time in decades, and the backup move at Palmer was retired. The reroute saved 25 minutes between Springfield and Brattleboro [Wikipedia — Vermonter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermonter_(train)).

Vermont, meanwhile, invested $70 million of its own, in partnership with Amtrak and the New England Central Railroad, to raise speeds along the route: 59 mph between St. Albans and White River Junction, 79 mph south of that. By October 2012, 190 miles of track had been refurbished — continuous welded rail, new ties and ballast, bridge repairs, grade-crossing improvements [Wikipedia — Vermonter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermonter_(train)).

The investments worked — up to a point. Ridership, which peaked at 99,280 in fiscal year 2019 before collapsing to 18,585 in FY21 during the pandemic, has steadily climbed back. In FY25, the Vermonter carried 115,940 passengers — a 1.2% increase over FY24 and the route's busiest since 2005 [Amtrak FY25 Year-End Ridership](https://media.amtrak.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/FY25-Year-End-Ridership-Fact-Sheet.pdf), [CT Insider](https://www.ctinsider.com/connecticut/article/amtrak-record-ridership-ct-acela-nec-hartford-line-19959476.php).

But the numbers tell a more complicated story. Despite the ridership gains, the Vermonter posted a $3.6 million operating loss in FY25. Total revenue was $9.4 million, of which only $3.3 million came from ticket sales [Wikipedia — Vermonter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermonter_(train)). The route that was once one of Amtrak's best financial performers among state-supported corridors is now one of its most heavily subsidized — a fall from grace that would be tragic were it not so instructive. As analyst Nolan Buonomano noted after FY24: "Despite increasing ridership by 9,820, revenue per passenger dropped $41.26. Fare recovery declined from 63.80% to 26.09%. This is catastrophic for a route which was once one of the best financial performers" [Buonomano, Amtrak's 2024 Ridership and Financials](https://nolanbuonomano.substack.com/p/amtraks-2024-ridership-and-financials).

## II. The Downeaster: The Little Train That Could

The Downeaster's origin story is one of stubborn persistence. Passenger rail service between Boston and Maine ended in 1965, a casualty of the interstate highway system and the decline of the Boston & Maine Railroad. For 36 years, the only way to get from Portland to Boston by train was to visit a museum.

The campaign to restore service began in earnest in the late 1980s, led by a grassroots citizens' organization called TrainRiders Northeast. In 1995, the Maine Legislature created the Northern New England Passenger Rail Authority (NNEPRA), a public transportation authority charged with developing and providing passenger rail service between Maine and Boston [Maine State Rail Plan](https://www.maine.gov/dot/sites/maine.gov.dot/files/docs/ofps/docs/railplan/MaineStateRailPlan.pdf). Negotiations with the freight railroad Guilford Industries (later Pan Am Railways) began in 1996 and promptly stalled over equipment weight and speed limits. A speed limit of 79 mph was approved in 1999. Track improvements were largely complete by 2000. Then Guilford refused to let Amtrak test its tracks above 59 mph — a final obstacle that captures, in miniature, the full absurdity of American passenger rail.

The Downeaster made its first run on December 15, 2001 — more than a decade after planning began [Wikipedia — Downeaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downeaster_(train)). It was immediately popular. In its first full fiscal year (2003), it carried 223,287 passengers. By FY2014, that number had more than doubled to 520,790. By FY2019, it reached 574,404 — a record at the time [NNEPRA Service Development Plan](https://nnepra.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/062816-Final-Draft-Downeaster-SDP-Summary.pdf).

The Downeaster's growth rests on a series of carefully sequenced infrastructure investments. In November 2012, service was extended 29 miles north from Portland to Brunswick, funded by a $35 million federal stimulus grant and state matching funds [Amtrak Maine Fact Sheet](https://www.amtrak.com/content/dam/projects/dotcom/english/public/documents/corporate/statefactsheets/MAINE17.pdf). Two round trips reached Brunswick initially. In November 2016, NNEPRA opened a $13 million, 60,000-square-foot indoor train layover facility in Brunswick — long enough to shelter three train sets overnight, ending the need for dead-head moves to Portland and enabling a third Brunswick round trip. The Royal Junction Siding project — a $9.3 million passing track completed in 2018 — allowed all five daily round trips to reach Brunswick [Press Herald, Aug 2016](https://www.pressherald.com/2016/08/17/rail-project-in-falmouth-and-cumberland-will-allow-downeaster-to-expand-service/). The Wells Area Improvement Project — a $16.9 million CRISI grant for a six-mile passing siding and new station platform — added capacity for a sixth daily round trip between Brunswick and Wells [Maine State Rail Plan](https://www.maine.gov/dot/sites/maine.gov.dot/files/docs/ofps/docs/railplan/MaineStateRailPlan.pdf).

The result: in FY2024, the Downeaster carried a record 598,426 passengers — 4% above the 2019 record and 27% above the prior year [NNEPRA Statistics](https://www.nnepra.com/reports-stats/statistics/). Ticket revenue topped $13 million, up from $10.4 million the year before [Wikipedia — Downeaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downeaster_(train)). The farebox recovery ratio reached 50%.

FY2025 saw a modest pullback — 549,120 passengers, down 7.2% — attributed to service disruptions from a major tie-replacement project and construction on the MBTA-owned portion of the line. But the financial fundamentals held. Total expenses were $27 million, $3.1 million below budget. Cost recovery reached 55%, exceeding the 50% target. The café achieved 73% cost recovery. Customer satisfaction scores remained high [NNEPRA Board Packet, Oct 2025](https://www.nnepra.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/2025-1009-Board-Packet-Final.pdf).

"Despite challenges due to construction, ridership and revenue remained strong," NNEPRA Executive Director Patricia Quinn told the board in October 2025. "July performance reached 95% of the best year on record."

## III. The Geography of Success

Why has one route flourished while the other struggles for financial footing? The usual answer — geography — is partly right, but only partly.

The Downeaster is a dense, short-haul corridor: 145 miles, 12 stations, an average trip of 44 miles per passenger. More than half of all Downeaster trips start or end in Maine. The top city pair — Portland to Boston, 116 miles — accounts for a significant share of ridership. This is the ideal distance for a train: too far to drive comfortably for a day trip in heavy traffic, but short enough that the train wins on time [Rail Passengers Association Amtrak Fact Sheet](https://www.railpassengers.org/site/assets/files/3476/9.pdf). The train competes directly with Interstate 95, one of the most congested highways in New England, and offers a three-hour-and-twenty-minute ride that is often quicker than driving during peak periods.

The Vermonter, by contrast, is a 611-mile route whose average passenger travels a considerably longer distance. Its top Vermont city pair in FY25 was St. Albans to Washington — roughly 500 miles. The train serves small towns along the Connecticut River Valley — Brattleboro, Bellows Falls, White River Junction, Randolph, Montpelier — where the population base is thin and the stations are far apart. It shares tracks with freight trains that have priority, and its on-time performance reflects this arrangement: the southbound Vermonter departed Northampton on schedule only 3 days out of 353 in 2024, with a median delay of 12 minutes [Trains in the Valley — Vermonter OTP](https://trainsinthevalley.org/data/). In November 2025, the Vermont Rail Advisory Council discussed four long-term slow-order spots on the New England Central Railroad that need repairs — deferred maintenance that the railroad has said will not be addressed until summer 2026.

But geography is not destiny — a truth that infrastructure policy seems to rediscover every decade or so, and then promptly forget. The Vermonter's underlying ridership trend is upward. The Knowledge Corridor reroute proved that strategic infrastructure investment can unlock demand; ridership at the new stations in Northampton and Greenfield grew 84% in the first month after the route change [Wikipedia — Vermonter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermonter_(train)). The Downeaster's Brunswick extension showed the same pattern writ large: 29 miles of new track, $35 million in investment, and a corridor that now carries 598,000 passengers a year.

The difference may owe less to the trains themselves than to the political ecosystems that sustain them. NNEPRA is a focused, single-mission authority: a board appointed by the governor and approved by the legislature, a dedicated funding stream from state and federal formula funds, and the ability to plan and execute capital projects over multi-year horizons. The Vermonter, by contrast, depends on annual appropriations from three separate states — Vermont, Massachusetts, and Connecticut — each with its own transportation priorities and budget cycles. Connecticut alone contributes $10.6 million annually [CT Insider](https://www.ctinsider.com/connecticut/article/amtrak-record-ridership-ct-acela-nec-hartford-line-19959476.php). The coordination required to sustain a three-state partnership is a diplomatic achievement in its own right — but it is not the same as having a dedicated, empowered authority at the helm.

## IV. The Road Ahead: Montreal, Bangor, and the Limits of Ambition

Both routes have ambitious expansion plans. Both face barriers that have less to do with engineering than with politics, money, and bureaucratic alignment — which is to say, they face obstacles entirely of human making, and therefore the most difficult to remove.

### The Vermonter's Long Road to Montreal

Thirty miles. That is all that separates the Vermonter's northern terminus in St. Albans, Vermont, from Montreal's Central Station — 30 miles of existing freight rail track, owned by Canadian National, that has already been upgraded to passenger standards with a $7.9 million federal grant completed in 2014 [Wikipedia — Vermonter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermonter_(train)). The distance is laughably short. The obstacles are absurdly tall. And the gap between the two tells you almost everything about the state of American infrastructure ambition.

The fundamental challenge is customs. To run a train across the U.S.-Canada border without stopping every passenger at the frontier for inspection, both countries must agree on a pre-clearance facility — a combined U.S. Customs and Border Protection and Canada Border Services Agency processing center at Montreal's Central Station where passengers would be screened before boarding, then free to exit the train at any stop without further inspection. The legal framework exists: the Agreement on Land, Rail, Marine, and Air Transport Preclearance was signed by the U.S. and Canada in March 2015. The U.S. enabling legislation passed in December 2016 as the Promoting Travel, Commerce, and National Security Act [Wikipedia — Vermonter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermonter_(train)). So the law is ready. The politics are willing. The facility is not.

What does not exist is a built facility, an engineering design, or a clear timeline. As of early 2026, the project has entered a one-year "Opportunity and Design Analysis" — a study to evaluate building conditions, seismic requirements, and the conversion of Track 23 at Central Station. The analysis is scheduled for completion in mid-2026 [Trains in the Valley — Vermonter Extension](https://trainsinthevalley.org/vermonter-extension-to-montreal/). But the analysis itself is only a study of a plan for a design of a facility that has been under discussion for more than a decade. It is the kind of bureaucratic recursion whose structure mirrors its subject: a thing that points to another thing that points to another thing, and none of them is the thing itself.

"You're gonna ask me what the timeline is, and I'm gonna say, I don't know," Dan Delabruere, VTrans Rail Bureau Director, told the Community News Service in 2025 [Community News Service](https://vtcommunitynews.org/2025/08/22/passenger-trains-to-montreal-are-in-the-works-what-does-it-mean-for-vermonters/).

Amtrak sought $30 million in its FY2025 grant request for cross-border service improvements, including the design of the pre-clearance facility and rail infrastructure upgrades within Quebec [Trains in the Valley — Vermonter Extension](https://trainsinthevalley.org/vermonter-extension-to-montreal/). The Quebec government allocated CA$1 million in its 2022-2023 budget for a feasibility study. Public Safety Canada, which oversees the border agency, said in 2024 that it "continues to be supportive of pre-clearance expansion in Canada — provided that all the conditions associated with establishing and maintaining pre-clearance operations are met" [St. Albans Messenger](https://www.samessenger.com/news/train-sportation-north-checking-in-on-amtrak-s-vermonter-connection-to-montreal/article_e7f3568e-8581-45de-8bc8-f639382900d5.html).

The market case is strong. Montreal's metro area has 1.8 million people. A restored rail connection would link Vermont's tourism economy — already worth $3 billion annually — to a major international urban center without requiring visitors to rent cars at Burlington airport. Higher-speed upgrades on the Vermont side, which VTrans estimated could save 90 minutes between New Haven and Montreal, were forecast to attract 31,000 to 41,000 additional riders per year by 2040 [Wikipedia — Vermonter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermonter_(train)). The economic logic is clear. The political will is, at best, intermittent — which is to say, entirely consistent with the history of American passenger rail.

### The Downeaster's Northern Vision

Maine's ambitions run in the opposite direction: north and east, deeper into the state's interior and coast.

The most concrete near-term prospect is the Rockland Branch Coastal Connection pilot. NNEPRA, in partnership with MaineDOT and Midcoast Rail, is planning a seasonal passenger service extending from Brunswick 56 miles east to Rockland, with stops in Bath (home to Bath Iron Works, Maine's largest employer), Wiscasset, Newcastle, and Waldoboro [Downeaster Corridor Application](https://www.nnepra.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Downeaster-Corridor-Narrative-Application.pdf). The pilot would use self-propelled rail cars and operate three daily round trips. The route follows the Maine Eastern Railroad's Excursion Train route, which already operates seasonal service between Brunswick and Rockland. The track is state-owned. The stations exist in various states of readiness. The question — as it always is — is whether demand justifies the roughly $100 to $200 million in signal upgrades and bridge replacements that full regular service would require.

The larger prize is Bangor. In May 2025, the Rail Passengers Association released a study commissioned by the Maine Rail Group and TrainRiders Northeast projecting that a twice-daily extension of the Downeaster from Portland to Bangor and Orono — with intermediate stops in Lewiston, Winthrop, Waterville, and Pittsfield — would generate $61.7 million in annual economic benefits for the state of Maine [Rail Passengers Association, May 2025](https://www.railpassengers.org/happening-now/news/releases/extending-amtrak-downeaster-to-bangor-would-deliver-61.7-million-in-annual-benefits-statewide/). The study projected 260,000 new riders in the first year, $15.5 million in new visitor and traveler spending supporting 191 new jobs, $12 million in annual savings to passengers over other modes, and up to 5.9 million vehicle miles removed from Maine roads annually.

The proposal has encountered political headwinds. A bill directing NNEPRA to apply for federal funding for a Bangor feasibility study failed in the Maine Legislature's transportation committee in April 2025, opposed by both NNEPRA and the Maine Department of Transportation on grounds of cost and projected ridership [Central Maine, Apr 2025](https://www.centralmaine.com/2025/04/24/proposal-for-passenger-rail-to-bangor-loses-steam-in-committee/). Nate Moulton, director of freight operations at MaineDOT, estimated that track and equipment improvements would cost $375 million to $538 million, and noted that the existing Downeaster service covers only about 50% of its costs with passenger fares. The study commissioned by advocates, supporters counter, shows a very different picture — and the gap between official skepticism and advocacy optimism is precisely the kind of disagreement that further study is designed to resolve.

Other less expensive upgrades are already in motion. The $50 million Positive Train Control installation on the CSX-owned segment between Haverhill, Massachusetts, and Brunswick — funded by Amtrak, built by CSX — is expected to be complete in 2026 [Wikipedia — Downeaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downeaster_(train)). PTC will remove the frequency cap that currently limits the Downeaster to six daily round trips and potentially permit higher speeds. The Portland station relocation — moving from the current Thompson's Point location on a branch line to a mainline site on St. John Street — has an estimated cost of $30-$50 million and would eliminate a 15-minute reverse move that currently adds time and complexity to every trip [Maine State Rail Plan](https://www.maine.gov/dot/sites/maine.gov.dot/files/docs/ofps/docs/railplan/MaineStateRailPlan.pdf). A new West Falmouth station at Exit 53 of I-95 is in the planning stages. And after years of negotiation, the CSX acquisition of Pan Am Railways in June 2022 brought the Downeaster's host railroad under the ownership of North America's third-largest freight carrier, which has promised significant infrastructure investment along the route.

## V. The Common Thread

What the Vermonter and Downeaster share is more interesting than what divides them — which is fortunate, because what divides them could fill a rather long policy paper. Both trains are expressions of the same American infrastructure bargain: a federal passenger railroad (Amtrak) operating over tracks owned by private freight companies, with operating costs shared by state governments that must justify the expense to legislatures and taxpayers. Both serve regions that lost passenger rail in the mid-20th century and fought to get it back. Both have seen ridership rebound from the pandemic to historic levels. Both face futures constrained by decisions made — or not made — in state capitols, corporate boardrooms, and, in the Vermonter's case, a foreign capital.

The Downeaster has been more successful, by almost any measure, because it has been more fortunate in its circumstances: a shorter route, denser population centers, a single-minded governing authority, and a freight railroad that — after a painful start — evolved from obstacle into partner. The Vermonter has had to navigate a more complex geography, a thinner revenue base, and a tri-state funding structure that makes long-term planning a perpetual negotiation — which is to say, a permanent exercise in managed disappointment.

But the gap between them is not fixed. The same infrastructure investments that transformed the Downeaster from a plucky political compromise into a regional transportation asset are available to Vermont, Massachusetts, and Connecticut — provided they can sustain the political will. The Knowledge Corridor showed what is possible when federal stimulus money meets state determination. The question now is whether the same formula can work for the 30 miles to Montreal, and whether the lessons of one success can be exported to a corridor still waiting for its own — or whether, as is so often the case in American infrastructure, the answer will be almost, but not quite.
