# The Necessity Puzzle: Why Every Creature That Sleeps Risks Everything for Rest

![A jellyfish of the genus Cassiopea pulses slowly on the seafloor at night. It has no brain. It still sleeps.](/api/blog/images/sleep_cassiopea_jellyfish.jpg)

On a tropical seafloor, the upside-down jellyfish *Cassiopea* spends its life reclining on its bell, tentacles waving upward like a crown that rules nothing. It has no brain — no central nervous system at all, merely a diffuse nerve net. It cannot plan, remember, or dream. And yet, when night falls over the coral reefs it inhabits, *Cassiopea* performs an act that science once considered impossible: it goes to sleep.

Its pulsing rate drops by a third, from about 58 contractions per minute to 39. Lift the jellyfish from the seafloor at night, pull away its platform, and the animal drifts in the water column for up to five seconds before it "wakes" and swims to safety. During the day, the same jellyfish rights itself in under a second. When scientists from Caltech kept *Cassiopea* awake by squirting jets of water at it every 20 minutes, the jellyfish was 17 percent less active the following day — sluggish, sleepy, unmistakably in need of rest it had been denied [Nath et al., 2017, *Current Biology*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2017.08.014).

If a brainless jellyfish sleeps, then sleep is no luxury of the cerebral cortex. It is not a byproduct of complex cognition, nor a quirk of the mammalian line. Sleep appears to be a property of neurons themselves — perhaps of life itself. And that raises a puzzle so fundamental that scientists still cannot agree on the answer: why would any organism evolve to spend a third of its existence in a state of helpless vulnerability, unable to eat, mate, or defend itself, unless the return on that investment justified every danger it incurred?

## The Great Sleep Gap

The first lesson of comparative sleep science is that there is no normal amount of sleep. The "average" adult mammal sleeps 11.7 hours per day, according to the Phylogeny of Sleep Database — a compendium of 178 studies covering 127 species assembled by researchers at Boston University and the Max Planck Institute. But that average conceals a range almost grotesque in its spread [McNamara et al., 2008, *The Open Sleep Journal*](https://benthamopen.com/contents/pdf/TOSLPJ/TOSLPJ-1-11.pdf).

The donkey sleeps three hours. The armadillo sleeps twenty. The big brown bat packs nineteen hours of daily sleep, while the giraffe gets by on four and a half. The horse's sleep cycle — the time it takes to move from NREM sleep through REM and back again — runs sixty minutes. The chinchilla's runs six. These differences do not obey evolutionary order: primates as a group possess no characteristic sleep signature to distinguish them from rodents or insectivores. Within the same order, closely related species can spend wildly different amounts of time asleep [Siegel, 2005, *Nature*](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature04285).

![Total daily sleep time across 71 mammal species, with carnivores, herbivores, and omnivores plotted separately. Humans cluster at the low end, alongside other large-bodied omnivores.](/api/blog/images/sleep_mammal_hours.png)

For decades, sleep researchers sought to explain this variation by tying it to some functional purpose. Bigger brains demand more REM sleep, the theory ran — time to process information and consolidate memories. Higher metabolic rates require more sleep for energy conservation. The altricial young of certain species need extra REM for brain development. These explanations possessed a satisfying logic: sleep must serve a purpose commensurate with the organism's needs.

But when Isabella Capellini and her colleagues at the University of Bristol applied rigorous phylogenetic methods to the largest mammalian sleep dataset ever assembled — controlling for laboratory conditions and evolutionary history — the traditional correlations collapsed. Brain size did not predict REM sleep duration. Basal metabolic rate correlated *negatively* with sleep time, not positively. The only robust predictors of how much a mammal sleeps were ecological: how safe its sleeping site, what it eats, and how much time it must spend foraging [Capellini et al., 2008, *PNAS*](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0712043105) [Lesku et al., 2006, *The American Naturalist*](https://doi.org/10.1086/506973).

The herbivore, forever chewing and digesting, sleeps little. The carnivore, with its rich diet and fewer meals to pursue, sleeps long. The animal that dozes in an exposed burrow sleeps less than the one tucked into a safe tree hollow. Social sleepers — species that bed down together — sleep less than solitary ones, perhaps because more eyes watch for danger. These patterns suggest that sleep time is not primarily determined by how much sleep an animal *needs*, but by how much it can *afford*.

Here, then, is the first paradox at the heart of the necessity puzzle: the animal kingdom's sleep patterns are shaped less by the supposed benefits of sleep than by the costs of staying asleep. If sleep were not essential, evolution would have eliminated it in species that cannot afford it. And yet no species has.

## The Riddle of REM

![Sleep in the platypus: REM sleep occurs with a moderate-voltage EEG — a primitive feature linking monotremes to the earliest mammals. The platypus spends more time in REM than any animal on Earth.](/api/blog/images/sleep_platypus_rem.jpg)

If total sleep time is puzzling, REM sleep is positively confounding. Our most vivid dreams occur during this state of high brain activation and near-total muscle paralysis. But REM is no human invention, nor even a mammalian one in any straightforward sense. Its distribution across the animal kingdom defeats every simple explanation.

The platypus, a monotreme that diverged from other mammals some 166 million years ago, spends between 5.8 and 8 hours per day in REM sleep — more than any animal ever measured. That is roughly 40 percent of its total sleep time, and it occurs while the platypus's electroencephalogram shows a moderate- to high-voltage pattern, unlike the low-voltage, wake-like EEG that characterizes REM in placental mammals [Siegel et al., 1999, *Neuroscience*](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0306-4522(98)00588-0). The echidna, the platypus's fellow monotreme, occupies an even stranger position: it appears to combine aspects of REM and NREM sleep into a single, undifferentiated state, its brainstem activated while its forebrain generates slow waves. Siegel and his colleagues have argued that this represents the ancestral condition — that the two distinct sleep states we know today did not evolve sequentially, but instead *differentiated* out of a primitive precursor state that held the seeds of both [Siegel et al., 1998, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.1998.0272).

If REM sleep evolved to serve some specific cognitive function — memory consolidation, say, or emotional regulation — then one would expect animals with more complex brains to have more of it. They do not. Birds, which have densely packed, neuron-rich brains and demonstrate sophisticated cognition, have remarkably little REM sleep — often less than 30 minutes per day, in brief bursts averaging under 10 seconds each [Roth et al., 2006, *Journal of Sleep Research*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2006.00559.x). The great frigatebird, during its 10-day foraging flights over the open ocean, sleeps for an average of 42 minutes per day, often with only one cerebral hemisphere at a time. REM sleep episodes in flight are vanishingly rare [Rattenborg et al., 2016, *Nature Communications*](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms12468).

Dolphins and other cetaceans present an even more extreme case. Many researchers believe that dolphins do not experience REM sleep at all — or at least not in any form that resembles the REM sleep of terrestrial mammals. Instead, they engage in unihemispheric slow-wave sleep, one half of the brain at a time, which allows them to swim, surface for air, and remain vigilant continuously. If REM sleep were essential for brain function in mammals, the dolphin — with its large, complex brain — should need it most. Instead, it may have eliminated it entirely [Lyamin et al., 2008, *Sleep Medicine Reviews*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2007.08.006).

Siegel has proposed that REM sleep may have a thermoregulatory function: brain temperature drops during NREM sleep and rises during REM, and REM sleep time across species correlates negatively with body and brain temperature. The monotremes, with lower body temperatures, have more REM. The birds, with the highest body temperatures among endotherms, have the least. Under this view, REM sleep is not primarily about dreaming or memory — it is about warming the brain back up after the deep cool of slow-wave sleep [Siegel, 2005, *Nature*](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature04285).

## The Dreaming Animal

But if REM sleep serves a thermoregulatory function, that cannot be its only role. The evidence that animals dream — that they replay their waking experiences during sleep — is now overwhelming, and it points to a cognitive function that cannot be dismissed as a mere byproduct.

In a landmark 2001 study, Matthew Wilson and his colleagues at MIT recorded the firing of hippocampal place cells in rats as they ran a circular track for food rewards. Each neuron fired when the rat was in a specific location, creating a distinctive sequence of neural activity that mapped the animal's journey. Later, during REM sleep, the same sequences replayed — but at a slower speed, approximately in real time, as if the rat were mentally rerunning the maze. The researchers found that memories of a sequential experience were reactivated during REM sleep, and that the reactivation was robust even 24 hours later, suggesting that REM sleep was involved in the long-term storage of memories [Louie & Wilson, 2001, *Neuron*](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0896-6273(01)00186-6).

A follow-up study, published the next year, found that the same replay occurred during slow-wave sleep as well — but in a very different form. Brief memory sequences corresponding to single laps on the track were replayed in compressed bursts of 100 to 200 milliseconds. A four-second run through the maze was condensed into a neural snapshot. Unlike REM replay, this SWS replay appeared only in the sleep period immediately following the behavior, suggesting it was part of the initial encoding of memory rather than long-term consolidation [Lee & Wilson, 2002, *Neuron*](https://news.mit.edu/2002/dreams).

More recent work has shown just how central this replay is to memory. In a 2024 study published in *Nature*, researchers recorded from the CA1 region of the rat hippocampus during sleep and during periods of sleep deprivation. They found that while sharp-wave ripples — the neural events that carry replay — continued to occur at normal or even elevated rates during sleep deprivation, the actual *content* of the replay was diminished or completely abolished. The brain produced the machinery of memory consolidation, but without sleep, it could not use it [Giri et al., 2024, *Nature*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07538-2).

If rats replay their mazes during sleep, do they dream in the way we do? Wilson, asked this question, answered with characteristic caution: "A lot of things suggest there is an extensive re-experiencing of the awake state during sleep. If we want to call that dreams, I'm perfectly comfortable with that description" [quoted in National Geographic, 2022](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/animals-dream-too-heres-what-we-know).

## The Octopus's Gif

The question of animal dreaming took an unexpected turn in 2021, when Sylvia Medeiros of the Federal University of Rio Grande do Norte published video evidence of sleeping octopuses changing color. *Octopus insularis*, a species found off the coast of Brazil, cycles between two distinct sleep states. In quiet sleep, the octopus is pale and motionless, its pupils narrowed to slits. Then, every 30 to 40 minutes, it transitions to an active sleep phase: its skin darkens, textures shift, colors race across its body, its eyes dart, and its suckers contract. These active bouts last about 40 seconds [Medeiros et al., 2021, *iScience*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2021.102332).

The resemblance to mammalian REM sleep is striking — and deeply puzzling, because octopuses and vertebrates last shared a common ancestor roughly 550 million years ago. Two-stage sleep, with a quiet phase alternating with an active, wake-like phase, appears to have evolved independently in cephalopods and vertebrates. A follow-up study in *Nature* in 2023 recorded direct neural activity from octopus brains during sleep and found that the active phase was accompanied by patterns resembling wakefulness in the superior frontal and vertical lobes — brain regions associated with learning and memory [Pophale et al., 2023, *Nature*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06202-1).

"If they are dreaming, they are dreaming for up to a minute," Sidarta Ribeiro, a co-author of the study, told NPR. "It should be more like small video clips, or even gifs" [NPR, 2021](https://www.npr.org/2021/03/25/980944495/sleeping-octopuses-may-have-dreams-but-theyre-probably-brief).

The independent evolution of REM-like sleep in a lineage separated from our own by half a billion years suggests that the phenomenon is not a contingent evolutionary accident, but an emergent property of complex nervous systems. When a brain reaches a certain threshold of complexity, it may *need* to produce an active sleep state — regardless of the evolutionary path that built it.

## The Human Exception

Among primates, humans are outliers — and not in the direction one might expect. We sleep less than any other primate that has been studied. Using phylogenetic prediction methods that control for body mass, brain size, diet, predation risk, and foraging needs, David Samson and Charles Nunn found that humans ought to sleep about 9.55 hours per day based on our biological characteristics. Instead, we average about 7 hours — the lowest total sleep time recorded among 30 primate species [Nunn & Samson, 2018, *American Journal of Physical Anthropology*](https://doi.org/10.1002/ajpa.23444).

But we are not simply sleeping less. We are sleeping differently. Humans pack a higher proportion of REM sleep into their shorter nights than any other primate. Our REM-to-NREM ratio is about 22:78, compared to 5 to 10 percent REM in many other primates. We achieved this not by increasing REM duration, but by dramatically reducing NREM — especially the lighter stages of sleep. We evolved, in other words, to sleep less but deeper [Samson & Nunn, 2015, *Evolutionary Anthropology*](https://doi.org/10.1002/evan.21464).

Why? The leading hypothesis, which Samson calls the "social sleep hypothesis," proposes that when our early ancestors descended from the trees to sleep on the ground, they faced new threats from predators and hostile conspecifics. The costs of sleep — the time spent vulnerable and unproductive — rose sharply. At the same time, the benefits of wakefulness — learning, social bonding, toolmaking, cultural transmission — became more valuable than ever. The solution was not to eliminate sleep, which remains mandatory, but to compress it: to obtain the same essential rest in less time, by sleeping more intensely [Samson, 2021, *Annual Review of Anthropology*](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/10.1146/annurev-anthro-010220-075523).

The hunter-gatherer populations studied by Samson and his colleagues — the Hadza of Tanzania, the San of Namibia, the Tsimane of Bolivia — sleep 5.7 to 7.1 hours per night. They do not use electric light. They do not set alarms. And yet they sleep less than people in industrial societies, not more. The human sleep pattern is not a modern artifact of smartphones and blue light. It is an ancient adaptation — the signature of a species that traded sleep time for social time, and apparently came out ahead on the deal [Yetish et al., 2015, *Current Biology*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2015.09.046).

## The Ancient Constant

If down-to-earth hominins and soaring frigatebirds and deep-sea octopuses all sleep — if even the brainless jellyfish, pulsing on its ancient seafloor, shows a sleep-like state — then when did sleep begin?

The evidence from *Cassiopea* pushes the origin of sleep back to at least the emergence of neurons, more than 600 million years ago. Cnidarians, the phylum that includes jellyfish, were among the first animals to evolve tissue-level organization and differentiated cell types including neurons. Their nerve nets are diffuse and decentralized, lacking a brain of any kind. Yet they show all the behavioral criteria of sleep: a quiescent state, reduced responsiveness to stimuli, and homeostatic regulation — the "sleep rebound" that follows deprivation [Nath et al., 2017, *Current Biology*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2017.08.014).

The fact that melatonin — the same hormone that promotes sleep in humans — also induces quiescence in *Cassiopea* suggests that the molecular machinery of sleep is even more ancient than the behavioral state itself. The Caltech researchers who made the discovery tested several sleep-promoting compounds on the jellyfish. All of them worked [Caltech News, 2017](https://www.caltech.edu/about/news/surprising-ancient-behavior-jellyfish-79701).

This has led some researchers to propose that sleep's original function was metabolic, not cognitive. The earliest neurons may have needed periods of reduced activity to clear metabolic waste, manage oxidative stress, or conserve energy. Once that cellular function was established, it became a platform on which later evolutionary elaborations — memory consolidation, synaptic pruning, dreaming — could be built [Anafi et al., 2019, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-018-0098-9).

Under this view, the difference between a jellyfish's night of quiet pulsing and a human's vivid, narrative dream is a difference of degree, not of kind. The same fundamental process — a cellular housekeeping function that no nervous system can do without — has been co-opted and elaborated by evolution into the rich internal theater we experience each night.

## The Puzzle Remains

After decades of research and hundreds of studies, sleep science has arrived at a remarkable conclusion: we still do not know, with any certainty, why we sleep. The leading hypotheses are not mutually exclusive, and none explains all the data. Sleep conserves energy, but some animals that need energy conservation most sleep the least. Sleep consolidates memory, but animals with extraordinary cognitive abilities — birds, dolphins — get by on astonishingly little REM sleep. Sleep clears waste from the brain, but we do not know if this is its primary function or a secondary benefit.

What the comparative evidence makes clear is that sleep is not a single thing serving a single purpose. It is a bundle of functions — metabolic, cognitive, thermoregulatory, restorative — woven together over hundreds of millions of years of evolution. Different species, facing different ecological pressures, have emphasized different aspects of the bundle. The platypus dreams for eight hours. The dolphin may not dream at all. The frigatebird catches micro-naps while circling in rising air currents at three thousand meters. The jellyfish slows its pulse on the reef at night.

All of them are solving the same problem in their own way: the necessity of sleep is absolute, but the form it takes is endlessly adaptable. We still do not know what the necessity is — only that no animal that has ever lived has found a way around it.
