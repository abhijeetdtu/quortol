# The Hyperloop Went From Hype to Doom. Here's the Paper Trail.

---

In the Nevada desert, thirty minutes north of Las Vegas, a 500-meter tube of steel stands on concrete pylons, bleached by the sun and silent now. It was called DevLoop, and in its day it was the most expensive promise in transportation. Built in 2016 and 2017 by a company then named Hyperloop One, it was the first full-scale hyperloop test track in the world — a vacuum-sealed pipe in which a pod could, at last, fly. By December 2023, [Bloomberg reported](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2023-12-21/hyperloop-one-to-shut-down-after-raising-millions-to-reinvent-transit) that the company behind it had laid off almost everyone and was selling the tube, the machinery, and everything else to whoever would take it. The only remaining employees were there to liquidate the dream. The dream was worth, at that point, roughly the price of scrap steel.

Ten years and roughly [half a billion dollars](https://techcrunch.com/2023/12/21/hyperloop-one-shutting-down) after it began, the commercial hyperloop industry in the United States and Europe is dead — not slowed, not postponed, but dead. Hyperloop One shut down on December 31, 2023, [having failed to win a single contract](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/high-speed-transportation-firm-hyperloop-one-shut-down-bloomberg-news-2023-12-21) to build anything. The Dutch firm Hardt Hyperloop, recipient of a [€15 million investment from the European Commission](https://bits-chips.com/article/ec-accelerates-hardt-hyperloop-with-e15m-investment/), was [declared bankrupt](https://bits-chips.com/article/hardt-hyperloop-files-for-bankruptcy) by a court in The Hague in March 2026. Weeks later, Spain's Zeleros — backed by supermarket billionaire Juan Roig, Acciona, and EU investor EIT InnoEnergy — [filed for insolvency](https://www.railwaygazette.com/railtech-archive/2026/04/09/top-spanish-hyperloop-developer-files-for-bankruptcy-assets-set-for-defence-and-energy-use/), and its assets were sold to a defense company for about [one million euros](https://renewablesnow.com/news/spains-amper-buys-failed-battery-hyperloop-firm-zeleros-for-eur-1m-1295316/). Across the entire industry, the fastest speed ever achieved with a passenger aboard is [172 km/h](https://www.nytimes.com/2020/11/08/business/virgin-hyperloop-passenger-test.html) — about the speed of a commuter train, and roughly 15 percent of the speed Elon Musk promised in 2013. The story of the hyperloop is not the story of a technology that failed. It is the story of a sales pitch that succeeded so thoroughly that physics was never consulted.

## The Whitepaper: August 12, 2013

On August 12, 2013, SpaceX and Tesla published Elon Musk's [*Hyperloop Alpha*](https://archive.org/details/hyperloop_alpha) whitepaper. It proposed a sealed, low-pressure tube running from Los Angeles to San Francisco, with aluminum capsules gliding on air bearings at a cruising speed of 760 mph (1,220 km/h), pushed by linear induction motors. Passengers would board 28 at a time, 15 million a year; the trip would take about 35 minutes; and the whole system, Musk asserted, would cost approximately [US$6 billion](https://archive.org/download/hyperloop_alpha/hyperloop_alpha.pdf) for the passenger version — a tenth of the cost of the California high-speed rail project it was designed to humiliate. The design was released open-source, free for anyone to build — a price that, in hindsight, was exactly right.

It was, by design, a brilliant piece of engineering theater. Musk, an aerospace engineer by training, had done enough real work on the concept to make the document feel technical — real equations, real tube diameters, real compressor design. But the cost estimate lasted about three days in the open air. [Critics said no](https://bits.blogs.nytimes.com/2013/08/15/could-the-hyperloop-really-cost-6-billion-critics-say-no/): the $6 billion figure assumed land acquisition costs that California rail planners could only dream of, ignored seismic and permitting realities, and treated a 35-mile tube as a feat of plumbing. The whitepaper itself was candid about its own limits, asking for feedback and noting the design had not been optimized. That caveat was not the part anyone remembered. What the world remembered was the word "hyperloop," the speed, and the feeling that the future had arrived early.

The most important sentence in the document, in hindsight, is a quiet one near the end. Musk wrote that the system would be "[a faster, cheaper alternative to high-speed rail and traditional short-haul aircraft](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170001624/downloads/20170001624.pdf)" — a phrase NASA's own later study would adopt nearly verbatim. NASA's [Conceptual Feasibility Study of the Hyperloop Vehicle](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170001624/downloads/20170001624.pdf), published for AIAA SciTech 2017, built a rigorous multidisciplinary model of pod and tube. Its authors found the physics workable but the economics strained, and concluded that real capital costs would land far above Musk's arithmetic. The US Department of Transportation's Volpe Center reached a similar verdict in its own [Hyperloop Commercial Feasibility Analysis](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/12308) in 2016: no fatal physical flaw, but a long list of unanswered questions about cost, safety, and regulation. The studies did not kill the idea. They merely established that the idea was a research program, not a product — and the industry, unfortunately, was not organized for research.

## The Money: A Decade of Raising, and Raising

The first company to take the whitepaper seriously was Hyperloop One, founded in November 2014 — in a garage, as such things should be — by former SpaceX engineer Brogan BamBrogan and venture capitalist Shervin Pishevar. By January 2015 it had raised [US$9 million](https://www.nextbigfuture.com/2023/12/the-fundings-and-failing-of-hyperloop-one.html) from Sherpa Capital, Formation 8 and Zhen Fund. By May 2016 it had [closed $80 million](https://www.siliconrepublic.com/companies/hyperloop-one-funding-test-las-vegas) and renamed itself from Hyperloop Technologies. In September 2017, an [$85 million round](https://www.vox.com/2017/9/21/16344268/hyperloop-one-raised-85-million-tunnel-700-million-valuation) valued the company at over $700 million, bringing total funding to $245 million in three years. Investors included Khosla Ventures, GE Ventures, 137 Ventures, and — with a certain irony — SNCF, France's national rail operator. The [final documented total](https://forgeglobal.com/hyperloop_one_ipo) was $462 million across eleven rounds, including a $100 million Series D in April 2020 that valued a company with no product, no revenue, and no route at [$423 million](https://forgeglobal.com/hyperloop_one_ipo). In finance, as in romance, the absence of substance is rarely a deterrent; it is merely a detail for the next round to address.

![Hyperloop funding: documented raises](/api/blog/images/hyperloop-hype-to-doom_documented_funding.png)

The money came with a gravitational pull toward spectacle. In October 2017, Richard Branson's Virgin Group invested and renamed the company [Virgin Hyperloop One](https://www.privateequitywire.co.uk/hyperloop-one-closes-series-b-1-round-usd85-million), with Branson as chairman. Dubai's state-owned port operator DP World joined the cap table, and soon owned the largest stake. In February 2018, the company signed a deal with the Indian state of Maharashtra to build a Mumbai–Pune hyperloop that would, it was promised, [cut a three-hour drive to 25 minutes and unlock $55 billion in benefits](https://www.globalconstructionreview.com/india-proceeds-hyperloop-between-mumbai-and-pune/). In October 2018, the killing of Jamal Khashoggi prompted Branson to criticize Saudi Arabia; the Kingdom, which had signed its own hyperloop memorandum, [pulled the project and Branson resigned as chairman](https://techcrunch.com/2018/10/22/richard-branson-steps-down-as-chairman-of-virgin-hyperloop-one/). In February 2022, the company abandoned passenger transport entirely, laid off [111 employees](https://techcrunch.com/2022/02/22/virgin-hyperloop-lays-off-111-staffers-as-it-abandons-plans-for-passenger-transport/) — about half its staff — and pivoted to freight, because, as DP World candidly explained, [cargo faces less regulation than people](https://www.cartoq.com/virgin-abandons-hyperloop-passenger-transit-plans-mumbai-pune-bengaluru-hyperloop-unlikely/). Twenty-two months later, it was gone.

The competitors did not fare better. Hyperloop Transportation Technologies (HyperloopTT), founded in 2013 on a crowdfunding platform and sustained by hundreds of volunteer engineers, [claimed $100 million in commitments by 2016](https://techcrunch.com/2016/12/01/hyperloop-transportation-technologies-claims-more-than-100-million-in-total-investment/), built a test track in Toulouse, and in November 2022 announced a merger with a SPAC at a [$600 million valuation](https://www.reuters.com/markets/deals/hyperlooptt-go-public-via-spac-led-by-former-disney-execs-2022-11-22). The deal [collapsed](https://techcrunch.com/2022/11/22/hyperlooptts-spac-public-debut-may-be-going-nowhere-fast) — the SPAC was itself racing its liquidation deadline — and by March 2024, the company [was struggling to pay salaries and rent](https://fortune.com/2024/03/02/hyperlooptt-elon-musk-italy-rent-salaries/), pivoting to cargo capsules and feasibility studies in Brazil. BamBrogan, expelled from Hyperloop One in a [bitter lawsuit](https://www.theverge.com/2016/7/12/12162422/hyperloop-one-lawsuit-bambrogan-pishevar-lloyd-noose), founded Arrivo in 2017; it [shut down in December 2018](https://www.theverge.com/2018/12/14/18128848/hyperloop-arrivo-furloughs-layoffs-money-trouble), having raised a reported [$10 million](https://www.theverge.com/2018/12/14/18128848/hyperloop-arrivo-furloughs-layoffs-money-trouble) and built nothing. The pattern was consistent: raise, announce a route, hold a test, raise again. The route was the product. The test was the product. The passengers were always next year.

## The Physics: What Actually Got Built

What did a decade of fundraising actually buy? It bought three real test tracks, all of them short, and one memorable ride.

The first was DevLoop, Hyperloop One's 500-meter tube in Nevada. On December 15, 2017, an unmanned pod reached [387 km/h (240 mph)](https://newatlas.com/virgin-hyperloop-one-speed-record/52668/) inside the evacuated cylinder — a genuine engineering achievement, and roughly 30 percent of the whitepaper's promised cruising speed. The company called it a record — which it was, in the way that a stepladder is a staircase — and prepared, per its [own press release](https://www.freightwaves.com/news/2017-12-20-virgin-hyperloop-one-sets-new-speed-records-secures-funding), for commercial agreements in 2018.

![Hyperloop speed: promised vs. delivered](/api/blog/images/hyperloop-hype-to-doom_speed_promise_vs_reality.png)

The second was SpaceX's own competition tube, built for the Hyperloop Pod Competition at its California headquarters. There, in July 2018, the Technical University of Munich's WARR team pushed a pod to [457 km/h (284 mph)](https://www.bbc.com/news/technology-44924796) — a record for the entire industry that stands to this day as the fastest hyperloop pod ever run, a distinction with roughly the prestige of the world's fastest elevator. The pod carried no one. It was not designed to. It was a test sled, and its achievement was, in the most literal sense, academic.

The third and most consequential was the moment the industry touched a human being. On November 8, 2020, at DevLoop, Virgin Hyperloop's XP-2 pod carried two passengers — CTO Josh Giegel and Director of Passenger Experience Sara Luchian — over the 500-meter tube at a top speed of [172 km/h (107 mph)](https://www.nytimes.com/2020/11/08/business/virgin-hyperloop-passenger-test.html), for about 15 seconds. It was the first passenger ride in hyperloop history, and it was slower than the German ICE, slower than the Japanese Shinkansen, slower than a Ford Mustang at full throttle. The company was ecstatic. It had, after all, been 7 years since the whitepaper, and this was the only speed at which a human had ever been hurled through a vacuum tube.

There were no other records, because no other hyperloop company ever ran a full-scale pod at all. HyperloopTT's Toulouse track [carried cargo capsules](https://www.hyperlooptt.com/press_articles/) — the Quintero One — at speeds measured in dozens of km/h. Hardt's European Hyperloop Center in Veendam, a [420-meter facility](https://bits-chips.com/article/hardt-hyperloop-files-for-bankruptcy), demonstrated lane-switching and set modest records before its parent went bankrupt in 2026. And Musk's own follow-up, The Boring Company, quietly abandoned the vacuum-tube concept entirely; its first tunnel, beneath Hawthorne, California, [moved a Tesla at about 40 mph](https://la.curbed.com/2018/12/18/18147366/elon-musk-tunnel-tesla-test-opening-grimes) — 5 percent of the whitepaper's speed, with a human driver at the wheel. The man who invented the hyperloop had, by then, built the most expensive rejection of it in history.

Meanwhile, the feasibility studies kept arriving, and they kept pointing the same direction. NASA's 2017 study found the technology workable but stressed that [tube and pod must be designed as a single coupled system](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20170001624/downloads/20170001624.pdf), driving up cost and complexity; the Volpe Center analysis for the US DOT catalogued [regulatory, safety, and cost hurdles](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/12308) with no precedent in any existing transport mode. A vacuum tube is an airplane fuselage on its side, 600 km long, with every weld under cyclic load and every centimeter under the stress of a full atmosphere of pressure. Airplanes are certified; tubes are not; and no regulatory body on Earth had rules for this, because no one had ever built one.

## The Projects That Vanished

The signature habit of the hyperloop industry was announcing projects, and its signature achievement was never completing any of them. The full list is a geography lesson in the art of the memorandum: Dubai, Russia, the UK, Colorado, Missouri, Ohio, the Netherlands, Finland, Korea, Saudi Arabia. Almost all of them were feasibility studies, MoUs, or "letters of intent" — documents that bind no one to anything but a press release. It is a curious economy in which the memorandum is the only product ever delivered on time.

The most instructive case was India. In February 2018, Maharashtra signed an agreement with Virgin Hyperloop One to build a Pune–Mumbai line that would, per the then-Chief Minister, [create tens of thousands of jobs and make Maharashtra "a global hyperloop supply chain"](https://www.globalconstructionreview.com/india-proceeds-hyperloop-between-mumbai-and-pune/). The state approved the company as "Original Project Proponent" in August 2018. In January 2020, the incoming Deputy Chief Minister, Ajit Pawar, killed it with a sentence of devastating plainness: "[Hyperloop has never been constructed anywhere in the world till now, so let this be tried somewhere else before us. Once it is successful, then we can think of it](https://www.timesnownews.com/india/article/maharashtra-drop-ambitious-pune-mumbai-hyperloop-project-ajit-pawar-says-let-it-be-tried-somewhere-else-firs/541558)." There was no answer to that sentence, and none was attempted. The state government later confirmed the project had no progress and no data.

The freight pivot of 2022 was the industry's final concession. DP World, which by then owned [76 percent of Virgin Hyperloop](https://www.cartoq.com/virgin-abandons-hyperloop-passenger-transit-plans-mumbai-pune-bengaluru-hyperloop-unlikely/), explained the logic plainly: "[focusing on pallets is easier to do – there is less risk for passengers and less of a regulatory process](https://www.cartoq.com/virgin-abandons-hyperloop-passenger-transit-plans-mumbai-pune-bengaluru-hyperloop-unlikely/)." A port operator had bought a transportation miracle and reduced it to a conveyor belt. Even that did not save it. Hyperloop One was shut down on December 31, 2023; its intellectual property was transferred to DP World, and its test track, its machinery, and its patents were [sold off or simply abandoned](https://techcrunch.com/2023/12/21/hyperloop-one-shutting-down).

## The European Epitaph

Europe's hyperloop companies died with their hands cleaner but their outcomes identical. Zeleros, founded in 2016 by Polytechnic University of Valencia students who had competed at SpaceX's pod competition, raised [more than €15 million](https://rollingstockworld.com/economy/another-european-hyperloop-start-up-goes-bankrupt/) from private and public investors, and never got past the design phase. By 2026 its headcount had fallen [from 80 to 26](https://rollingstockworld.com/economy/another-european-hyperloop-start-up-goes-bankrupt/); its attempt to pivot into autonomous rail platforms, port maglev, and batteries attracted no stable funding; and in March 2026 a Valencia court declared the company insolvent. In May, Spanish defense and energy group Amper bought its production unit — electromagnetic launch systems, battery technology, and 18 employees — for [about €1 million](https://renewablesnow.com/news/spains-amper-buys-failed-battery-hyperloop-firm-zeleros-for-eur-1m-1295316/), planning to repurpose the linear motors as a UAV launch system. The hyperloop's last technological asset in Spain now launches military drones — progress of a kind, though not the kind that was promised.

Hardt Hyperloop was the cruelest case, because it was the most legitimate. Founded in 2016 in Delft, backed by a €15 million European Commission investment and investors linked to Booking.com's co-founder, it built the European Hyperloop Center in Veendam — the [continent's only full-scale test facility](https://www.therailagenda.com/p/european-hyperloop-sector-suffers) — and demonstrated working lane-switching. It had institutional support, genuine engineering progress, and a functioning track, and it still [filed for bankruptcy](https://www.hardt.global/press/hardt-group-statement-on-hardt-bv) in March 2026, its statement pledging to "actively explore all opportunities for continuation" even as a trustee took control. If a company with EU funding, real infrastructure, and real tests could not convert technical credibility into a single commercial contract, the problem was not any one company. The problem was that the product did not exist.

## The One Place It Survives

There is exactly one place on Earth where the hyperloop is advancing, and it is a missile company in China. The China Aerospace Science and Industry Corporation (CASIC) — the country's largest manufacturer of strategic and tactical missiles — built a 2 km low-vacuum tube in Yanggao County, Datong, Shanxi Province. In February 2024, its T-Flight maglev vehicle hit [623 km/h (387 mph)](https://railmarket.com/news/technology-innovation/19076-china-s-t-flight-hyperloop-exceeds-600km-h-in-record-breaking-test), surpassing Japan's 603 km/h crewed maglev record and becoming the fastest train of any kind ever tested. The track had to be [flat to within 0.3 mm and circular to within 2 mm](https://railmarket.com/news/technology-innovation/19076-china-s-t-flight-hyperloop-exceeds-600km-h-in-record-breaking-test). CASIC now plans to extend the track to 60 km and push toward [1,000 km/h](https://newatlas.com/transport/tflight-chinese-maglev-train-test-successful), with a stated vision of Beijing–Shanghai in 90 minutes. In December 2025, a separate team at China's National University of Defense Technology [accelerated a one-ton test sled from zero to 700 km/h in about two seconds](https://news.cgtn.com/news/2025-12-25/China-sets-world-record-in-maglev-tech-with-700-km-h-in-2-seconds-1JoGe1RA7T2/p.html) on a 400-meter track — an astonishing physics experiment, and one that would kill every human aboard. Which is to say, an experiment only a missile company could love.

The contrast writes the entire obituary of the Western industry in a single line: CASIC does not need quarterly returns, does not need to reassure investors, and does not need a commercial contract to continue. It is a state enterprise building a national technology platform, funded by the state, for reasons the state has decided are sufficient. The Western startups had to sell the future to survive the present, and the future, unhappily for them, refused to be sold in installments. In 2023, the last year of the American industry, [Reuters noted](https://www.reuters.com/business/autos-transportation/high-speed-transportation-firm-hyperloop-one-shut-down-bloomberg-news-2023-12-21) that Hyperloop One had failed to win any contract to build a working hyperloop. In a decade, across two continents, an industry that raised roughly half a billion dollars of documented capital produced exactly one passenger ride, fifteen seconds long, at 172 km/h.

## The Bottom Line

The hyperloop did not fail because the physics was wrong. The physics was, at every step, the least interesting part of the story — NASA said it could work; the US DOT said it could work; the problem was never the vacuum. The problem was the gap between what was promised and what was sold. The whitepaper promised $6 billion and 760 mph; the industry delivered $462 million in funding, one 500-meter tube, and a passenger ride slower than a Porsche. The market, it turned out, had priced the dream correctly from the start.

The real lesson of the hyperloop is not about tubes or pods or magnets. It is about the difference between a hypothesis and a product, and the terrible ease with which a decade of capital can be spent bridging the two. Every company in the story was selling the same thing — a rendering, a press release, a feasibility study, a 15-second ride — and the gap between those things and a train that someone can actually board was not a gap at all. It was a chasm, and the chasm was the entire business model.

The tube in the Nevada desert is still there, waiting for a buyer who will almost certainly not come. Somewhere above it, a straight line of empty air runs from Los Angeles to San Francisco, exactly where the capsules were supposed to fly. The desert has a way of keeping its appointments. The future, as it turned out, preferred to arrive by other means.
