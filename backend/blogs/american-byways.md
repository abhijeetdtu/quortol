# The Roads You've Never Driven: America's Hidden Byway Empire

---

On a September morning in 1935, more than one hundred men gathered at the North Carolina-Virginia line, where a two-lane dirt track crossed between Lowgap and Sparta. They came from the unemployment rolls of Alleghany County — farmers without farms, millworkers without mills, fathers who had not worked in years. Their first job: clear a two-hundred-foot-wide swath of timber off a mountainside. A diesel-powered shovel that had cost twenty-five thousand dollars sat waiting. The nation was in its sixth year of the Great Depression. The Blue Ridge Parkway was about to begin.

They worked with picks, shovels, and the quiet desperation of men who had been told, over and over, that there was no work for them. They built stone bridges by hand. They drilled holes for blasting charges into granite that had sat untouched since the last ice age. They lived in Civilian Conservation Corps camps — tidy rows of tents organized in military fashion, nicknamed the "tree army." They planted trees to hold the soil, built retaining walls, and carved a road along the crests of the Southern Appalachians where no road had ever been. They were building something they could not yet see: the most visited unit in the entire National Park System, which would one day draw nearly seventeen million visitors a year [Blue Ridge Parkway construction, September 1935 - Mt. Airy News via superscenic.com](https://superscenic.com/omeka/items/show/5); [CCC camp at Black Camp Gap, August 1935 - UNC DocSouth](https://docsouth.unc.edu/blueridgeparkway/content/4650/).

Ninety years later, that road — the Blue Ridge Parkway, a 469-mile ribbon of asphalt that follows the spine of the Appalachian Mountains from Shenandoah National Park to the edge of the Great Smoky Mountains — generates $1.52 billion in visitor spending every year and supports 15,679 jobs across Virginia and North Carolina. This is not an incidental side effect. It is, and always was, the point. The parkway was conceived during the Depression as an economic stimulus — a way to channel federal money into the most impoverished region of the eastern United States, paying unemployed mountaineers to build a road that would, in turn, deliver tourists and their dollars back into the same hollows and coves. It worked so well that the National Park Service now calls it "the most heavily visited unit of the National Park System." It draws more people than Yellowstone, Yosemite, and the Grand Canyon combined [NPS 2024 Visitor Spending Effects report](https://www.nps.gov/nature/customcf/NPS_Data_Visualization/docs/NPS_2024_Visitor_Spending_Effects.pdf); [HAER Blue Ridge Parkway significance statement, Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/nc0478/).

The Blue Ridge Parkway is the crown jewel of a national program so obscure that most Americans have never heard of it, though it touches nearly every state in the union. The National Scenic Byways Program, established by Congress in 1991 under the Intermodal Surface Transportation Efficiency Act, has quietly designated 184 roads across 48 states as "America's Byways" — a collection that includes National Scenic Byways and the elite All-American Roads. The criteria for designation are exacting: a road must possess at least one of six "intrinsic qualities" — archaeological, cultural, historic, natural, recreational, or scenic — and must be nominated by the state or an Indian tribe before it can even be considered for federal recognition. The U.S. Secretary of Transportation personally signs off on each designation [FHWA National Scenic Byways Program](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/hep/scenic_byways/); [FHWA Designations page](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/hep/scenic_byways/designations/).

And yet: ask the average American how many National Scenic Byways exist, and they will guess zero. Ask them to name one, and they might mention Route 66 — which is, indeed, a National Scenic Byway in multiple states. But Route 66 is the exception that proves the rule of invisibility. There are roads in this collection that wind through the most spectacular landscapes on the continent, roads that trace the path of freedom seekers escaping slavery, roads that follow the route of the Lewis and Clark expedition, roads that climb to nearly eleven thousand feet through the Beartooth Highway in Montana and Wyoming — roads that could change the way you see the country, if only you knew they were there.

## The Road You Have Driven

Route 66 is the only byway that needs no introduction — a 2,448-mile highway from Chicago to Santa Monica that John Steinbeck called "the Mother Road" and that Americans have elevated to something approaching mythology. In 2026, it celebrates its centennial, and the occasion has prompted a wave of reexamination. The Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City, of all institutions, published "The Byways Report: The Scenic Route to Rural Prosperity" in February 2026, focused on the 432 miles of Route 66 that cross Oklahoma and the small towns that have rebuilt themselves around the Mother Road's enduring appeal [Kansas City Fed Byways Report, Feb 2026](https://www.kansascityfed.org/community/the-byways-report/).

The report's conclusion is bracing in its practicality: "Tourism is an efficient form of economic development for towns and regions, as it doesn't require building infrastructure such as schools or hospitals to support new residents." You can build a tourism economy on what already exists — a historic motel, a diner that has served hamburgers since 1953, a filling station repurposed as a museum, a roadside attraction of such magnificent oddity that people will drive five hundred miles to photograph it and post it on social media. The Blue Whale of Catoosa, Oklahoma — a concrete whale emerging from a pond, with a slide built into its mouth — has been doing precisely this since 1972 [Kansas City Fed Byways Report photos](https://www.kansascityfed.org/community/the-byways-report/).

But the Kansas City Fed's report is about something more than kitsch. It is about a fundamental insight into American economic geography: the roads built to connect places also create the possibility of staying in them. "When linked along a route — a byway — small towns use what makes them special to enchant visitors and boost prosperity, improving the quality of life for their residents," the report's foreword reads. Among the ten themes it outlines for strengthening byway economies, the first is: "Take an appreciative view of what your town has to offer visitors."

The economic data makes it clear why appreciation matters. The Route 66 Economic Impact Study, conducted by Rutgers University in collaboration with the National Park Service and the World Monuments Fund, found that travelers on the Mother Road spend $132 million per year in the communities along its path — eighty-five percent of them visiting historic places and museums. Those dollars support 2,400 jobs, generate $127 million in gross domestic product, and produce $37 million in tax revenue annually [Route 66 Economic Impact Study, NPS](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/route-66-economic-impact-study.htm); [WMF Synthesis of Findings](https://www.wmf.org/sites/default/files/press_releases/Rt%2066%20Econ%20Impact%20Study.pdf). The restored Route 66-themed motels, restaurants, and gift shops that anchor downtowns in towns like Vinita, Oklahoma and Galena, Kansas are not quaint relics. They are the economy.

## The Road You Should Drive

The Harriet Tubman Underground Railroad Byway is a 125-mile driving tour of forty-five historic sites across Maryland's Eastern Shore, following the landscape where Tubman was born into slavery around 1822 and from which she escaped to freedom. It is a road that runs through the same marshes, farm fields, and tidal creeks that Tubman navigated on her thirteen rescue missions, during which she led approximately seventy enslaved people to freedom — and, by her own account, "never lost a single passenger." The byway connects sites where she was enslaved, where she hid, where she received assistance, and where she found safety. It ends at the Harriet Tubman Underground Railroad Visitor Center, a 10,000-square-foot LEED-certified building that opened in 2017 on seventeen acres of parkland, complete with a meditation garden and nature trails [Scenic America, History of the Harriet Tubman Byway](https://www.scenic.org/2022/06/22/the-history-of-the-harriet-tubman-underground-railroad-byway/); [Harriet Tubman Byway travel guide](https://harriettubmanbyway.org/newhtbw/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/TubmanBywayGuide_2017.pdf).

What makes this byway remarkable is not merely its historical significance — though it is difficult to overstate the power of driving a road that follows the literal path taken by one of the most extraordinary figures in American history. It is that the byway functions simultaneously as a memorial, an educational tool, and an economic engine. A 2016 study by the economic development firm Camoin 310 found that the 251,155 net new visitors drawn to the byway each year contribute $23 million in annual sales to Dorchester and Caroline Counties, supporting 363 jobs and $9.9 million in earnings. A separate analysis from the same study found that Maryland residents who use the byway for recreation derive an additional $187,000 per year in direct use benefit — the value of having a beautiful road to drive [Harriet Tubman Byway Economic Impact Study, via visitdorchester.org](https://visitdorchester.org/media/markets/dor-co/links-information/full-report.pdf).

There is, in other words, a business case for remembering who you are.

The same principle applies to roads with less freighted histories. The Paul Bunyan Scenic Byway in Minnesota generates $21.2 million in economic output and supports 331 jobs. The Lake Country Scenic Byway, also in Minnesota, generates $31.7 million and supports 512 jobs — or $344,000 per mile. The Beartooth Highway in Montana and Wyoming, which reaches nearly 11,000 feet and is open only four months a year, generates more than $50 million in that short season. Florida's twenty-six scenic highways collectively produce $63.1 million in direct economic impact, supporting 1,070 jobs [University of Minnesota Tourism Center, Paul Bunyan and Lake Country Byways study](https://conservancy.umn.edu/items/a5d481ff-afe5-4c43-9149-7350f0060966); [Florida Scenic Highways study, ROSA P](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/54531/dot_54531_DS1.pdf); [Beartooth Highway thesis, University of Montana](https://scholarworks.umt.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2146&context=etd).

None of these figures include the most staggering statistic in the entire byway system: the 184 designated roads are a subset of a much larger phenomenon. The National Park Service units that overlap with or function as scenic byways — the Blue Ridge Parkway, the Natchez Trace Parkway, the George Washington Memorial Parkway — generated a combined $29 billion in visitor spending in 2024, supporting 340,100 jobs across the country. The Blue Ridge Parkway alone accounts for $1.52 billion of that total, making it the second most economically valuable unit in the entire Park Service, behind only the Great Smoky Mountains National Park [NPS 2024 VSE report](https://www.nps.gov/nature/customcf/NPS_Data_Visualization/docs/NPS_2024_Visitor_Spending_Effects.pdf).

Why does virtually no one know this? There is no single answer. Part of it is that the National Scenic Byways Program has never had the marketing budget of the National Park Service, which itself struggles for visibility. Part of it is that roads are so ordinary — so much a part of the background of daily life — that the idea of a "designated" road sounds bureaucratic rather than thrilling. The program's funding has also been erratic: authorized at $74.3 million under ISTEA in 1991, it was cut to $16 million in the 2021 appropriations, then partially restored to $27.5 million by 2024. It is a program that has always done more with less, and that has paid for its efficiency in obscurity [FHWA Reauthorization page](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/hep/scenic_byways/byway_quality/projects/reauthorization_phases.cfm); [FHWA NSBP page](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/hep/scenic_byways/).

## The Roads That Almost Were

The idea of a national scenic highway system did not spring from nowhere in 1991. Its roots reach back to the earliest days of the automobile. In 1922, the Bronx River Parkway opened in Westchester County, New York, the first road in America designed not for speed or commerce but for pleasure — a winding, landscaped drive with moderate speeds, intended to let motorists enjoy the scenery. It was followed in 1932 by the Mount Vernon Memorial Highway, linking Washington, D.C. to George Washington's estate, the first federally built scenic parkway. In 1935, the Blue Ridge Parkway began its fifty-two-year construction odyssey, a New Deal project that would employ thousands of men through the Great Depression [FHWA History of Scenic Road Programs](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/infrastructure/scenichistory.cfm).

President John F. Kennedy established the Recreation Advisory Council in 1962, which recommended the development of a national program of scenic roads and parkways. The resulting 1966 interagency study, "A Proposed Program for Scenic Roads and Parkways," remains the most exhaustive review of the subject ever conducted. It cited five reasons for a national scenic road program: driving for pleasure was one of America's most popular recreational pursuits; automobile ownership and leisure time were increasing; urban growth was consuming open space; tourism generated substantial economic benefits; and road planning and design could produce aesthetic and recreational gains. The study recommended a minimum program and an expanded program, with financing, administration, and planning mechanisms [FHWA History of Scenic Road Programs](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/infrastructure/scenichistory.cfm).

It took another twenty-five years for Congress to act.

When ISTEA finally passed in 1991, it created the National Scenic Byways Program as a "grass-roots collaborative effort" to "recognize, preserve, and enhance selected roads throughout the United States." The program's structure was deliberately bottom-up: roads are nominated by states or Indian tribes, not by the federal government. Local communities must develop corridor management plans that spell out how they will preserve the road's intrinsic qualities while promoting tourism. The federal role is limited to designation and grant funding — a light touch that has proved remarkably durable [FHWA US Code page](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/hep/scenic_byways/us_code.cfm); [Interim Policy, Federal Register 1995](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1995-05-18/html/95-12211.htm).

## The Map

What does the collection look like? It is stranger and more wonderful than you might imagine.

You can drive the Selma to Montgomery March Byway in Alabama, which traces the route of the 1965 voting rights march — a road that is not merely historic but actively didactic, teaching the geography of the civil rights movement mile by mile. You can drive the San Juan Skyway in Colorado, a 236-mile loop through the San Juan Mountains that passes through the old mining towns of Ouray, Silverton, and Telluride, offering views of fourteen-thousand-foot peaks and the remnants of a silver boom that ended 130 years ago. You can drive the Kancamagus Scenic Byway in New Hampshire through the White Mountain National Forest, a 34.5-mile ribbon of pavement that is so spectacular in autumn that traffic jams form at overlooks, and so remote that there are no gas stations, restaurants, or lodges along its entire length — by design, to preserve what the Abenaki called the "fearless place" [FHWA complete list of America's Byways, 2021](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/hep/scenic_byways/designations/designated_byways.cfm).

You can drive the Florida Keys Scenic Highway, an All-American Road that crosses forty-two bridges over 113 miles of open ocean. You can drive Utah's Scenic Byway 12, which the Federal Highway Administration has called "a journey through time," winding through red rock canyons, alpine forests, and the Grand Staircase-Escalante National Monument. You can drive the Trail of the Ancients in Colorado and Utah, which follows the archaeological traces of the Ancestral Puebloans — a road that requires you to see not just the landscape but the civilization that lived in it a thousand years before your own. You can drive the Journey Through Hallowed Ground in Maryland, Pennsylvania, and Virginia, which passes through more historic sites per mile than any other road in America, including Gettysburg, Harper's Ferry, and Monticello.

In 2021, the last round of designations, the U.S. Department of Transportation added forty-nine new roads to the collection — fifteen All-American Roads and thirty-four National Scenic Byways in twenty-eight states. Among the new designations: the Harriet Tubman Underground Railroad Byway was elevated to All-American Road status. The St. John Valley Cultural Byway in Maine, which traces the border with Canada through Acadian communities that have preserved their French language and culture since the eighteenth century, was added as a National Scenic Byway. The Sandhills Journey Scenic Byway in Nebraska, which runs through the largest sand dune formation in the Western Hemisphere — grass-stabilized dunes that stretch for 20,000 square miles — was also added. The Battle Road Scenic Byway in Massachusetts, which follows the route of the British retreat from Concord on April 19, 1775, was designated an All-American Road [FHWA 2021 Designations booklet](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/hep/scenic_byways/designations/); [2021 Designated Byways list](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/hep/scenic_byways/designations/2021_designated_byways.cfm).

## The Mother Road at One Hundred

In 2026, Route 66 turns one hundred years old. It will be celebrated, photographed, and driven by thousands of people who will post photographs of the Wigwam Motel in Holbrook, Arizona, of the Blue Whale in Catoosa, Oklahoma, of the neon sign of the Hi-Way Cafe in Vinita that was relit in a ceremony in 2023. It will be the most famous road in America for a year, and then it will settle back into its accustomed role as the most famous road that very few people actually drive — at least, not end to end.

But the centennial is an occasion worth marking for a reason that goes beyond nostalgia. Route 66 is the road that taught America what scenic byways could be. It demonstrated, over the course of a century, that a road could be more than a conduit for commerce and commuting — that it could be a destination in itself, a place where the journey matters as much as the arrival. The National Scenic Byways Program took that lesson and multiplied it across the continent, creating a network of roads that preserve the best of what America has been, even as the country hurtles toward whatever it is becoming.

The question is whether Americans will discover them in time. The roads are there — 184 of them, in 48 states, waiting. The archives and studies are full of numbers proving their value. What is missing is not infrastructure or funding, but attention. We drive past them every day, on our way to somewhere else, not knowing that we are already there.
