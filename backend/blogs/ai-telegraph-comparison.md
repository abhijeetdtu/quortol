# The Biggest Lie About AI (And What the Telegraph Gets Right)

![Telegraph growth over time](/api/blog/images/telegraph_growth_mileage.png)

## "What God Hath Wrought."

Six years later, twelve thousand miles of wire connected much of the eastern United States.

Today, we watch artificial intelligence unfold with a speed that would have made Morse's contemporaries dizzy. Two hundred and fifty-two billion dollars invested globally in 2024. A Tracxn estimate reported by Exploding Topics identified approximately 33,089 companies doing AI as of October 2025. The technology moved from research papers to boardrooms in a single decade instead of half a century.

The telegraph/AI comparison has become a genre. Every investor writes it. Every journalist publishes it. They are both infrastructure revolutions. Both followed similar price curves. Both developed strong concentration dynamics. Both disrupted entire industries.

The problem is that they're mostly wrong.

The real story isn't that AI is like the telegraph. The real story is that AI is something the telegraph couldn't have imagined: an information technology that doesn't transmit information but generates it, and whose economics rest on a model the telegraph never needed — one where better performance demands more, not less, spending.

Morse's first transmission in 1844 used an experimental line funded by a $30,000 congressional appropriation — not a paying commercial message. It took years for the telegraph to develop a commercial fee-per-message model. AI has found demand — customers are paying billions — but OpenAI has not demonstrated that its revenue can produce durable profitability after compute, research and capital costs. And that difference — small, almost technical on the surface — explains everything about why AI is heading toward a crisis the telegraph never faced.

---

## The First Decade: Numbers That Look Identical

The telegraph grew from one wire to twelve thousand miles of connected lines in the first six years — and that was only the eastern United States. The United States had 75 telegraph companies in 1851, running 21,147 miles of wire, according to the U.S. Bureau of the Census. In 1846, wheat prices in Buffalo lagged four days behind New York City. In 1848, the two cities were linked telegraphically, and prices synchronized overnight.

![AI funding by year](/api/blog/images/ai_funding_by_year.png)

AI grew from a research curiosity to a global industry in half the time. According to the Stanford AI Index Report, global corporate investment in AI reached $252 billion in 2024. The technology moved from conference papers to commercial products faster than any infrastructure revolution in history.

The price curves are similar in direction, if not in scale. Telegrams cost $1.55 per message in 1850. By 1890, they cost forty cents — a seventy-four percent reduction over forty years. The cost of AI inference has fallen more than 280-fold between November 2022 and October 2024 at GPT-3.5-level performance, according to Stanford's AI Index Report. Both technologies followed the same trajectory: expensive and rare at first, then cheap and ubiquitous.

![AI startups and acquisitions](/api/blog/images/ai_startups_acquisitions.png)

Both developed strong concentration dynamics. The telegraph went from 75 companies in 1851 to Western Union's 90% market dominance by 1866. Today's AI industry — with approximately 33,089 companies as of October 2025 — is following the same path toward concentration, not through mergers but through infrastructure dependency. The funding charts show the same explosive curve.

The numbers line up. The parallels are seductive. The story seems clear.

There's just one problem.

## The Lie: Profit

Commercial telegraph services developed a straightforward fee-per-message revenue model relatively early. Western Union earned roughly thirty to forty cents of every revenue dollar as net profit at the height of its dominance.[1] Customers sent messages. Western Union collected fees. That was the business.

AI has found demand without durable profitability for every player. OpenAI reported a $20.9 billion operating loss on $13.1 billion in revenue.[2] Its $38.5 billion attributable net loss was further affected by a large noncash fair-value charge and accounting offsets associated with its restructuring.[2] Microsoft alone received $17.2 billion from OpenAI that year for compute infrastructure, exceeding OpenAI's total annual revenue. Anthropic's cash burn was approximately $5.6 billion in 2024, according to The Information.[3] The gap between revenue and valuation is significant: OpenAI is worth $852 billion and Anthropic $965 billion.[4] Microsoft, however, already derives substantial revenue from AI-related cloud and software services, while Anthropic has said it expects its first quarterly operating profit in the second quarter of 2026.

The cost escalation is structural, not cyclical. A research preprint by researchers including Tamay Besiroglu and Cullen O'Keefe found that the amortized cost of compute for frontier AI training runs has grown by around 2.4x per year (95% confidence interval: 2.0x to 3.1x) since 2016.[5] Total model development costs at the frontier are now well over $100 million. R&D staff alone make up 29–49% of total costs. The total compute used throughout the entire development process — including experiments, evaluations, and fine-tuning — is roughly 2.2x the compute of the final training run alone.

The energy costs are even more striking. According to the International Energy Agency, global data centre electricity consumption reached approximately 485 TWh in 2025 and is projected to approach 950 TWh by 2030 — around 3% of all global electricity demand.[6] In the United States, Morgan Stanley estimates that by 2028, US data centers will need 74 GW of power but only 25 GW of capacity will be accessible — a 49 GW shortfall large enough that every major hyperscaler has signed long-term power agreements to secure supply.[7] Goldman Sachs Research projects data center power demand to more than double from 31 GW in 2025 to 66 GW in 2027, with data centers' share of total US peak summer power demand jumping from 4.1% to 8.5% over the same period.[8]

Western Union spent money building poles and wires. Every pole and wire generated revenue. The capital investment was a cost with a calculable return.

AI companies spend money on compute and researchers. Neither generates direct revenue. The capital investment is a cost with an uncertain return.

The reason this matters is not philosophical. It is mechanical. The telegraph's business worked because the cost structure was linear: more messages, more revenue, same infrastructure. Under the current frontier scaling paradigm, AI's costs are rising rapidly: better performance requires more compute, which costs more, which requires more revenue — which may never catch up.

## The Trap: Bigger Is Better

The most powerful force in both the telegraph and AI industries is a single idea: bigger is better.

In the telegraph era, the "bigger-is-better" dynamic showed up as scale. A longer wire carried more messages. A larger network connected more cities. More capacity meant lower cost per message. Western Union's 44,000 miles of wire in 1864 weren't just bigger — they were *more useful* than smaller competitors' lines because long-distance messages traveled over multiple companies' networks, and a single integrated company reduced errors and increased reliability. Scale was the product.

In AI, the "bigger-is-better" dynamic shows up the same way. Around 2012, researchers recognized that substantial gains in model performance came simply from applying larger and larger scale data and computational resources to existing algorithms — a finding the AI Now Institute catalogued as the industry's pivot toward compute-intensive scaling.[9] Tech giants moved quickly to shore up their advantages. More compute means better models. Better models attract more users. More users justify more compute.

But here's where the comparison breaks down in a way that matters.

The telegraph's scale had a natural limit: geography. Once you had wires between every major city, you couldn't get meaningfully bigger. The U.S. telegraph network grew to roughly 600,000 miles by 1900.

AI's scale has no natural limit. The Epoch AI study found that Gemini Ultra drew roughly 35 megawatts of power capacity during training. If the historical growth trend continued, Epoch AI projects that a leading training run could require approximately one gigawatt of power capacity by 2029.

The telegraph's scale was bounded by geography. AI's scale is not. That distinction explains why the industry is accelerating rather than stabilizing.

When the telegraph reached its maximum useful scale, the industry stabilized. Profits were predictable. Prices stabilized. Companies competed on service quality, not on capacity.

AI faces a different dynamic: when one company achieves a performance breakthrough, others must spend more to surpass it. The costs keep rising because the definition of "good enough" keeps moving.

## What the Telegraph Actually Changed

The telegraph didn't just make communication faster. It restructured entire industries in ways that were concrete, documented, and quantifiable.

Railroads needed the telegraph because before 1851, the U.S. rail system ran on single-track, time-interval basis. Head-on collisions between trains traveling in opposite directions were a constant danger. Station managers could see exactly what trains were on the tracks under their supervision. Double-tracking the U.S. rail system in 1893 would have cost an estimated $957 million. Western Union's book capitalization was $123 million in 1893.[10] The telegraph made safe coordination possible without the catastrophic capital expenditure of double-tracking every line.

Perishable goods industries were transformed by the combination of the telegraph and the refrigerated car. Meatpacking and fresh produce distribution — once constrained by geography and season — became national industries. The telegraph enabled exact coordination of refrigerated shipments. Livestock was slaughtered and processed in centralized stockyards in Chicago, Kansas City, St. Louis, and Omaha. The resulting beef was shipped east at 50% the cost of shipping live cattle. The telegraph was the nervous system that made centralized logistics possible.

Financial markets were reshaped by price synchronization. New York became the financial center of the country because the telegraph enabled centralized price discovery. Over the course of the nineteenth century, hundreds of regional exchanges appeared and disappeared. By 1910, 90 percent of all bond trades and two-thirds of all stock trades occurred on the New York Stock Exchange.[11]

Each of these transformations was specific. Each had a measurable economic effect. Each followed a clear causal chain: telegraph technology → specific operational improvement → measurable economic change.

AI's transformations are vague and contested. Meta is investing heavily in AI advertising infrastructure. Microsoft is selling Copilot to software engineers. The technology is being applied across industries, but the economic value created is still being negotiated — and in many cases, the technology is being deployed against the interests of the people affected by it, with no mechanism for appeal.

The telegraph had a clear value proposition: send a message from point A to point B, faster than a horse. AI has found enormous demand — OpenAI earned $13.1 billion in revenue in 2025.[2] Anthropic has said it expects its first quarterly operating profit in the second quarter of 2026. Customers are paying at scale. But whether those revenues cover compute, research, distribution and capital costs is a different question.

The telegraph created skilled jobs — telegraph operators, linemen, repair workers, station agents — a new class of technical workers. AI is driving task automation in knowledge-intensive work. Current evidence shows reduced hiring in some exposed occupations, augmentation effects, and possible wage impacts. Stanford reports that large-scale job losses have not yet appeared in aggregate employment data — but the direction of change runs in the opposite direction from the telegraph, which created a workforce to build and maintain its infrastructure.

## The Technology Western Union Underestimated

In 1876, Alexander Graham Bell patented the telephone. Bell and his associates approached Western Union with an offer: sell us the patent for $100,000.

Western Union built a competing telephone system using technologies associated with Elisha Gray and Thomas Edison. Facing Bell's patent lawsuit as well as wider corporate pressures, it reached an out-of-court settlement in 1879 and withdrew from telephony.

Western Union did not collapse overnight. Telegraph traffic grew from 5.8 million messages in 1867 to 63.2 million by 1900.[1] The company's decline came much later. The lesson still matters: Western Union withdrew from telephony under legal and commercial pressure, yet retained its telegraph dominance for decades afterward. Whether AI incumbents face similar legal and regulatory reckoning — or will be displaced by entirely new architectures — remains an open question.

## The Architecture of Control

Both industries consolidated around the same pattern: fragmentation, chaos, then monopoly.

In 1851, the telegraph industry had 75 companies operating in the United States. They were fragmented, competing, chaotic. Messages traveled over multiple companies' lines for a single transmission. By 1866, Western Union had absorbed its last competitors and achieved over 90% market share — the first nationwide industrial monopoly in the United States. The telegraph's consolidation happened through system integration: connecting regional lines into a single network reduced error rates and improved service quality, but it also created barriers to entry. To compete with a large incumbent system, a new entrant needed to build equally large infrastructure. The "bigger-is-better" dynamic created a natural monopoly.

AI is following the same pattern. The key players haven't shifted: Microsoft, Google, Meta, Musk's xAI, OpenAI (backed by Microsoft), and Anthropic (backed by Amazon and Google). But the mechanism of consolidation is different. Telegraph consolidation happened through mergers and acquisitions. AI consolidation is happening through infrastructure lock-in. Many startups depend heavily on major cloud providers, GPU suppliers, and distribution channels, even if they can also use independent hosting, smaller models, or open-weight models. An OpenAI executive testified the company would be interested in purchasing Chrome if a court forced Google to divest it. Perplexity later made an actual unsolicited bid. The point was the same: infrastructure dependency creates barriers to entry that did not exist in the telegraph era.

There's a deeper insight about the telegraph that most comparison pieces miss. It's not about scale or monopoly — it's about the economics of information advantage. When price information about wheat in New York arrived in Buffalo, anyone with a telegraph subscription could act on it. But once everyone had the information, no one had a competitive advantage. The private incentive to discover information quickly disappeared once the information was widely shared. This is what economists call a collective action problem: once everyone has the same information, the race is to the bottom.[1] AI is experiencing the same dynamic, amplified. Once every company has access to ChatGPT, once every coder has access to Copilot, once every student has access to an AI tutor — the competitive advantage disappears. But here's the difference: in the telegraph's case, the marginal cost of transmitting additional messages was low and stable. In AI, every improvement requires more compute, more cost, and renders previous models obsolete. Every improvement makes the previous investment worthless.

### The Regulatory Response

Western Union was eventually challenged. The government responded — slowly, imperfectly, but persistently. State-level regulation was largely ineffective because telegraph transmission was interstate. Bills were introduced in almost every session of Congress calling for either regulation of or government entry into the industry. Western Union's lobbying apparatus successfully blocked almost all of them. Telecommunications companies were brought under Interstate Commerce Commission jurisdiction by the Mann–Elkins Act of 1910.[12] In 1911, the Department of Justice successfully used the Sherman Antitrust Act to force a breakup of Standard Oil. This created real pressure on AT&T, which had gained control of Western Union in 1909. Independent telephone companies lobbied for regulation. In response, AT&T issued the "Kingsbury Commitment" in 1913 — a unilateral agreement to interconnect with independent telephone companies and step back from acquiring them. It did not break up the monopoly, but it shaped its boundaries. The pattern was clear: monopoly power → public outrage → regulatory threat → negotiated settlement.[12]

### The Regulatory Gap

Today's AI regulation landscape looks nothing like the telegraph's. The European Union's AI Act entered into force on August 1, 2024 — the world's first comprehensive AI regulation framework. But the United States lacks a comprehensive federal AI statute. Instead, it relies on executive orders, state-level legislation, existing federal laws, and enforcement through agencies. In its 2026 Winter issue, The Business Lawyer described the state of AI regulation in 2025 as "one step up and two steps back" — the EU advanced its framework while the U.S. eliminated previous governance advances.[13] The AI industry is actively lobbying against regulation, using the same arguments Western Union used: innovation will be stifled, the U.S. will fall behind in the global competition, and self-regulation is preferable to government intervention.[13]

The telegraph's monopoly was eventually challenged. Whether AI's concentration of power will be constrained depends on choices being made right now, in courtrooms, in legislatures, in boardrooms. The choices policymakers make today will define the next century of information technology — just as the choices made about telegraph regulation defined the twentieth century.

## The Bubble: Same Pattern, Different Problem

The dot-com bubble peaked in March 2000. Companies with no revenue, no customers, and no business model were valued at billions of dollars. When the bubble burst, the Nasdaq lost 78% of its value. Thousands of companies went bankrupt. "Pets.com," "Webvan," "Boo.com" — the names became synonyms for financial foolishness.

But the internet didn't die. Amazon survived. Google survived. Microsoft survived. The internet went on to become the most important technology of the 21st century.

There is an important comparison here, and a crucial difference.

The dot-com bubble burst because the market realized that many companies were worthless. An AI bubble could burst for a different reason: the market could realize that the underlying technology, for all its hype, has not yet proven that it creates more economic value than the cost of building it. But the two bubbles are structurally different.

The crucial difference: when Amazon made money, it made money. It sold books online. People paid for them. It was a simple business model. The internet's value was in connecting buyers and sellers — and that value was immediately evident.

AI has found demand — OpenAI earned $13.1 billion in revenue in 2025, and enterprises are paying substantial amounts for API usage, coding tools and workplace products.[2] But there is no simple business model that demonstrates clear, undeniable profitability at scale. Meta uses AI for ads. Microsoft bundles AI with Office. But these are extensions of existing businesses, not new value propositions that customers are independently willing to pay for.

That distinction matters. The dot-com bubble was a valuation error — companies with no revenue were priced as if they had profitable businesses. The AI situation is different: revenue exists, but profitability has not yet been proven. The question is not whether companies are overvalued despite having no product. The question is whether the industry can become profitable given its cost structure.

There is a reasonable counterargument to all of this. The telegraph industry also faced financial crises, near-collapse moments, and years of massive overinvestment before it settled into profitability. Western Union went through its own brutal shakeout in the 1870s. Investors lost fortunes. If the telegraph is any guide, the AI crisis might resolve the same way — a period of massive losses followed by a profitable equilibrium once the infrastructure stabilizes.

That is possible. But there is a critical difference: the telegraph's cost curve was a one-time fix that reduced expenses going forward. Once you laid the wire, the marginal cost of an additional message was tiny. Under the current frontier scaling paradigm, AI's development costs may continue rising faster than revenue unless efficiency or monetization improves.[5] The telegraph's crisis was a problem of scale. AI's may be a problem of direction.

---

## The Thread

The telegraph and AI share a pattern: explosive growth, concentration dynamics, falling prices, transformative potential. But they are different in a way that matters.

Morse's first transmission used a government-funded experimental line, not a commercial message. The telegraph took years to develop a fee-per-message model. AI has found demand — but OpenAI has not demonstrated durable profitability after compute, research and capital costs.

The telegraph's scale was bounded by geography. AI's scale is not.

The telegraph created skilled jobs to build and maintain its infrastructure. AI is driving task automation in knowledge-intensive work.

The telegraph faced regulation and was eventually constrained by government. AI is facing minimal regulation and is actively lobbying against it.

The telegraph's incumbents were displaced by a technology they overlooked — and by legal battles they could not win. AI's incumbents may face similar disruption, whether from new architectures or regulatory change.

The telegraph changed the world. AI is changing the world. But the telegraph's revolution was profitable, bounded, and regulated. AI's revolution is generating enormous revenue — and it is not yet profitable, unbounded, and largely unregulated.

There's a way to read the telegraph's story that gives you hope: the chaos we see in AI now is just the growing pains of a technology finding its footing. Western Union went through its own brutal shakeout in the 1870s. Investors lost fortunes. Markets panicked. And then it settled. The telegraph didn't just survive the crisis — it became the infrastructure of the twentieth century.

That's what the optimists are betting on. That the massive spending will stabilize, the scale will find equilibrium, and AI will eventually generate profits that justify the billions being poured into it.

But the uncertainty remains: under the current frontier scaling paradigm, development costs may continue rising faster than revenue unless efficiency or monetization improves.[5] Whether AI becomes profitable — or remains an R&D arms race with a venture capital endowment — depends on which path the industry chooses.
