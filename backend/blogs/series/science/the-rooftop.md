# The Rooftop

The insects had stopped singing before she noticed the sky.

Luzia was on the rooftop because her mother had sent her up to bring the laundry in before the rain. That was what she thought was coming. That was what the heavy air felt like — the stillness before a storm, the way the heat pressed down and the leaves hung motionless. She had gathered the dry clothes into a pile and was reaching for the last sheet when she looked south and saw that the sunset was wrong.

It was 7:14 PM. The sun had set an hour ago. The sky should have been darkening to its usual deep blue, then to black, with the first stars showing over the Atlantic. Instead, the southern horizon was glowing a dull, bruised red. The color of a fading wound. The color of something that should not be there.

She stood still with the sheet in her hands and watched it deepen.

The glow rose slowly, like a tide. Not the sharp edge of city lights reflecting off clouds. Not the brief flash of lightning on the horizon. A steady diffusion of light, pushing upward into the dark, spreading along the curve of the sky. Red at the bottom, shading to a pale pink higher up, then gone.

"Luzia!"

Her mother's voice from the kitchen window, two floors down.

"The clothes!"

She blinked. Folded the sheet. Gathered the rest of the pile in her arms and went down the stairs.

---

The apartment had three rooms and a rooftop that belonged to no one. Luzia's mother had hung the laundry up there for eight years, ever since they moved in, and no one had ever told her she couldn't. The agreement was unspoken: the rooftop was for everyone, which meant it was for no one, which meant whoever used it first in the morning kept it.

Luzia dropped the laundry on her bed and sat down at her desk. Her phone was on the charger. The screen showed no new messages. She picked it up and opened the weather app. The forecast said clear skies. She refreshed it. Clear skies. She refreshed it again. The app stalled on a loading spinner, then returned the same forecast.

She put the phone down and looked out the window. The glow was still there, visible even through the glass. Brighter now. She could see it without searching.

In the living room, her grandmother was sitting in the armchair by the window, the one she had been sitting in for twelve years, ever since she came to live with them after Luzia's grandfather died. She was not watching television. She was looking out at the same sky.

"Vó." Luzia stood in the doorway. "Do you see that?"

Her grandmother did not turn. She was a small woman with hair that had gone gray early and kept going, past white to something Luzia had no word for. The color of dried river stones.

"I see it," she said.

"What is it?"

Her grandmother was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "I don't know."

---

The power went out at 7:52 PM.

Luzia was in her room when the lights flickered once, twice, and then held. She waited. They flickered again, dimmed to a brownish glow, and died. The refrigerator compressor wound down with a descending hum. The television went dark. The hum of the city outside — the distant traffic, the air conditioners, the background thrum of a million electrical devices all breathing together — dropped by a register. Not silence. But closer to it than she had ever heard.

Her phone screen was still on. She checked the battery: 67 percent. The charger was dead. The Wi-Fi was down. The mobile signal showed one bar, then zero.

"Luzia."

Her grandmother's voice from the living room. Calm.

"Bring the flashlight from the kitchen."

She found it in the drawer where it always was, next to the candles and the lighter. She brought it to her grandmother, who took it without a word and set it on the table beside her, beam facing the wall, creating a circle of soft light in the room.

They sat together in the half-dark.

"Was it the storm?" Luzia asked.

"What storm?"

"The —" She gestured at the window. "The sky. The light."

Her grandmother looked at her. In the flashlight's glow, her face was all shadows and hollows.

"Maybe," she said. "Or maybe the storm is something else."

---

At 8:30 PM, Luzia went back up to the rooftop.

She did not ask permission. Her mother was in the kitchen lighting candles, her voice low on the phone with someone, trying to find out what had happened. Her grandmother was still in the armchair, watching the window. Luzia took the stairs two at a time and pushed open the door to the roof.

The sky had changed.

The red glow was no longer a distant stain on the horizon. It had risen overhead, spreading across the dome of the sky like a slow hemorrhage. Curtains of light hung from the darkness — not the sharp green ribbons of the aurora she had seen in videos from Norway and Iceland on her phone, the ones that looked like special effects. These were different. They were red and pink and deep magenta, almost invisible at the edges, like the sky itself was bleeding. They did not ripple or dance. They pulsed. A slow, breathing rhythm, as if something vast and distant was alive and turning over in its sleep.

She stood in the middle of the rooftop, the laundry line empty above her, and watched.

The city was dark. She had never seen Recife dark. There was always light somewhere — the glow of the downtown towers, the streetlights along the avenues, the distant flicker of the favelas climbing the hillsides. Now there was nothing. The black shapes of buildings against the black sky. A few candles in windows. The stars, which she had never seen clearly from her rooftop because there was always too much light, were emerging in numbers she did not have a name for.

The aurora moved above them. Red. Silent. Wrong.

---

The rooftop door creaked open behind her.

Her grandmother stepped out, wrapped in the thin shawl she wore even in summer. She looked up at the sky and did not speak for a long time.

"It was red the last time too," she said finally.

Luzia turned. "The last time?"

"Your bisavó told me. When she was a girl. Before I was born." Her grandmother's voice was quiet, not with fear but with effort, as if she was pulling the words from a place she had not visited in years. "She said the sky turned red. Three nights. The whole village gathered in the plaza and watched. The priest said it was a sign from God. The old people said it was the jaguar trying to eat the sun."

"The jaguar?"

"The Celestial Jaguar." Her grandmother pointed south, where the red was deepest. "There. Do you see the bright star? The one that flickers?"

Luzia followed her finger. A red star near the horizon, flickering through the auroral glow.

"Antares," she said. "It's a red supergiant. It's in the constellation Scorpius."

Her grandmother lowered her hand. "That is the eye of the jaguar. When he opens his eye, the sky bleeds. That is what my grandmother told me. I never believed her."

She looked at the sky again. The curtains of light were shifting, the red deepening to magenta at the edges.

"Until now."

---

They sat on the rooftop for hours.

The flashlight was between them, pointed away from their faces, making a small pool of ordinary light in the middle of the extraordinary dark. Luzia's phone was dead. Her grandmother had stopped checking the time. The city below them was a scatter of candlelit windows and the occasional headlight of a car moving slowly, carefully, through intersections that had no signals.

The aurora did not stop. It changed — fading to a pale pink, then surging back to deep red, then shifting to a color Luzia had no name for, something between violet and gray. The curtains moved, but not like fabric. Like smoke. Like water. Like something that had its own physics, which it did, she knew. Charged particles from the Sun, funneled down the magnetic field lines, struck oxygen atoms at high altitude and released photons at 630 nanometers. Red. The color of a specific energy transition. She had learned this in school. She had aced the test.

But sitting on the rooftop, watching the sky bleed, the textbook explanation felt like a description of a song. Accurate. Complete. And nothing at all like the experience of hearing it.

"Vó," she said. "How did your grandmother know? About the jaguar?"

Her grandmother shifted in her chair. The shawl pulled tighter around her shoulders.

"She was Tupi," she said. "From the interior. Her family had been watching the sky for longer than anyone remembers. They knew when to plant, when to harvest, when the rains would come, by where the stars were. They called the Milky Way the Tapir's Path. They said the stars were the footprints of animals crossing the sky."

"The Tapir's Path," Luzia repeated. She looked up. The Milky Way was visible overhead, a band of pale light cutting through the darkness. She had seen it before, but never like this — never with the city dark enough to let it through.

"Joykexo," her grandmother said, pointing. "Do you see those three stars in a line?"

"Orion's Belt."

"In the old language, it is Joykexo. A beautiful woman. She is the path of the dead." She paused. "My grandmother told me that when you die, you walk along the Tapir's Path until you reach Joykexo, and then you cross into the sky. The stars are your footprints."

Luzia stared at the three stars. She had seen them a thousand times. She had never thought of them as a path.

Then her grandmother pointed again, to a cluster of faint stars low in the east.

"And there. The beehive. The Pleiades. That is Eixu. It marks the new year. When it rises before the sun, the cycle begins again."

"Does it still work?" Luzia asked. "The old calendar?"

Her grandmother was quiet. Then: "It used to. But the seasons have shifted. The rains come at the wrong time. The birds migrate differently." She looked at her hands. "My grandmother's grandmother might still recognize the stars. But I think the stars no longer recognize us."

---

The aurora peaked at some hour Luzia could not name.

The time felt irrelevant. She had stopped measuring. The red curtains intensified until the whole southern half of the sky was glowing, the light bright enough to cast a faint shadow on the rooftop. The air felt strange — charged, tight, the way it feels before a thunderstorm but without the pressure. Her skin prickled — or she imagined it did. The night had become unfamiliar.

"The dogs have been quiet all evening," her grandmother said. "Did you notice?"

Luzia had not. But now that she thought about it, she realized she had not heard a single dog bark since the power went out. The city was silent in a way that felt deliberate, as if every living thing was holding its breath.

"Do you think it's dangerous?" Luzia asked.

Her grandmother considered the question.

"Dangerous for the machines," she said. "The power lines. The satellites. The things we built that depend on the sky being quiet." She looked at Luzia. "But not for us. The sky has done this before. It will do it again. We are small enough to survive it."

---

At some point, Luzia's mother came up to the rooftop. She stood in the doorway, a candle in her hand, and looked at the sky. She did not say anything for a long time.

Then she said, "The radio says the whole northeast is down. The transmission lines. They don't know when it will come back."

She sat down on the rooftop beside them, the candle flickering in her hand, and the three of them watched the sky together.

"The radio still works?" Luzia asked.

"For now. They're saying it's a geomagnetic storm. The worst one in decades." Her mother shook her head. "There's a warning about the GPS. About the power grid. They're telling people to stay home."

Luzia looked at her grandmother. She had not said the words "geomagnetic storm." She had said "the jaguar." Both descriptions were true. Neither was complete.

---

The aurora began to fade at the first hint of dawn.

The red curtains softened to pink, then to a pale glow that was indistinguishable from the approaching daylight. The eastern horizon brightened, ordinary and reliable, as if the sky had decided to stop performing and go back to being itself.

Luzia's eyes were heavy. Her body ached from sitting on the concrete for hours. Her grandmother had fallen asleep in her chair, the shawl pulled up to her chin, her breathing slow and even. Her mother had gone back inside to check the radio.

Luzia watched the stars disappear one by one as the sky lightened. The Milky Way faded. The Pleiades sank toward the horizon. Orion's Belt tilted westward.

The power was still off. The city was still dark. But the sky was normal again, and that felt like mercy.

She stood up, stiff and cold, and walked to the edge of the rooftop. The Atlantic was visible to the east, a sheet of gray metal under the rising sun. No ships on the horizon. No planes in the sky. Just water and light and the slow return of color to the world.

She thought about what her grandmother had said. *The stars no longer recognize us.* She thought about the Tapir's Path and the Celestial Jaguar and the beautiful woman who carried the dead across the sky. She thought about her textbook, closed on her desk, which explained everything and nothing.

She did not know which version of the sky was the real one. She did not know if the question even made sense. The sky was not a story or a physics problem. The sky was the sky. It had done something extraordinary last night, and she had watched it, and that was the only thing she was certain of.

She heard her grandmother stir behind her.

"Luzia."

She turned. Her grandmother was awake, looking at her with the same eyes that had been looking at the sky for seventy years.

"Come," she said. "I will make breakfast. When the power comes back, we can talk about what we saw."

They went down the stairs together, leaving the rooftop empty in the morning light. The laundry line swayed in the breeze. The sky was blue. The insects had started singing again.

---

The power came back at 4:17 PM that afternoon.

Luzia was in her room when the lights flickered once, held, and stayed on. The refrigerator compressor kicked in with a shudder. The Wi-Fi router blinked green. Her phone, which she had plugged in the moment the power returned, began to charge.

Messages arrived in a flood. Friends asking if she was okay. News alerts about the storm. A video from someone in João Pessoa who had filmed the aurora from their balcony. She scrolled through them, reading, absorbing, but not responding.

Her grandmother was in the armchair by the window. She had not moved all day. She was looking at the sky, which was ordinary now, blue and empty and unremarkable.

"Vó," Luzia said.

Her grandmother turned.

"Will it happen again?"

Her grandmother looked back at the window. She did not answer for a long time. Then she said, "The old stories say the jaguar always comes back. They do not say when."

"That's not an answer."

"It is the only one I have."

Luzia stood in the doorway, her phone warm in her hand, the notifications piling up unread. She looked at her grandmother. She looked at the ordinary sky through the window.

She walked back to her room, sat down at her desk, and opened a new notebook. She had never kept a journal before. She did not know where to start.

She wrote:

*The sky is not what I thought it was.*

She read the line. Crossed it out.

She wrote:

*My grandmother knows things that are not in any textbook.*

She stared at the words. Below them, she began to draw the three stars of Joykexo, the tail of the Celestial Jaguar, the curve of the Tapir's Path as she had seen it last night, when the city was dark and the sky was red and she had understood, for the first time, that there were older ways of knowing.

She did not know if she believed in the jaguar. She did not know if she believed in the path of the dead.

But she knew that her grandmother had seen something in the sky last night that the textbooks could not explain. And she knew that she wanted to understand what it was.

The notebook page filled slowly. Outside, the sky stayed blue. The insects sang. The city hummed back to life.

But Luzia kept drawing.

And she kept listening for the jaguar.
