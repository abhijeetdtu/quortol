# The Weight of a Pagoda

## 1. The World Before

The silk came from the east, from the weavers of Kanchipuram, and Mallana Chetti could judge its quality by the sound it made when he let a length run through his fingers. Good silk whispered. Poor silk grated. This bale whispered.

He folded it and set it on the stack. Twenty-three bales in the high-grade lot, six in the second tier, and a remainder lot of uneven weave that he would sell to the coastal merchants who did not know better. The remainder would go to Goa, where the Portuguese factors wore local cloth and thought themselves subtle.

The shop was a stone pavilion with three pillars facing the main bazaar street. It had been in his family for two generations. The pillars were carved with yalis — lion-faced beasts with elephant trunks — and the morning light fell through the gap between the roof tiles and the lintel in a single yellow beam that moved across the floor as the day passed. Mallana had sat in this shop for twenty-five years, since he was twenty years old, and he knew the beam's path the way a farmer knows the path of the sun across his field.

From where he knelt, he could hear the bazaar waking. To his left, the pepper merchant was opening his sacks, and the sharp, aromatic dust drifted across the pavilions. To his right, the oil sellers were filling their bronze vessels. Further down the street, a Portuguese factor was shouting in accented Kannada about the quality of his horses — *bons cavalos, bons cavalos* — and a crowd of palace buyers was gathering. The bells of ox-carts sounded from the direction of the river, and from the Virupaksha temple, half a mile away, came the sound of the morning prayers, the conch shells and the bells and the chanting of pilgrims who had walked hundreds of miles to see the god.

Mallana's sword lay beside him, wrapped in an oiled cloth. He did not wear it in the shop — no one did, it would be like wearing armor to bed — but it was always within arm's reach. His father had taught him that a man who moves goods between ports must carry steel, because the road does not care about your caste or your ledger. A Balija Chetti was a warrior when required and a merchant the rest of the time, and the distinction was a matter of circumstance, not nature.

His son Chinna sat cross-legged in the corner, copying a ledger. The boy was fifteen, with his mother's serious eyes and his father's habit of pressing his lips together when he concentrated. He had been learning the trade for three years now — how to weigh gold, how to grade raw diamonds from the Golconda pits, how to speak enough Portuguese to negotiate with the factors from Goa. He was good with numbers but impatient with silk. He wanted to travel. He wanted to see the coast.

"When I am sixteen," Chinna said, not looking up from his slate, "I want to go to Goa with the next caravan."

"We will see."

"You said that last year."

"Then you know my position has not changed."

Chinna made a sound that was not quite a laugh and not quite a sigh. He dipped his stylus and went back to the ledger.

A shadow fell across the threshold. Mallana looked up.

The man standing in the doorway was a messenger from the palace stables — Mallana recognized the brass badge on his shoulder. He was young, maybe twenty, and he was sweating despite the cool morning.

"Mallana Chetti?"

"Yes."

"The quartermaster sends word. He wants to know when the next shipment of horses arrives from Goa. The stables are running low. The campaign has taken most of the good stock."

Mallana set down the silk. "The horses are in transit. I expect them within the fortnight."

"Faster, if possible. The quartermaster says there is movement to the north. The sultanates."

"Which sultanates?"

"All of them."

The young man left. Mallana sat very still for a long moment, his hand resting on the silk bale.

Chinna looked up. "Father?"

"Finish the ledger."

The bazaar continued around him — the haggling, the chanting, the bells — but Mallana was no longer listening. He was calculating. He had thirty horses on the road from Goa, paid for with gold borrowed at interest from a Chetti lender in the city. If the sultanates marched, the horses would be needed. If the sultanates did not march, the horses would still be needed — the palace always needed horses. But if the sultanates marched *and* the horses did not arrive in time, the debt would still be due, and the interest would still accrue, and the lender would still expect payment.

He pressed his thumb against the silk. Good silk. Whispering silk.

He had built this business from nothing. His father had been a small trader, moving palm sugar and jaggery between village markets. Mallana had taken the family's savings at twenty and bought a share in a caravan to the coast. He had learned Portuguese from the factors, learned to tell the difference between a good Arabian stallion and a horse that had been ridden hard and fed poorly and called Arabian anyway. He had learned that gold pagodas weigh the same in any language. He had married Nagamma, who had brought a small dowry and a sharp mind, and together they had built a house with a courtyard and a mango tree. He had sent Chinna to the temple school to learn letters.

Twenty-five years. Twenty-three bales of silk.

He wrapped the cloth around his sword and stood.

"I am going to the horse market," he said to Chinna. "Stay with the shop. Do not leave."

## 2. The Rumor

The horse market was at the northern edge of the city, near the great tank, where the water lay flat and green and the sun reflected off it so brightly that men squinted even at midday. The dealers had set up their pens under awnings of palm leaf, and the horses stood in the heat, switching their tails, their ribs visible through their skin.

Mallana walked among them, looking. He did not need to buy — his own shipment was coming — but he needed to hear. The horse market was where news traveled first, because horse dealers had no loyalty to anyone and would say anything to anyone, and because the horses themselves came from outside the empire, from Arabia and Persia and Africa, and the men who brought them had seen things the men of the city had not.

He found what he was looking for near the far end of the market: an old horse trader named Basava, who had been bringing animals down from the northern passes for forty years. Basava sat on a folded blanket, drinking buttermilk from a clay cup. He had no horses left to sell.

"You sold out," Mallana said, sitting down beside him.

"I sold out," Basava agreed. "To the palace. Every animal I had, at any price. Do you know why?"

"Tell me."

"The sultanates have allied. Bijapur, Golconda, Ahmednagar, Bidar. The four of them, together. They are marching south."

"Five," Mallana said.

"What?"

"There are five sultanates." He counted on his fingers. "Bijapur, Golconda, Ahmednagar, Bidar, Berar. Five."

Basava looked at him. "It does not matter how many. One is too many. Five is the same as fifty. They are allied. When have they ever been allied?"

Mallana had no answer. The Deccan Sultanates had fought each other for as long as anyone could remember. They had raided each other's borders, seized each other's forts, beheaded each other's ambassadors. That they had agreed to a common cause meant that they feared Vijayanagara more than they hated each other, and that frightened him more than the alliance itself.

"Has Rama Raya marched?" he asked.

"He marched three days ago," Basava said. "Sixty thousand infantry, forty thousand cavalry. They say he has fifteen hundred elephants. They say the Gilani brothers are commanding the artillery."

"The Gilani brothers are Muslims," Mallana said.

"Yes. In the army of a Hindu king, fighting against a Muslim alliance. Do you see the problem?"

Mallana saw it. The Gilani brothers were Persian mercenaries who commanded the empire's artillery. They had been in Rama Raya's service for years, well paid, well treated. But when a man's loyalty is purchased with gold, it can be purchased again.

He walked back through the city, and the city looked the same as it always had. The women were drawing water from the wells. The priests were sweeping the temple steps. The Portuguese factors were drinking wine in the shade of their lodgings and complaining about the heat. Life was happening as if the world were not tilting.

At home, Nagamma was pounding spices in the courtyard. The mango tree cast a small circle of shade. She looked up when he entered and saw his face, and she set down the pestle.

"You heard something," she said.

"The sultanates have allied. Rama Raya has marched north."

Nagamma was silent for a moment. Then she said: "Send Chinna to Kanchipuram. To your sister's house. Let him stay there until the trouble passes."

"It is not trouble. It is a war."

"Then send him because of the war."

"I cannot send him," Mallana said. "If I send him, I am saying that I expect to lose. The lenders will hear. The palace will hear. I have thirty horses arriving from Goa. I have orders to fill. I have —"

"You have a son," Nagamma said. It was not quiet. It was the flat voice she used when she had already decided something and was not going to argue.

Mallana looked at her. Her hands were still dusted with turmeric. Her sari was old and patched. She had been saving the patches for new cloth from his own inventory, the best quality, the whispering silk, and he had promised her a new sari every year for the last three years and never delivered.

"The horses are already paid for," he said. "If I cancel the shipment, I lose the deposit. I lose the interest on the loan. I lose face with the Portuguese."

"Then you will have your horses and your loans and your reputation," Nagamma said, "and you will have no son to inherit them. What will a Chetti be, with no one to pass his gold to?"

"He is fifteen. He is old enough to travel. He is not a child."

"He is my child."

Mallana sat down on the step beside her. The courtyard was quiet. From the street came the sound of a flute, someone practicing a raga, the notes rising and falling and rising again.

"Let me think," he said.

"There is no time."

"There is always time. The battle has not been fought yet."

Nagamma said nothing. She picked up the pestle and began to pound again, harder than before, and Mallana sat in the small circle of shade and listened to the rhythm of her work.

He thought about the thirty horses. He thought about the Portuguese factor in Goa, João da Silva, who had advanced him credit on the strength of their ten-year friendship. He thought about the silk merchants of Kanchipuram, who expected payment for the twenty-three bales. He thought about the diamond merchants of Golconda, who had given him a parcel of rough stones on consignment, and the cutter who was waiting for them, and the buyer in the palace who had already paid a deposit.

Everything was linked. Every transaction was a chain that connected him to someone else, and if he broke one link, the whole thing would unravel. He could not afford to unravel. He had a family. He had a name. He had built this.

He would wait.

He would see what happened.

## 3. The Break

The messenger arrived at dawn on the twenty-fifth of January.

Mallana was awake — he had not slept well in a week — and he heard the running footsteps in the street before the pounding came at the door. He opened it with his sword in his hand.

The man outside was not a messenger from the palace. He was a horse trader from the northern border, a man Mallana knew by sight but not by name. He was covered in dust and dried sweat. Two fingers were missing from his left hand, the wound fresh, wrapped in a dirty cloth that was dark with blood.

"When?" Mallana said.

"Three days ago," the man said. "At Talikota. The army is destroyed."

The words did not make sense. Mallana stood in the doorway, holding his sword, and the words passed through him without landing.

"That is not possible," he said.

"The Gilani brothers turned. They had loaded the cannons, but they had not loaded them properly. When our cavalry charged, the cannons fired into their own men. Then the brothers took their gunners and changed sides. The sultanates hit us from both flanks. Rama Raya was captured. They cut off his head on the battlefield."

"Rama Raya is dead."

"He is dead. The army is gone. The men who survived are running. I am one of them."

The horse trader looked down at his missing fingers. "The Gilani brothers were buying their lives. They sold ours."

Mallana stood in the doorway. The light was still gray, the street still quiet. Somewhere a rooster crowed, and the sound was so ordinary that it seemed obscene.

"The palace," he said. "Have you told the palace?"

"The palace is empty. Tirumala fled during the night. They say he took the treasury. Fifteen hundred elephants loaded with gold. He is gone."

Mallana felt the world tilt. The stone pillar beside him was solid. He put his hand on it.

"Three days," he said.

"Maybe two. Maybe less. The sultanates are marching south. They will be here as fast as their horses can carry them."

The horse trader left. Mallana did not see where he went. He stood in the doorway for a long time, and the light grew brighter, and the rooster kept crowing, and the city began to wake.

Chinna came to the door, rubbing his eyes.

"Father? Who was that?"

Mallana looked at his son. The boy was still half-asleep, his hair mussed, his dhoti twisted from sleeping.

"Pack your things," Mallana said.

"What?"

"Pack your things. We are leaving."

The city took three days to understand what had happened, and the understanding came in waves. First came the rumor — the army had lost. Then came the confirmation — Rama Raya was dead. Then came the panic.

Mallana did not go to the palace. He did not try to verify the news. The horse trader's missing fingers were verification enough. He had spent twenty-five years learning to read people — their faces, their hands, the way they held their bodies when they spoke — and the horse trader had been telling the truth.

He went to the shop.

The bazaar was already changing. The Portuguese factors were gone — they had lodgings near the palace, and they had seen Tirumala's elephants leaving, and they had packed their things and headed for the coast within the hour. The pepper merchant was boarding his stall. The oil sellers were loading their bronze vessels onto carts. A woman was weeping in the middle of the street, and no one stopped to help her.

Mallana opened the chest under the counter. It contained three hundred gold pagodas — his savings, his working capital, the accumulated profit of a decade. He had planned to use it to buy a share in a ship that traded with the Malay ports. He had dreamed of becoming a shipowner, of seeing his goods carried across the ocean.

He took the gold out and wrapped it in cloth. Then he knelt and pried up a stone in the floor of the shop, behind the pillar with the yali carving. The earth underneath was dry and hard. He dug with his hands, scraping the dirt away, and he placed the wrapped gold in the hole. He covered it, pressed the earth down, replaced the stone.

It was not a hiding place that would fool anyone who looked carefully. But the Sultanate soldiers would not be looking carefully at the floor of a silk merchant's shop. They would be looking at the temple treasures, the palace vaults, the houses of the rich. They would not dig in the dirt of a man who had already fled.

He hoped.

The shop looked different now. The twenty-three bales of silk, the chest of diamonds, the bronze weights, the ledgers — suddenly they were not wealth but weight. He could not carry them. He could not hide them. They were evidence of a life that was about to be erased.

He took two bales of the best silk. He took the chest of diamonds. He took the ledgers — he would need the ledgers, to prove his debts and credits if he ever found a new place to trade. He took his sword.

The rest he left.

At home, Nagamma was packing. She had filled two ox-carts with astonishing efficiency — silk bales, a small chest of diamonds, dried food, water jars, the household gods, a bag of rice, blankets, cooking pots, a medicine box, Chinna's writing slate. She had put everything that mattered into two carts, and she was standing in the courtyard with her hands on her hips, looking at the mango tree.

"We cannot take the tree," Mallana said.

"I know."

"Then what are you looking at?"

"I am saying goodbye."

Chinna helped him yoke the oxen. The boy was pale but steady. He did not ask questions. He did not cry. He worked.

The streets were already choked. Everyone in the city had the same idea at the same time — leave, leave now, leave before the sultanates arrive. The southern gate was a nightmare of bodies and animals. A family of Brahmins had abandoned their cart in the middle of the road, and no one could move it, and the crowd was pushing past, and a child was screaming somewhere, and an elephant was trumpeting, and the dust was so thick that Mallana could taste grit between his teeth.

At the gate, he saw Kondama, a fellow Chetti who traded in copper and brass. Kondama had two carts loaded high, and his wife and children were walking beside them, and he was shouting at someone to get out of his way.

"Mallana!" Kondama called out. "Where are you going?"

"South. Penukonda."

"Fool. The sultanates will take everything south. They will burn every town between here and the sea. Come north with me — to Goa. The Portuguese will protect us."

"The Portuguese protect their own," Mallana said. "They will not protect us."

"They will protect my gold. I have a factor in Goa who owes me favors. I will be safe there."

"Go then. I am going south."

Kondama shook his head. "You are making a mistake."

"Perhaps. But it is my mistake to make."

They did not embrace. There was no time. Kondama turned his carts north, and Mallana turned his carts south, and the crowd swallowed them both.

On the road, the city fell behind them. Mallana did not look back. He walked beside the oxen with his hand on the hilt of his sword, and Chinna walked on the other side, and Nagamma walked behind, carrying the household gods wrapped in her best sari.

They marched through the afternoon. The road was lined with refugees — people on foot, people on oxen, people carrying what they could. The wealthy had elephants and palanquins. The poor had nothing but the clothes on their backs. Everyone was moving south.

Near sunset, Mallana stopped and looked back.

The sky behind them was red. Not sunset red — fire red. The smoke was rising from the capital, a black pillar that spread across the sky like a spreading stain.

The sultanates had arrived.

Nagamma came and stood beside him. She did not say anything. She took his hand, and they stood together, watching the smoke, and the oxen lowed, and the refugees streamed past them, and the fire burned all night and all the next day and for months afterward.

## 4. The Flight

The first mile took them past the city's outer wall, where the road narrowed to a funnel of bodies. An old woman had fallen and no one stopped to help her up. A Tamil Brahmin's cart had lost a wheel and he stood in the road, wailing at the sky, while the crowd pushed past him. A child sat alone on a milestone, crying for a mother who was already gone. Mallana kept his hand on his sword and his eyes on the road ahead. He did not look at the child. He did not look at the woman. He looked ahead, because looking ahead was the only way to keep moving.

They walked for seven days. The oxen were slow, and the carts were heavy. Mallana learned to read the landscape for threats: a cluster of abandoned carts meant a bandit ambush ahead; a village with no smoke rising meant it had already been looted; a group of armed men moving southeast meant the Sultanate cavalry was spreading out to raid the countryside.

He learned to sleep with his sword in his hand.

On the third night, they camped in a dry riverbed. Chinna gathered wood for a fire. Nagamma cooked rice in a small pot. They ate in silence, sitting close together for warmth, and the stars were so bright that Mallana could see the dust on his own hands.

"Father," Chinna said.

"Yes."

"Will we ever go back?"

Mallana considered his answer carefully. He had always told his son the truth about trade — about risk, about calculation, about the difference between a good debt and a bad one. He could not lie to him now.

"I do not know," he said. "The city is burning. The temple will burn. The palace will burn. Everything we built is in ashes."

"But we are alive."

"Yes. We are alive."

Chinna nodded, as if this were a piece of information he was filing away for later use. He wrapped himself in a blanket and lay down, and within minutes he was asleep.

Nagamma sat beside Mallana. The fire crackled. The stars turned overhead.

"You taught him well," she said.

"I taught him to read ledgers."

"You taught him not to panic. That is more valuable."

Mallana looked at his wife. Her face was lined with dust and exhaustion. She had walked every step of the way, carrying the household gods, while he walked beside the oxen with his sword. She had not complained once.

"The gold," he said. "I buried it under the shop."

"I know."

"You did not ask."

"I did not need to ask. You are a merchant. Of course you buried the gold."

He laughed, a short, surprised laugh. It was the first time he had laughed in days, and it felt strange in his throat.

"How much?" she asked.

"Three hundred pagodas. Maybe more, with what was in the chest. I did not count carefully."

"That is enough."

"It is enough to start again. If we can find a place to start."

"We will find a place."

On the fifth day, they passed a village that had been burned. The houses were blackened shells. The bodies were still in the streets, swollen in the sun. Flies covered everything. Mallana covered Chinna's eyes and walked faster.

On the seventh day, they reached the outskirts of Penukonda. The town was overflowing with refugees. The court of Tirumala had set up in the old fort, but the fort was small, and the town was not equipped to hold tens of thousands of people. The streets were clogged. The wells were running dry. The price of rice had tripled.

Mallana found a space at the edge of the town, near a dried-up tank, and set up camp. He did not try to enter the fort. He did not try to present himself to the court. He was a nobody now — a merchant without a shop, without inventory, without credit. The court would not receive him until he had something to offer, and he had nothing but two bales of silk and a small chest of diamonds.

He sat under the ox-cart, in the shade, and he counted his resources.

Silk: two bales, high quality. Value in normal times: maybe fifty pagodas. Value now, in a starving town full of refugees: perhaps fifteen, if he could find a buyer.

Diamonds: a mixed parcel, some good, some mediocre. Value in normal times: perhaps two hundred pagodas. Value now: unknown. The diamond cutters were all in the capital, and the capital was burning.

Gold: three hundred pagodas, buried under a stone floor fifty miles north, in a city occupied by enemy soldiers.

He was not destitute. He was close.

Nagamma came and sat beside him. She had found a small clay lamp somewhere and was cleaning it with a rag.

"When the town settles," she said, "I can sell the silk. Piece by piece. To the wives of the courtiers. They will need cloth, even in a diminished court."

"Women's work," Mallana said, and immediately regretted it.

"It is work," she said. "That is what matters."

He looked at her. Her hands were cracked. Her face was thin. She had not complained once.

"I am sorry," he said.

"For what?"

"For the sari I never gave you. For the three years I promised and did not deliver. For this." He gestured at the camp, the dust, the ox-cart, the refugees. "For all of this."

Nagamma set down the lamp. She took his hand. Her palm was rough against his.

"You brought us out alive," she said. "That is the only sari I need."

That night, Mallana could not sleep. He lay under the ox-cart, listening to the sounds of the camp — crying children, arguing men, the lowing of cattle, the distant drumming from the fort. He thought about the thirty horses that were somewhere on the road from Goa, if they had not been seized by the Sultanate cavalry or turned back by the Portuguese. He thought about João da Silva, who had trusted him with credit and would now demand payment that he could not make. He thought about the nanadesi — the network of merchants from many lands that had connected Vijayanagara to the ports of the Coromandel coast, to the diamond mines of Golconda, to the silk-weaving villages of Kanchipuram, to the horse markets of Arabia and Africa. The network was broken. The capital was gone. The roads were dangerous. The buyers were dead or scattered.

He would have to rebuild. But where?

Penukonda was safe for now, but it was a diminished court. Tirumala had fled with the treasury, but a treasury is not an empire. The sultanates would not stop at the old capital. They would send raiding parties south, probing, testing, looking for more to take. Penukonda might fall. The court might scatter again.

Kanchipuram was further south, beyond the immediate reach of the sultanates. It was a temple city, wealthy, stable. His sister lived there. The silk weavers were there. If he could reach Kanchipuram with his remaining goods, he could find a place in the local market. He could start small — a stall, a few bolts of cloth, some diamonds sold one at a time. He could build again.

He would go south.

In the morning, he told Nagamma.

"South," she said. "Further south."

"Yes."

"What about the gold under the shop?"

"I will come back for it. When the sultanates leave. When the city is safe. In a month, maybe two."

"Come back alone?"

"Yes."

She looked at him for a long time. Then she said: "You taught Chinna to read ledgers, not to beg. If you go back to the city and do not return, what will he read? Ledgers with no entries?"

"I will return."

"You do not know that."

"No. But I know where the gold is. And I know that without it, we are beggars."

## 5. The Aftermath

Weeks passed. The camp at Penukonda grew. More refugees arrived every day. Food grew scarce. The price of rice rose again, and then again. A man was beaten to death for stealing a handful of grain from a merchant's cart.

Nagamma began to sell the silk, piece by piece, as she had promised. She stood at the entrance of the camp with a length of cloth draped over her arm, and she negotiated with the wives of the courtiers who came down from the fort. She was good at it — better than Mallana would have been, because the courtiers' wives trusted a woman in a patched sari more than they would have trusted a man with a sword. She sold the first bale for the equivalent of twelve pagodas in copper coins and rice. It was a fraction of what it was worth, but it was food.

Chinna found work as a scribe. A clerk in the fort needed someone to copy ledgers, and Chinna's handwriting was clean and even. He came home each evening with ink-stained fingers and a handful of copper coins, and he set them in a pile beside his sleeping mat, and he counted them.

Mallana did nothing. He sat under the ox-cart and watched. He had not been idle for twenty-five years. He had woken every morning with a purpose — a shipment to track, a debt to collect, a deal to negotiate. Now he had nothing but the weight of his sword and the memory of three hundred pagodas buried under a stone floor.

He waited for news from the north.

It came in fragments. A trader from the coast said the sultanates were still in the capital, still looting. The destruction was systematic. They were smashing the temples, defacing the images of the gods, pulling down the pillars of the great hall. They had found the royal treasury, what Tirumala had left behind, and they were dividing the spoils. The city was a ruin.

Another trader said the horse shipment had been intercepted. The Sultanate cavalry had caught the caravan on the road, killed the grooms, taken the horses. Mallana's debt to João da Silva was still due.

Another said that Kondama had reached Goa safely. He had sold his goods and was living in a Portuguese house, waiting for the war to end.

Mallana listened and said nothing.

One night, a moonless night, he made his decision.

"The gold is still there," he said to Nagamma. "I am going to get it."

"Now?"

"Now. While the sultanates are still in the city. While the roads are dangerous. They will not expect anyone to go toward the city. Everyone is running away."

"The city is occupied."

"I know the streets. I know the alleys. I know the shop. I can move at night. I can be in and out in two days."

Nagamma was silent for a long time. Then she said: "Take Chinna."

"No."

"He is fifteen. He is old enough to travel. You said so yourself."

"That was before."

"Before what?"

"Before I decided to walk into a city full of enemy soldiers."

She did not argue. She did not cry. She took his face in her hands, the same hands that had pounded spices and sold silk and carried the household gods out of a burning city, and she kissed him on the forehead.

"Come back," she said.

"I will."

He left that night. He walked north, alone, with his sword and a bag of food and a water jar. The road was dark and quiet. The stars were hidden behind clouds. He walked all night and slept in a dry irrigation ditch during the day, and he walked again the next night.

He reached the outskirts of the city on the third day.

The city was a corpse.

He had lived here for forty-five years. He had been born in a house three streets from the great temple. He had learned to walk on these streets. He had bought his first horse in this market. He had married Nagamma in this temple. He had held Chinna for the first time in this house.

He did not recognize it.

The great bazaar street was a field of ash and broken stone. The pillars of the shop pavilions were still standing — the stone was too heavy to knock down, too solid to burn — but the roofs were gone, the walls were gone, the goods were gone. The bodies had been removed, or the animals had taken them, and the street was quiet except for the wind.

He walked down the center of the road. His footsteps echoed. The pillars of his shop came into view — the three pillars, the yali carving, the one that leaned slightly to the left after the earthquake of '52. He stopped in front of them.

The shop was gone. The beams that had supported the roof were charred stumps. The counter was a pile of ash. The chest where he had kept his ledgers was smashed and empty. The ledgers were gone — the record of twenty-five years of trade, the names of his debtors and creditors, the contracts he had signed, all burned.

He went to the pillar with the yali. The carving was still there, the lion-faced beast with the elephant trunk, watching him with stone eyes. He knelt and pried up the stone.

The earth was undisturbed. He dug with his hands, and the dirt was cool and loose, and after a moment his fingers touched cloth. He pulled the bundle out.

It was heavy. Three hundred gold pagodas, wrapped in cloth, tied with a cord. He held it in his hands and felt the familiar weight — the weight of a decade of work, of a thousand bargains, of a life that was supposed to be secure.

He stood up. He tucked the bundle under his arm. He turned to leave.

And he stopped.

A tiger was sitting in the shadow of what had once been a jewel merchant's stall. It was a large male, its coat golden in the afternoon light, its eyes half-closed. It was watching him without fear — without interest, even, as if he were not worth the effort.

The city had already begun to return to wilderness. The gardens had grown wild. The canals were choked with weeds. The tigers had come down from the hills, drawn by the silence and the absence of people and the easy pickings of abandoned cattle.

Mallana stood still. The tiger did not move. It blinked slowly, once, and then it yawned, showing teeth that could tear his throat out, and then it looked away.

He walked.

He walked south, past the broken pillars of his street, past the defaced temple, past the empty houses, past the white bones that lay bleaching in the sun. He walked with the weight of three hundred pagodas under his arm, and the tiger did not follow.

Behind him, the city of Vijayanagara lay silent under the sky, waiting for the monsoon rains to wash the ash into the river, waiting for the jungle to swallow the stone, waiting to become a story that travelers would tell in disbelief — a city of five hundred thousand souls, vanished into dust.

Ahead of him, on a road he had walked a thousand times, his son was learning to write ledgers, and his wife was selling silk to the wives of a diminished court, and somewhere in the south, in a town he had never seen, there was a shop with his name on it, waiting to be built.
