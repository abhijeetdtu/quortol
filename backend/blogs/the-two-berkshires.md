# The Two Berkshires: $839 Million in Visitor Spending and the Towns Still Waiting

## 1. The Lawn at Dusk

The gates open at five, but those who take their pleasures seriously arrive by mid-afternoon. They come through the Lion Gate at Tanglewood with their picnic baskets and folding chairs, their bottles of Sancerre wrapped in linen, their cheese boards composed with the care of a Dutch still life. By the time the Boston Symphony Orchestra's first violins begin tuning, the lawn holds 18,000 people — more than the entire year-round population of Lenox, Stockbridge, and Richmond combined.

On a July evening in 2024, when James Taylor played his annual Fourth of Weekend show, the Koussevitzky Music Shed and its surrounding acres reached exactly that capacity. The license plates told the story the Berkshires, on its best days, likes to tell about itself: New York, Connecticut, New Jersey, Florida, a scattering of California. The hotel rooms within a twenty-mile radius were booked solid weeks in advance. At the nearby Red Lion Inn, guests paid peak-season rates and considered themselves fortunate to have secured a cancellation.

The 2024 season at Tanglewood drew 304,000 ticketed visitors across 121 performances — up nearly 4 percent from the year before [Berkshire Eagle — Tanglewood 2024 attendance](https://www.berkshireeagle.com/news/central_berkshires/tanglewood-season-attendance-2024/article_9f4c71f6-75d1-11ef-a975-e34a568b7c0a.html). The 2025 season, now underway, is the largest in the venue's history: 187 performances, with Paul Simon, Brandi Carlile, Hugh Jackman, and Carrie Underwood among the headliners [Live 959 — Tanglewood security changes / record season](https://live959.com/tanglewood-lenox-security-bag-check/).

This is the Berkshires the outside world knows: "America's Premier Cultural Resort," the summer home of the Boston Symphony, a landscape of world-class museums and Gilded Age estates where the air smells of pine and the sound of a cello drifts across a lake at sunset.

But there is another Berkshire County, and it tells a different story.

## 2. The Paradox, in Numbers

In 2024, visitors to Berkshire County spent $839.3 million. That money supported 6,530 direct tourism jobs, generated $202.2 million in earnings, and produced $72.4 million in state and local tax revenue [Dean Runyan Associates / Mass.gov MOTT — 2024 Economic Impact of Travel](https://www.visitma.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2024p_MA_TravelImpacts_8.22.25.pdf). The county's concentration of arts, entertainment, and recreation employment is more than double the national average — a location quotient of 2.39 [MassHire Berkshire Regional Workforce Blueprint, 2024](https://www.mass.gov/doc/berkshire-regional-workforce-blueprint/download). For hotels and accommodations, the concentration is nearly triple: 2.89.

By any measure a tourist board would think to apply, the cultural economy is thriving.

And yet Berkshire County is shrinking. The 2020 Census counted 129,026 residents. By July 2025, the U.S. Census Bureau estimates the population had fallen to 128,224 — a decline of 0.6 percent in just five years [U.S. Census Bureau — Berkshire County QuickFacts (V2025)](https://www.census.gov/quickfacts/fact/table/berkshirecountymassachusetts/PST045224). The decade before that was no better: the county lost 2,193 people between 2010 and 2020 [Berkshire Benchmarks — 2020 Census Summary](https://berkshirebenchmarks.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/2020-Census-Summary.pdf).

The median age in Berkshire County is 47.8 — significantly older than the national median of 38.8. In the town of Alford, the median age is 61.3. In Stockbridge, it is 59.6 [Berkshire Benchmarks — 2020 Census Summary](https://berkshirebenchmarks.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/2020-Census-Summary.pdf). The arithmetic is unsparing: people are dying faster than babies are being born, and natural decrease — more deaths than births — is the primary driver of population loss [USAFacts — Berkshire County population](https://usafacts.org/answers/is-the-population-growing-or-shrinking/county/berkshire-county-ma/).

The region's own workforce blueprint projects 8 percent population growth by 2033, but that projection depends on attracting thousands of new residents and building 23,000 housing units — neither of which is happening at the required pace [MassHire Berkshire Blueprint, 2024](https://www.mass.gov/doc/berkshire-regional-workforce-blueprint/download).

The gap between the Berkshires the world sees and the Berkshires that year-round residents inhabit is not a contradiction. It is a transformation — from an industrial economy to a cultural one — that remains incomplete.

## 3. From Mills to Museums

The transformation did not happen overnight. To understand what the Berkshires have become, one must first reckon with what they were.

In 1957, manufacturing accounted for 59.3 percent of all nonagricultural employment in Berkshire County. Nearly six out of every ten workers made things — textiles, paper, electrical equipment, shoes, machinery [Massachusetts State Archives — "Tourism industries in Berkshire County, 1979"](https://archives.lib.state.ma.us/server/api/core/bitstreams/4f0d11be-f3fa-4a4d-8221-18d55cdbc036/content). The Housatonic River, which had powered sawmills and woolen mills since the eighteenth century, still drove the economy.

General Electric arrived in Pittsfield in 1903, drawn by the same water power and rail access that had built the textile mills before it. By mid-century, GE's Pittsfield Works employed more than 13,000 people — roughly a quarter of the city's workforce — manufacturing power transformers shipped around the world [Berkshire Eagle — GE demolition, 2024](https://www.berkshireeagle.com/news/central_berkshires/general-electric-plans-to-demolish-building-12-building-14-at-power-transformer-site/article_8788afb0-beef-11ee-a223-2375ca9712bf.html); [ETHW — "Transformers at Pittsfield"](https://ethw.org/Archives:Transformers_at_Pittsfield,_part_1).

In North Adams, Sprague Electric Company grew from a wartime manufacturer of gas masks, bomb casings, and atomic bomb components into a major electronics research and development center. At its peak in the 1970s and early 1980s, Sprague employed over 4,000 people at its 28-building, 16-acre complex on Marshall Street [CLUI — Mass MoCA / Sprague Electric site](https://clui.org/ludb/site/mass-moca-sprague-electric-site).

The paper mills of Lee and the textile mills of Adams gave the county an industrial identity that lasted 150 years. Berkshire-Hathaway's Mill Number Four in Adams — the last cotton mill in western Massachusetts — had employed 1,000 people until it closed in 1958 [MassMoments — Berkshire-Hathaway mill closing](https://www.massmoments.org/moment-details/berkshire-hathaway-announces-mill-closing.html).

Then, in the space of a few decades, almost all of it was gone.

The decline was not gradual in any terms that matter to the people who lived through it. Between 2001 and 2011 alone, manufacturing employment in Berkshire County fell by 45 percent — worse than the national decline of 30 percent [UMass Donahue Institute — Berkshire Advanced Manufacturing Study](https://donahue.umass.edu/documents/Berkshire_Advanced_Manufacturing_Study..pdf). Over 18 manufacturing companies closed their doors, eliminating 2,700 more jobs. GE's power transformer division shut down in 1986. Sprague Electric ceased operations in 1985.

By 2011, manufacturing provided just 8.8 percent of all employment in the Berkshires [UMass Donahue — Advanced Manufacturing Study](https://donahue.umass.edu/documents/Berkshire_Advanced_Manufacturing_Study..pdf).

What filled the void — slowly, deliberately, over the course of three decades — was the creative economy.

The Massachusetts Museum of Contemporary Art opened in 1999 in the abandoned Sprague Electric complex in North Adams, transforming 26 brick mill buildings into one of the largest contemporary art museums in the world. Joseph Thompson, MASS MoCA's founding director, had spent years raising money from public and private sources, winning a keystone state grant in 1988, surviving a recession, and reimagining the museum through what he later described as "a seemingly interminable birthing process" [MASS MoCA — "Transition Decade of Decision"](https://massmoca.org/transition-decade-of-decision-1989-1999-sprague-electric-mass-moca/).

By the time it opened, MASS MoCA had become a national model of post-industrial reinvention. A Williams College economic impact study estimated that the museum's annual expenditures combined with visitor spending generated approximately $16.7 million in economic activity and supported about 209 local jobs [Williams College — MASS MoCA Economic Impact Summary](https://web.williams.edu/Economics/ArtsEcon/library/pdfs/MassMocaSummary.pdf).

Tanglewood had been drawing audiences since 1937. The Clark Art Institute in Williamstown opened in 1955. The Norman Rockwell Museum in Stockbridge followed in 1969. Slowly, the Berkshire economy — once built on what people made — was being rebuilt on what people came to see.

## 4. The Jobs That Replace Jobs

No one disputes that tourism supports a significant number of Berkshire residents. The 6,530 direct tourism jobs span hotels, restaurants, retail, transportation, and the arts. Total tourism-related earnings reached $202.2 million in 2024 [Dean Runyan / MOTT 2024 Report](https://www.visitma.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2024p_MA_TravelImpacts_8.22.25.pdf).

But the arithmetic of replacement is unsparing. GE's Pittsfield plant alone employed more than twice the number of people that the entire tourism sector in Berkshire County now supports — and those were union-scale manufacturing jobs with benefits and middle-class wages.

The average tourism job in the Berkshires pays approximately $31,000 annually — more than a minimum-wage service job, but less than what you need to buy a home in a county where the median house price reached $318,000 in 2024 [UMass Donahue — Berkshire housing study](https://donahue.umass.edu/news-events/media-coverage/berkshire-edge-study-reveals-berkshire-county-and-western-massachusetts-have-long-way-to-go-toward-solving-housing-crisis). The median household income is $76,013 — well below the Massachusetts state median of $104,828 [Census Reporter — Berkshire County](http://censusreporter.org/profiles/05000US25003-berkshire-county-ma/).

Maynard Seider, an activist sociologist who spent decades studying the region, distilled the central question into the title of his book: *The Gritty Berkshires*. In its final chapter, he examines MASS MoCA as a case study and asks: "Can a tourist-service economy provide a meaningful and economically sustainable life for its residents?" [The Gritty Berkshires — Maynard Seider](http://www.thegrittyberkshires.com/)

The evidence, so far, is mixed.

## 5. The Second Berkshire

Drive forty minutes north from Tanglewood, and you arrive in North Adams. The city of 13,000 sits in a mountain bowl where the Hoosic River cuts through the northern Berkshires — isolated by the same geography that once made it an industrial center.

North Adams lost 3.6 percent of its population between April 2020 and July 2024 — one of the steepest declines of any municipality in Massachusetts [UMass Donahue Institute — V2024 Municipal Population Estimates](https://donahue.umass.edu/documents/UMDI_Census_V2024_Massachusetts_MCD_Pop_Estimates_Summary.pdf). Its median age of 42.6 is younger than the county average but still older than the national figure. And the jobs that Sprague Electric once provided — 4,000 at the company's peak — have not been replaced by anything comparable.

MASS MoCA is a triumph of adaptive reuse — a stunning museum that has drawn international attention and become a cultural anchor for the region. But a museum that primarily attracts visitors from beyond the county — 80 percent of its attendees come from outside Berkshire County — cannot function as a local employer the way a factory once did. Its total economic impact of $16.7 million and 209 jobs, while significant, remains a fraction of what Sprague provided [Williams College — MASS MoCA Economic Impact](https://web.williams.edu/Economics/ArtsEcon/library/pdfs/MassMocaSummary.pdf).

Across the county, the pattern repeats. Of Berkshire County's 32 towns and cities, only 25 percent gained population in the 2024 estimates. The fastest-growing towns are the rural retreats favored by second-home owners; the declining ones are the former mill and factory towns where year-round residents actually live [UMass Donahue Institute — V2024 Estimates](https://donahue.umass.edu/documents/UMDI_Census_V2024_Massachusetts_MCD_Pop_Estimates_Summary.pdf).

The pandemic briefly interrupted this trajectory. Between 2020 and 2021, a wave of remote workers and second-home buyers moved to the Berkshires, drawn by space and scenery and the sudden ability to work from anywhere. The population ticked upward. USPS data showed a more than doubling of moves into the region [Berkshire Regional Planning Commission — 2023-2027 CEDS](https://berkshireplanning.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/2023-2027-Berkshire-County-CEDS.pdf).

But by 2023, the trend had reversed. Domestic migration returned to its pre-pandemic patterns. In the 2023-2024 estimate year, Berkshire County lost another 137 residents [UMass Donahue — 2024 County Population Summary](https://donahue.umass.edu/documents/UMDI_2024_County_Pop_Summary_2025.03.13.pdf).

The pandemic bump was a sugar high. The underlying condition — an aging population, a shortage of younger workers, a housing market that serves the wealthy far better than it serves the employed — remained untreated.

## 6. The Housing Trap

The same tourism economy that generates $839 million in spending also generates a housing crisis.

Nearly 9,000 homes in Berkshire County are classified as second homes — properties occupied only part of the year by people whose primary residence lies elsewhere [Berkshire Regional Planning Commission — Survey of Second Homeowners](https://berkshireplanning.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Survey-of-Second-Homeowners-Results-220825.pdf). Another 2,700 short-term rental units are active in western Massachusetts, two-thirds of which are located in Berkshire County [UMass Donahue — Berkshire housing study](https://donahue.umass.edu/news-events/media-coverage/berkshire-edge-study-reveals-berkshire-county-and-western-massachusetts-have-long-way-to-go-toward-solving-housing-crisis). Every short-term rental is a long-term rental that does not exist.

The result is entirely predictable. Rents in Berkshire County increased 35 percent between January 2021 and October 2024. The median sale price of a single-family home, adjusted for inflation, rose from $225,260 in 2014 to $318,000 in 2024 — a 41 percent increase [UMass Donahue — Berkshire housing study](https://donahue.umass.edu/news-events/media-coverage/berkshire-edge-study-reveals-berkshire-county-and-western-massachusetts-have-long-way-to-go-toward-solving-housing-crisis). Homeowner vacancy dropped from 1.6 percent in 2018 to 0.7 percent in 2023, meaning virtually every available home was occupied or sold.

In December 2024, the state of Massachusetts designated eight Berkshire County towns — Alford, Becket, Hancock, Monterey, Mount Washington, Otis, Stockbridge, and Tyringham — as "seasonal communities," a formal acknowledgment that seasonal housing units comprise more than 40 percent of their total housing stock [Berkshire Eagle — Seasonal communities designation](https://www.berkshireeagle.com/news/local/seasonal-communities-berkshire-county/article_80f7ba9e-b8ac-11ef-bd9f-2f5fc983fc35.html). The designation grants access to specialized policies and grants, but it also acknowledges a fundamental imbalance: in these towns, housing exists primarily for people who do not live here year-round.

The Berkshire Regional Planning Commission's Survey of Second Homeowners in 2022 provided a revealing portrait. The typical second-home owner is wealthy, educated, and white, with a primary residence in New York City. Forty percent come from the New York metropolitan area. Over half are employed full-time outside Berkshire County. Only 3 percent work for a county employer [BRPC — Survey of Second Homeowners](https://berkshireplanning.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Survey-of-Second-Homeowners-Results-220825.pdf).

Twenty percent of second-home owners said they plan to move to the Berkshires permanently in the future, most of them as retirees. But retirees do not fill workforce gaps. They do not staff the restaurants, clean the hotel rooms, or become the next generation of public school students.

## 7. The Question That Remains

In April 2026, the 1Berkshire Economic Development team reported that they had held nearly 100 business consultations so far that year, written 31 letters of support that helped secure $460,000 in funding for local businesses, and hosted 189 unique job postings [1Berkshire — 2025 Economic Development Update](https://1berkshire.com/2025-update-from-economic-development-team/). They also launched "Belong Berkshires," a program designed to help transplants make social connections so they do not leave.

"Much like any rural place, this area has many tight-knit friend groups and families, and for someone without those connections, this can be a hard place to feel like you belong," the update noted. "That makes it harder for those people to find their niche here, and tends to result in some of them leaving despite the quality of life the region offers."

The region's challenges are not unique. Post-industrial communities across the Northeast — from upstate New York to western Pennsylvania to northern New England — are asking the same questions. Can cultural tourism replace manufacturing? Can a place built on making things survive on welcoming people? Can the creative economy support a middle class?

The Berkshires may be the best test case in America. The cultural assets are world-class. The natural beauty is genuinely spectacular. The marketing machine — 1Berkshire's 410 million visitor impressions in FY24 — is effective [1Berkshire — Marketing page](https://1berkshire.com/marketing/). The state is investing: $6.34 million in Destination Development Grants in FY26, including $180,000 for Berkshire Natural Resources Council trail improvements and $90,000 for the Berkshire Botanical Garden [Mass.gov — FY26 DDC Grants](https://www.mass.gov/news/massachusetts-awards-634-million-to-65-organizations-through-fy26-destination-development-grants).

But the fundamental question — "Can a tourist-service economy provide a meaningful and economically sustainable life for its residents?" — remains unanswered.

## 8. The Sound of What Comes Next

On a summer evening at Tanglewood, the question recedes. The music begins. The wine is poured. The sun sets behind Monument Mountain, and Stockbridge Bowl catches the last light.

This is the Berkshires that 304,000 people came to see in 2024. This is the Berkshires of the marketing campaigns, the guidebooks, the memories that visitors carry home with them.

It is real. The orchestra plays beautifully. The grass is soft. For a few hours, the place makes perfect sense.

But forty miles north, in the empty lots where General Electric's Building 12 and Building 14 were demolished earlier this year, the only sound is the wind through the chain-link fence. In Adams, where Berkshire-Hathaway's Mill Number Four has been converted into luxury apartments, the photographs of long-dead mill workers still line the hallways. And in North Adams, where Sprague Electric's 4,000 jobs became MASS MoCA's 209, the question hangs unanswered, as persistent as a half-remembered melody — what comes next for the people who live in the place that everyone visits?
