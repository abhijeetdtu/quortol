# The Game That Would Not Stand Still: Hydration Breaks and Football's Long History of Disruption

---

## The Reported Scene

The referee's whistle pierces the Atlanta humidity at 22 minutes and 14 seconds. Play stops. The crowd — 71,000 people who paid hundreds of dollars to watch Spain against Cape Verde in an air-conditioned stadium — begins to boo. On the pitch, players shuffle to the sidelines, towel off, sip from plastic bottles. Coaches gather their teams in tight circles, laptops open, diagrams drawn. A television network cuts to a commercial break.

This is the 2026 World Cup, and this is the new football: a game that pauses in the middle of each half, not because of injury or incident, but because the calendar says so. Three minutes. Twice per match. Every one of the 104 fixtures. Regardless of temperature, regardless of venue, regardless of whether the roof is open or closed.

"For every game, no matter where the games are played, no matter if there's a roof, temperature-wise, there will be a three-minute hydration break," said Manolo Zubiria, FIFA's chief tournament officer for the 2026 World Cup, in announcing the policy. [FIFA Inside](https://inside.fifa.com/organisation/news/hydration-breaks-world-cup-2026-player-welfare)

The stated rationale is player welfare. The surrounding controversy is about commerce, momentum, and the erosion of a game that has, for 160 years, defined itself by its continuity. France coach Didier Deschamps put it bluntly: "It's not two half-times, it is four quarter-times basically that we've got." [BBC Sport](https://www.bbc.com/sport/football/articles/cwye0zdm9j4o)

But if the hydration break feels like a rupture — an unprecedented intrusion of commerce and climate into the sacred architecture of 45-minute halves — it is worth asking: has football ever been any different?

The answer is no. The game has been rewritten, again and again, by crisis, by commerce, by climate, and by technology. The hydration break is merely the latest chapter in a story that has been unfolding since 1863.

---

## The 90-Minute Lie

The two-halves-of-45 structure feels like an eternal truth, as fixed as the goalposts themselves. It is not. The 45-minute half was formally adopted in 1897 — more than three decades after the Laws were first codified. [EL PAÍS](https://english.elpais.com/sports/2026-06-09/the-silent-change-in-soccer-from-45-minute-halves-to-four-quarters.html) Before that, matches were played to agreed time limits, or first goal, or simply until darkness fell.

And it has been altered many times since. The half-time interval, originally five minutes, was extended to ten, then fifteen. Stoppage time was formalized. Extra time was introduced for knockout matches. Every adjustment was seen, at the time, as a disruption. Each one was absorbed.

There is something almost touching about the belief that a game can be perfected and left alone. The hydration break is not the first pause to interrupt the 45-minute flow, and it will not be the last. But understanding why it feels so significant requires understanding the other disruptions that came before — the moments when football's lawmakers reached into the rulebook and changed the game.

## The Crisis That Changed Everything: 1990

Italia '90 is remembered for many things: Gazza's tears, Roger Milla's corner-flag dancing, Pavarotti's "Nessun dorma." It is not remembered for the quality of its football.

The 1990 World Cup produced 2.21 goals per match — the lowest average in the tournament's history, a record that still stands. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1990_FIFA_World_Cup) Sixteen red cards were shown. Knockout matches dissolved into cautious 120-minute stalemates decided by penalty shootouts. Argentina reached the final having scored just five goals in the entire tournament.

The nadir came on June 17, 1990, in a group-stage match between the Republic of Ireland and Egypt at Stadio Renzo Dall'Ara in Bologna. The game finished 0-0. Ireland manager Jack Charlton had built his team around a simple, brutally effective strategy: once in the lead — or even in a promising draw — his defenders would pass the ball back to goalkeeper Packie Bonner, who would pick it up, hold it, kill time. Egypt did the same. The result was described by one writer as "the worst match at the most boring World Cup in history." [Goal.com](https://www.goal.com/en/news/the-games-that-defined-modern-football-republic-of-ireland-0-0-egypt/10lty0rb3uf971ma3cdirzoova)

It is a delicious irony that the most boring match in World Cup history may also be the most consequential. Something had to give. Daniel Jeandupeux, a former Switzerland international managing French club Caen, watched the 1990 final with despair and wrote to FIFA with a proposal. His idea was simple: stop goalkeepers from handling deliberate back-passes from teammates. [The Athletic](https://www.nytimes.com/athletic/3397785/2022/07/20/thirty-years-of-the-backpass-rule/)

The International Football Association Board — IFAB, the eight-member body that has guarded the Laws of the Game since 1886 — trialled the change at the 1991 Under-17 World Championship in Italy. By May 1992, it was law. [The Athletic](https://www.nytimes.com/athletic/3397785/2022/07/20/thirty-years-of-the-backpass-rule/)

The immediate effect was chaos. Goalkeepers who had spent entire careers doing little more than shot-stopping were suddenly forced to become footballers. Defenders panicked. The comedy was vivid: Nottingham Forest defender Brian Laws was spotted kneeling down to head the ball back to his goalkeeper, a loophole FIFA quickly closed by deeming it "ungentlemanly conduct." [ESPN](https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/37479727/premier-league-chaos-backpass-law-invented-1992)

One does not often see grown men kneeling to head a ball to their own goalkeeper; having seen it, one understands why the lawmakers acted so swiftly. But the long-term effect was revolutionary. The back-pass rule is now widely regarded as the single best rule change in modern football. It accelerated the game, incentivized high pressing, and forced the evolution of the sweeper-keeper — the playmaking goalkeeper who could start attacks from the back. Without the back-pass rule, there is no modern possession football, no Pep Guardiola system, no Manuel Neuer sweeping behind a high defensive line. [Football365](https://www.football365.com/news/celebrating-30-years-of-the-back-pass-law-the-greatest-change-in-modern-football-history)

And it worked: after the 1990 nadir, World Cup goals per game recovered to 2.71 in 1994 and stabilized in the 2.5-2.7 range ever since. A game had to become nearly unwatchable before it could become watchable again.

## The Geometry of the Game: 1925

Sixty-seven years before the back-pass rule, another disruption redrew the tactical map of football.

In 1925, IFAB voted to change the offside law. Previously, a player needed three opponents between himself and the goal line to be onside. The new law required only two. [The Athletic](https://www.nytimes.com/athletic/6415406/2025/06/13/offside-rule-mccracken-ifab-newcastle/)

The change was driven by the same complaint that echoes today: the game had become too defensive. Newcastle's Bill McCracken had turned the old three-player offside trap into an art form, and goals were drying up. IFAB met in Paris in June 1925 and deleted the word "three," substituting "two."

The immediate result was an explosion of scoring. On the first day of the new law, Aston Villa beat Burnley 10-0. Across England, goals flowed. [The Athletic](https://www.nytimes.com/athletic/6415406/2025/06/13/offside-rule-mccracken-ifab-newcastle/)

But the deeper transformation was tactical. Arsenal manager Herbert Chapman, encouraged by his striker Charlie Buchan, moved his centre-half from midfield into a deeper defensive role, creating the "third back" system — the precursor to the modern center-back position. [The Athletic](https://www.nytimes.com/athletic/6415406/2025/06/13/offside-rule-mccracken-ifab-newcastle/)

The 1925 offside change is arguably the single most consequential rule revision in football history. It redrew the geometry of the pitch, created the roles we now take for granted, and shifted the balance between attack and defense. Every organized match played since has been shaped by that June afternoon in Paris — a meeting so obscure that one almost suspects the delegates did not fully grasp what they were doing. But they did it anyway, and football was better for it.

## From Zero to Five: The Substitution Revolution

For the first 35 years of the World Cup, there were no substitutions. If a player was injured, his team played on with ten — or nine, or eight. It was not until 1958 that IFAB officially added substitution to the Laws of the Game. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substitute_(association_football))

The 1970 World Cup in Mexico was the first to allow two substitutes. The first substitution in World Cup history was made by the Soviet Union against Mexico on May 31, 1970 — Anatoli Puzach replacing Viktor Serebryanikov at half-time. [FIFA](https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/articles/substitutions-substitutes-rule-changes-history)

From there, the number crept upward: two substitutes through 1990, two plus a goalkeeper in 1994, three from 1998 onward. Three substitutes became the global standard, and for two decades, it seemed settled.

Then came COVID-19.

When football resumed after the pandemic shutdown in 2020, players faced an unprecedented fixture crunch. IFAB approved a temporary amendment allowing up to five substitutions per match, with a maximum of three stoppages in play to make them. [IFAB](https://www.theifab.com/news/five-substitutes-option-temporarily-allowed-for-competition-organisers/)

The change was meant to be temporary. It was extended once, twice, three times. In 2022, IFAB made it permanent. [FIFA](https://inside.fifa.com/innovation/media-releases/the-ifab-permanently-approves-five-substitute-option-in-top-level)

A pandemic-driven disruption — one that seemed, at first, like an emergency measure — had permanently changed the fabric of the game. Managers now had the equivalent of an entire second team to deploy. The tactical substitution, once a late-game gamble, became a structural component of match planning. Nothing is so permanent as a temporary measure that proves useful to those in power.

The parallel to hydration breaks is unmistakable: an emergency response to an external pressure (COVID / heat) becomes normalized, then permanent, then expanded.

## Climate Forces the Calendar

The 2022 World Cup in Qatar was the first November-to-December World Cup in the tournament's history — not by choice, but by necessity. [AP News](https://apnews.com/general-news-23ffeae71dab4ea583379a1965933898) Summer temperatures in Doha regularly exceed 40°C and can reach 50°C. Two FIFA feasibility studies in 2014 and 2015 concluded that a summer tournament was not viable. The tournament was moved, upending domestic seasons across Europe.

It was the first time climate had forced a World Cup to change its calendar. It will not be the last. One suspects that future historians will look back on the winter World Cup in Qatar the way we look back on the first offside change: as the moment when external reality finally caught up with administrative convenience.

Research published in the International Journal of Biometeorology in January 2025 examined heat risk at the 16 stadiums hosting the 2026 World Cup across the United States, Mexico, and Canada. Led by Dr. Donal Mullan of Queen's University Belfast, the study found that 14 of the 16 host cities could exceed a Wet Bulb Globe Temperature of 28°C — the threshold at which some governing bodies recommend match postponement. [Springer/International Journal of Biometeorology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00484-025-02852-4)

Miami and Monterrey pose the greatest risk, with no air-conditioned stadiums. But Dallas, Houston, Kansas City, Boston, Philadelphia, and New York also face significant heat challenges. Even in an average summer, more than 80% of June and July days in Dallas and Houston exceed 28°C WBGT. [BBC Sport](https://www.bbc.com/sport/football/articles/c7vd85gl91lo)

In May 2026, a coalition of scientists and medical experts sent an open letter to FIFA urging stronger heat protections — including doubling hydration breaks to six minutes. The three-minute standard, they argued, was based on idealized laboratory conditions and might not provide sufficient cooling in real-world match scenarios. [AP News](https://apnews.com/article/fifa-world-cup-hydration-breaks-minutes-heat-8fca3f5cb73cbbb15816b7a09fbda1ce)

It is a curious world in which scientists must lobby a football governing body for the right to drink more water. But here we are.

## The Technology That Would Not Wait

Climate is not the only external force reshaping the game. Technology has been pushing against the traditions of football for decades, and in recent years, the dam has broken.

The pivotal moment came on June 27, 2010, in Bloemfontein, South Africa. England's Frank Lampard struck a shot that bounced off the crossbar and clearly crossed the goal line against Germany. The goal was not given. The error was seen by hundreds of millions of people around the world — and it was uncorrectable. [Central Fife Times](https://www.centralfifetimes.com/sport/national-sport/18545762.day-2010-frank-lampards-goal-changed-everything/)

FIFA had resisted goal-line technology for years. IFAB had rejected trials just three months before the tournament. But the Lampard incident — and a parallel incident involving Ukraine's Marko Devic against England in the same tournament — made the status quo untenable. By July 2012, IFAB had approved goal-line technology. By 2014, it was in use at the World Cup. [WIPO](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/wipo-magazine/articles/goal-line-technology-getting-it-right-37357)

The goal-line technology decision opened a door that could not be closed. Video Assistant Referees (VAR) followed in 2018. Semi-automated offside technology (SAOT) debuted in 2022. [ABC News Australia](https://www.abc.net.au/news/science/2022-12-16/world-cup-2022-qatar-var-offside-technology/101762934)

At the 2026 World Cup, the technological apparatus has reached a level of sophistication that would have seemed like science fiction a decade ago. Sixteen cameras per stadium track 1,248 pre-scanned player avatars. The match ball — Adidas's "Trionda" — contains a sensor measuring movement 500 times per second. AI sends offside alerts directly to the assistant referee's earpiece. Out-of-bounds technology can determine, with millimeter precision, whether the ball has left the field of play. [The Athletic](https://www.nytimes.com/athletic/7328008/2026/06/02/world-cup-technology-officiating-changes-explained/) [The Independent](https://www.independent.co.uk/sport/football/world-cup-2026-ref-cam-ai-technology-b3000317.html)

Each of these innovations was opposed at some point. Each was sold as a necessary correction. Each changed the game in ways both intended and unintended. And each was eventually accepted with the same weary resignation that the hydration break will one day receive.

## The Commercial Whistle

The hydration break controversy crystallizes a tension that has simmered for decades. When FIFA announced in March 2026 that broadcasters would be allowed to sell advertising during the mandatory breaks, the commercial motivation became explicit. [ESPN](https://www.espn.com/soccer/story/_/id/48945011/why-there-drinks-breaks-2026-world-cup-fifa-criticised)

FOX, the U.S. broadcaster, ran commercials during both hydration breaks in the opening match — and at one point missed live action when it failed to return before play resumed. FIFA did not penalize them. [Sofascore](https://www.sofascore.com/news/the-present-and-potential-future-for-fifas-three-minute-hydration-breaks)

The breaks have already changed how the game is played. United States women's coach Emma Hayes called them "momentum breaks," noting that stopping play benefits the team on the back foot. [BBC Sport](https://www.bbc.com/sport/football/articles/c621620wr77o) In the tournament's early matches, goals were scored shortly after hydration breaks with striking frequency — Canada equalized after one, Scotland scored the winner after one, Curacao's historic lead against Germany evaporated after the Germans used the pause to regroup.

But critics who see this as a rupture — a betrayal of the game's essence — might ask themselves: when has football ever been pure? The 1925 offside change was driven by the same commercial pressure to make the game more entertaining. The 1992 back-pass rule was a response to falling audiences. The 2020 five-substitute rule was a concession to the pandemic's disruption of the fixture calendar.

The game has never been pure. It has only ever been popular. And popularity, as any cynic will tell you, has its price.

## The IFAB's Slow Burn

All of these changes flow through one institution: the International Football Association Board. Founded in 1886, IFAB predates FIFA itself. Its eight voting members — the four British associations with one vote each, FIFA with four — have guarded the Laws of the Game for 140 years. [IFAB](https://theifab.com/organisation/)

A three-quarters majority (six of eight votes) is required to change the laws. This means FIFA cannot act alone; it needs at least two of the British associations to agree. The structure was designed to prevent rapid, reactionary change — and for most of its history, it succeeded.

But the pace has accelerated. From 1863 to 1990, the Laws changed slowly, incrementally. Since 1992 — the back-pass rule — the rate of change has increased dramatically. Goal-line technology, VAR, five substitutes, concussion protocols, semi-automated offside, hydration breaks, expanded World Cup rosters. Each change builds on the last.

The hydration break, notably, did not require an IFAB law change. IFAB allows competition organizers to implement cooling breaks of 90 seconds to three minutes "in certain meteorological conditions (high temperature and humidity)." FIFA interpreted this — or, critics would say, stretched it — to mandate breaks in all matches regardless of conditions. [IFAB Laws of the Game](https://www.footballrules.com/game-events/drinks-cooling-breaks/)

CONMEBOL followed suit in March 2026, implementing mandatory 90-second rehydration breaks in the Copa Libertadores and Copa Sudamericana. [EL PAÍS](https://english.elpais.com/sports/2026-06-09/the-silent-change-in-soccer-from-45-minute-halves-to-four-quarters.html)

The precedent is now set. What begins as a World Cup emergency measure can become a continental standard, then a global expectation. A rule is only as permanent as the next crisis that overturns it.

---

## The Game That Changes

The 2026 World Cup is the biggest and hottest in history: 48 teams, 104 matches, played across three nations in the northern summer. The hydration breaks are a response to that reality — to the carbon intensity of modern football, to the commercial demands of a global broadcast, to the physical limits of the human body in a warming climate.

Fans boo in Atlanta, where the air conditioning hums and the temperature is a pleasant 22°C. They boo in Boston, where the weather is overcast and mild. They boo because the pause feels wrong. It breaks the spell. It reminds them that the game they love is not a timeless ritual but a constructed thing, subject to revision by remote committees and commercial imperatives.

But the game has always been a constructed thing. The Laws of the Game are not etched on stone tablets. They are amended in conference rooms in Zurich, Belfast, and Cardiff. The 45-minute half is a convention, not a commandment. The back-pass ban was once heresy. The five-substitute rule was once an emergency measure. Offside was once defined by three opponents, not two.

The hydration break is the latest disruption. It will be absorbed, as all the others were. The game will be different. It was always going to be.

The question is not whether football will survive this change. It is what change will come next, and what crisis — climate, commercial, or medical — will justify it. The game changes so that it may remain the same. It has always been thus.

## Key Facts

- **First mandatory hydration breaks in World Cup history**: 3 minutes at 22nd minute of each half, all 104 matches, regardless of temperature or venue
- **First cooling breaks**: 2014 World Cup, Netherlands vs Mexico in Fortaleza, 39°C (102°F), at referee's discretion
- **First World Cup played in winter**: Qatar 2022 (November-December), moved from June-July due to extreme heat
- **Record low goals per game**: 2.21 at 1990 World Cup, triggered 1992 back-pass rule
- **Offside rule change**: 1925, from 3 defenders to 2, triggering tactical revolution
- **Substitution evolution**: 0 (1930-1954), 2 (1970-1990), 3 (1998-2018), 5 (2022-present)
- **Goal-line technology**: Approved 2012, first used at 2014 World Cup — response to Lampard ghost goal (2010)
- **IFAB governance**: 8 votes (4 British associations + 4 FIFA), 6 needed to change laws
