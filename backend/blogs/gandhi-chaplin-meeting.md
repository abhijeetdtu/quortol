# Two Little Men: When Gandhi Met Charlie Chaplin in the East End

**September 22, 1931. A twelve-foot room in Canning Town. Ten thousand people outside. Two of the most famous men on earth, neither of whom knew who the other was.**

## I.

In the autumn of 1931, two celebrated little men descended upon London, and the city — which flattered itself that nothing could still astonish it — was obliged to think again.

One had come from India, a nation of three hundred million souls whom he was patiently teaching to unlearn their fear of the British Empire. He wore a hand-spun loincloth and a shawl, carried nothing of any particular value beyond a walking stick and a dollar watch, and lived in a bare cell at a community settlement in Bow, sleeping on a mat on the floor with the door left open so the poor might enter as freely as the draught. His Majesty's Government had offered him a suite at the Hilton and a fleet of cars. He declined both. [BBC Radio 4 Making History](https://www.bbc.co.uk/radio4/history/making_history/makhist10_prog1a.shtml)

The other had been born in this very city — in Walworth, in 1889, above a public house — and had risen from the workhouses of Lambeth to become the most universally recognizable human being ever committed to film. He had returned to London for the premiere of his new picture, *City Lights*, and was spending his days in a sort of pilgrimage among the great minds of the age: George Bernard Shaw, H. G. Wells, Winston Churchill, Albert Einstein. [Chaplin, *My Autobiography*](https://www.gandhi-manibhavan.org/ebooks/excerpt-charles-chaplin.html)

The first was Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi, known to the world as the Mahatma. The second was Charlie Chaplin. On the evening of September 22, 1931, they sat down together on a sofa in a twelve-foot upstairs room in a doctor's house on Beckton Road, Canning Town, and talked for one hour about machinery, empire, prayer, and the nature of freedom. The meeting made the front page of every newspaper in London, was captured on Pathé newsreel, and would — by Chaplin's own account — help inspire the most politically charged film of his career.

## II.

The telegram arrived at Kingsley Hall while Gandhi was preparing to leave London. The Second Round Table Conference had been a disappointment: three months of debating the rights of minorities and the mechanics of constitutional reform while the central question of independence was laid aside with the sort of courtesy one reserves for matters one has no intention of addressing. Gandhi was tired, and he was packing.

The name on the telegram meant nothing to him.

"One of my clearest mental pictures," wrote Muriel Lester, Gandhi's hostess at Kingsley Hall and a Christian pacifist who had first visited him in India five years earlier, "is of Mr. Gandhi sitting with a telegram in his hand looking distinctly puzzled. Grouped round him were secretaries awaiting his answer. As I came in, the silence was being broken by a disapproving voice saying: 'But he's only a buffoon, there is no point in going to meet him.'" [Muriel Lester, *Entertaining Gandhi* (1932)](https://newhamstory.com/node/806)

The telegram was from Charlie Chaplin. Gandhi's secretary was handing it back for a polite refusal when Lester saw the name.

"'But don't you know that name, Bapu?' I inquired, immensely intrigued. 'No,' he answered, taking back the flimsy form and looking at me for the enlightenment that his secretaries could not give."

"'Charlie Chaplin! He's the world's hero. You simply must meet him. His art is rooted in the life of working people, he understands the poor as well as you do, he honors them always in his pictures.'" [Lester, *Entertaining Gandhi*](https://newhamstory.com/node/806)

Gandhi was persuaded. A friend, Dr. Chuni Lal Katial — an Indian physician and health pioneer who would later become the United Kingdom's first South Asian mayor — offered his home at 45 Beckton Road in Canning Town for the meeting. The arrangements were made. The time was set for 6:15 p.m.

The British public began arriving at 4:45.

## III.

The *Daily Express* dispatched a reporter whose account of the scene remains the most vivid contemporary record of what it actually looked like when two mythological figures collided in a slum.

The meeting, the *Express* noted, was meant to be "just a quiet and informal chat." [*Daily Express*, September 23, 1931](https://web.archive.org/web/20110123053820/www.newhamstory.com/node/2523)

"The British public arrived about 4:45 p.m. They began by breaking the railings by accident. They were perfectly good-humored, but heavy on the brickwork. Then a portion of a neighboring roof disappeared, and over the garden wall appeared several score of enthusiasts."

By the time Chaplin's taxi pulled up, some two thousand people had filled Beckton Road. Six policemen attempted crowd control. "It was pathetic to watch them," the *Express* observed.

Chaplin, wearing a bowler hat and a smart suit — nothing like the Little Tramp, whom Gandhi had never seen — paid his driver and sized up the situation. "Mr. Chaplin hung on to his bowler-hat and charged for the door."

Gandhi arrived moments later in a police motorcar. The crowd surged. "About two thousand happy enthusiasts tried to pat Mr. Gandhi and Mr. Chaplin on the back," the *Express* wrote. "Twenty missed their pats by a few inches. One man held up a large packet of salt which he wanted to give to Mr. Gandhi."

The door opened. "Mr. Gandhi and Mr. Chaplin made the quickest entry into a house I have ever seen. They went through that door like bullets."

Inside, photographers packed both floors. The two men appeared at an upstairs window together, waving. The crowd responded with chants that the Pathé newsreel would preserve: "Good old Charlie! Good old Gandhi!" [British Pathé Newsreel, 1931](https://www.listal.com/video/10703586)

## IV.

In the twelve-foot room, the noise of the crowd faded. Chaplin sat at one end of the sofa. Gandhi sat at the other. An admiring audience waited for them to speak. The silence grew leaden. Someone, at last, cleared the room.

Chaplin later described the moment with the devastating honesty of a performer who has forgotten his lines:

"Now came that uneasy, terrifying moment when I should say something astutely intelligent upon a subject I knew little about." [Chaplin, *My Autobiography*](https://www.gandhi-manibhavan.org/ebooks/excerpt-charles-chaplin.html)

He had rehearsed. But a young woman beside him was telling him a long story, and a more commanding voice — that of Sarojini Naidu, the poet and Indian political leader who had accompanied Gandhi — cut through: "Miss, will you kindly finish your conversation and let Mr. Chaplin talk to Gandhi?"

The packed room went silent. Chaplin felt "all India was also waiting on my words." He cleared his throat.

"'Naturally, I am in sympathy with India's aspirations and struggle for freedom,' I said. 'Nonetheless, I am somewhat confused by your abhorrence of machinery.'" [Chaplin, *My Autobiography*](https://www.gandhi-manibhavan.org/ebooks/excerpt-charles-chaplin.html)

It was the question Chaplin had come to ask. Machinery, to Chaplin's socialist instincts, meant liberation: shorter hours, more leisure, release from the bondage of brute labor. He had grown up in the nineteenth century and watched the twentieth transform the world. Why would anyone who cared about the poor oppose the one force that seemed capable of lifting them?

Gandhi nodded and smiled. He had been asked this question a hundred times. He answered with the calm patience of a man explaining something to a bright student who had almost grasped it for himself.

"'Machinery in the past has made us dependent on England, and the only way we can rid ourselves of that dependence is to boycott all goods made by machinery. That is why we have made it the patriotic duty of every Indian to spin his own cotton and weave his own cloth.'" [Chaplin, *My Autobiography*](https://www.gandhi-manibhavan.org/ebooks/excerpt-charles-chaplin.html)

This was not a Luddite argument, Gandhi explained. It was a strategic one. India had a different climate, different habits, different needs. "In England the cold weather necessitates arduous industry and an involved economy. You need the industry of eating utensils; we use our fingers." Mass production was a solution to a European problem. Imposed on India, it was a mechanism of colonial extraction.

Chaplin pressed further, and here the witness account of Mahadev Desai — Gandhi's secretary and the only journalist permitted in the room — takes up the story. Desai, who had heard Gandhi explain the same position a hundred times, watched with professional interest as the man who had made the world laugh sharpened his question to a blade.

"'So the question is confined only to India?' said Mr. Chaplin, grasping the point quickly. 'But supposing you had in India the independence of Russia, and you could find other work for your unemployed and ensure equable distribution of wealth, you would not then despise machinery? You would subscribe to shorter hours of work and more leisure for the worker?'" [Mahadev Desai, *Young India*, October 8, 1931](https://www.etvbharat.com/english/bharat/bharat-news/meeting-with-gandhiji-inspired-charlie-chaplin-to-make-his-classic-movie-modern-times/na20191005134745557)

"'Certainly,' said Gandhiji."

Desai, writing for the journal *Young India*, permitted himself a rare note of admiration: "The question has now been discussed with Gandhiji for the hundredth time, but I have not come across a lay foreigner who grasped the situation so quickly. The reason was perhaps his freedom from prejudice or prepossession and certainly his sympathy." [Desai, *Young India*](https://www.etvbharat.com/english/bharat/bharat-news/meeting-with-gandhiji-inspired-charlie-chaplin-to-make-his-classic-movie-modern-times/na20191005134745557)

## V.

The conversation drifted. Chaplin spoke of visiting a prison and discovering that he could face a room full of rich people but not the men behind bars. Gandhi responded with a remark that could serve as both men's epitaph:

"'Crime is a disease like any other and it should be treated not in prisons but in houses of correction.'" [Desai, *Young India*](https://www.etvbharat.com/english/bharat/bharat-news/meeting-with-gandhiji-inspired-charlie-chaplin-to-make-his-classic-movie-modern-times/na20191005134745557)

Then the room cleared, and Gandhi asked if Chaplin would care to stay for prayers.

He would.

"The Mahatma sat cross-legged on the floor while five others sat in a circle with him," Chaplin wrote. "It was a curious sight: six figures squatting on the floor in that small room, in the heart of the London slums, as a saffron sun was rapidly sinking behind the roof-tops, and myself sitting on a sofa looking down at them, while they humbly intoned their prayer." [Chaplin, *My Autobiography*](https://www.gandhi-manibhavan.org/ebooks/excerpt-charles-chaplin.html)

The comedian who had built an empire of laughter upon falling, tripping, straining against the inhumanity of machines, sat in stillness and watched an ascetic chant by gaslight in the East End. "What a paradox," he thought, "as I watched this extremely realistic man, with his astute legal mind and his profound sense of political reality, all of which seemed to vanish in a sing-song chant." [Chaplin, *My Autobiography*](https://www.gandhi-manibhavan.org/ebooks/excerpt-charles-chaplin.html)

He remained for the duration, kneeling with strict reverence.

## VI.

The meeting lasted about an hour. As Chaplin left, he told reporters: "He is one of the greatest men I have met." [*Daily Express*, September 23, 1931](https://web.archive.org/web/20110123053820/www.newhamstory.com/node/2523)

Later, writing in his autobiography, Chaplin refined that judgment into something more complex and more honest. Gandhi's legendary significance, he felt, "evaporated in the London scene" — the loincloth in the damp English climate seemed "incongruous." But the man himself had made an incision that would not heal. "I got a lucid object lesson in tactical maneuvering in India's fight for freedom," Chaplin wrote, "inspired, paradoxically, by a realistic, virile-minded visionary with a will of iron to carry it out." [Chaplin, *My Autobiography*](https://www.gandhi-manibhavan.org/ebooks/excerpt-charles-chaplin.html)

Five years later, Chaplin released *Modern Times* — a film about a factory worker devoured by the machinery he is paid to operate. The Tramp, at the end of his run, tightens bolts on an assembly line until his body convulses and he is pulled into the gears. It is a comedy. It is also the most devastating cinematic critique of industrial capitalism ever produced. The influence of that twelve-foot room in Canning Town — where a man in a loincloth had patiently explained that machines were not the enemy, but the empire that owned them was — runs through every frame.

Chaplin would go further. In 1940, he released *The Great Dictator*, a film whose final speech — a plea for decency, kindness, and the return of democracy — is shot through with the logic of nonviolent resistance. "I want to see the return of decency and kindness," Chaplin said upon the film's release. "I'm just a human being who wants to see this country a real democracy." [Royal Docks London](https://www.royaldocks.london/articles/when-chaplin-met-gandhi-in-canning-town-royal-docks-history) The Mahatma, who had never seen a Chaplin film, might not have laughed at *The Great Dictator*. But he could hardly have disagreed with it.

## VII.

Today, the house at 45 Beckton Road is gone — demolished in the mid-twentieth century as Canning Town was rebuilt after the war. The site is now a pocket park, the Gandhi Chaplin Memorial Garden, created in 2015 by Newham Council and the local community.

At each end of the park, mounted mosaics depict the two men who met there on a September evening ninety-five years ago. The *Stratford Express* captured the spirit of the moment in its headline the following week: "Two little men, both famous in his own sphere, met in the house of an Indian doctor in Beckton on Tuesday evening — Mr. Gandhi and Mr. Charles Chaplin." [Elin Morgan, OnLondon](https://www.onlondon.co.uk/elin-morgan-when-two-little-men-met-in-canning-town/)

Neither man quite knew what to expect from the other. One had never seen a film. The other had never spun cloth. They came from different centuries, different continents, different convictions about what a human life was for. And yet, in a cramped room above a crowded street, with the factory chimneys of East London smoking against the dusk, they found enough common ground to change the questions that each would carry forward.

Chaplin's son, Charles Chaplin Jr., who wrote his father's biography, said that his father admired Gandhi above almost any other figure because "he joined outcasts instead of living the comfortable life he could have had." [Madras Courier](https://madrascourier.com/insight/when-charlie-chaplin-met-gandhi/) That, perhaps, was the thread of sympathy that ran between the iconoclastic comedian and the half-naked ascetic: both had chosen the company of the poor, and both understood that the real machinery of the modern world was designed to crush them.

The two little men said goodbye in the September twilight. Police reserves arrived to escort them through the crowd. Gandhi went back to Kingsley Hall to sleep on his mat. Chaplin returned to his hotel, carrying an idea that would take five years to become a film. Neither would ever see the other again.
