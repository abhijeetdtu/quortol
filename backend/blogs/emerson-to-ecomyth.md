# The Transparent Eyeball and the Ecomyth: How New England Poets Learned to See

*From Emerson's "transparent eyeball" to Mary Oliver's daily walks to David Crews climbing Monadnock with Abenaki names in his pocket — a 190-year arc of poets who learned to see the natural world, and the climate-changed landscape that now demands a new kind of seeing.*

---

On a morning in October 2023, the poet David Crews stood on the summit of Mount Monadnock in southern New Hampshire — the second-most-climbed mountain on Earth after Mount Fuji, which tells you less about the mountain than about the species that climbs it — and tried to hold two ways of seeing the same place at once. In his notebook he carried the Abenaki name for the mountain — *Mônadenok*, "the place of the unearthed mountain" — and the 408-line poem that Ralph Waldo Emerson had written about the same peak in 1847, also titled "Monadnock." Crews was writing his own 408-line response, a revision and a reckoning. The mountain had not changed. The language around it had. And the climate that shaped the spruce and fir on its upper slopes had begun to shift in ways that would make Emerson's nineteenth-century vision, and perhaps Crews's twenty-first-century one, into a record of what had already passed [David Crews, Mônadenok](https://www.davidcrewspoetry.com/monadenok).

It is easy to mistake this for a local story — one poet, one mountain, one echo across 176 years. It is not. It is the story of a whole way of being in the world, born on a specific patch of North American ground, that has now run up against its own material limit. New England nature poetry — the tradition that runs from Emerson's ecstatic dissolution of self into landscape, through Thoreau's patient catalogues, through Dickinson's slanted natural history, through Frost's farmer's-eye negotiations with a rocky field, through Mary Oliver's exultant daily attention — is not merely a literary genre. It is a protocol for relationship. And the forests, ponds, birds, and seasons that gave it shape are now in retreat.

## I. The Transparent Eyeball (1836–1862)

The founding document of American nature poetry is not a poem at all. It is an essay published in 1836, when Ralph Waldo Emerson was thirty-three years old and still searching for a voice that could hold both the granite of New England and the ether of spirit. He called it simply *Nature*, and on its first page he issued a challenge to his generation: "Why should not we also enjoy an original relation to the universe? Why should not we have a poetry and philosophy of insight and not of tradition?" [Emerson, Nature (1836)](https://archive.vcu.edu/english/engweb/transcendentalism/authors/emerson/essays/naturetext.html).

The question was revolutionary. American literature in the 1830s was still derivative, still looking toward Europe for permission — a colony of the mind long after it had ceased to be a political one. Emerson proposed that a man walking in a New England wood could have an experience as valid as any Greek's on Parnassus — that the "mean and lowly things" of the Concord landscape were worthy of the same attention that Virgil had given to the Italian vales. "The sun shines to-day also," he wrote. "There is more wool and flax in the fields. There are new lands, new men, new thoughts. Let us demand our own works and laws and worship."

This was the seed. But the flower belonged to Henry David Thoreau, who took Emerson's philosophy and walked it into the woods — as philosophy always should be walked, if it is to be of any use. In *Walking*, his great essay on wildness, Thoreau wrote: "I wish to speak a word for Nature, for absolute Freedom and Wildness, as contrasted with a freedom and culture merely civil — to regard man as an inhabitant, or a part and parcel of Nature, rather than a member of society" [Thoreau, Walking (1862)](https://gutenberg.org/files/1022/1022-h/1022-h.htm). He wanted a poetry that would "nail words to their primitive senses, as farmers drive down stakes in spring, which the frost has heaved."

Thoreau got his wish in a way he could not have anticipated. The poetry that came out of New England over the next century did exactly what he asked — it nailed words to the visible world with such precision that the landscape itself became a language. When he surveyed Walden Pond in 1846 with "compass and chain and sounding line," he produced the first accurate map of that small, deep body of water [Walden Pond Survey, Concord Free Public Library](https://concordlibrary.org/special-collections/walden/list). He was making a map of a place. He was also, without knowing it, mapping a tradition.

## II. The Slanted Naturalist (1862–1886)

Emily Dickinson never left Amherst. She was born in 1830 in the same house where she would die fifty-six years later, in the Connecticut River Valley, a temperate deciduous forest of oak, maple, beech, and elm. From her window she watched the birds of that valley — robins, bobolinks, sparrows, phoebes, hummingbirds, orioles, crows — and she wrote about them with a precision that was both scientific and mystical, as if the microscope and the séance had somehow agreed upon a single method. Of the more than 250 bird species that nest in or pass through the Connecticut River Valley, she named a relatively small number. But she listened to all of them, and she understood that birdsong was "place-making" — a way of orienting a creature in space and time [Dickinson's Birds, Adagia](https://birds.adagia.org/project/about-overview).

Living on her father's fourteen-acre estate with its perennial gardens, orchard, conservatory, and woods, Dickinson was an observer of nature in an age before field recording. Her poems register seventy species of wildflower ("New feet within my garden go"), the behavior of winter snow ("It sifts from leaden sieves"), the precise aerodynamics of a hummingbird ("A Route of Evanescence"). She is sometimes called a nature poet, but the label misses the strangeness of her project. She was not celebrating nature. She was interrogating it — asking what the natural world could tell her about the nature of consciousness, the existence of God, and the meaning of death, as though nature were a witness she was cross-examining rather than a landscape she was admiring. As literary scholar Grace Mei-shu Chen has observed, Dickinson "did not remain a passive receiver or advocate of [transcendental] influences; rather, she balanced assimilation with interrogation, skepticism, disruption, and exploration" [Chen, "Coda: Natural Messages and Aesthetic Pleasure in Emily Dickinson's Nature Writing," Dickinson Electronic Archives](https://www.emilydickinson.org/emily-dickinson-lyrical-ecologies-forays-into-the-field/coda-natural-messages-and-aesthetic-pleasure-in-emily-dickinson-s-nature-writing).

Her poetry reminds us that the New England nature tradition was never merely pastoral. It was always shadowed by awareness — of time passing, of winter coming, of the small death that waits in every beautiful thing. She saw a train cut through Amherst in 1853, its jarring hiss a "strange stimulus for birdsong," and wrote: "A train went through a burial gate, / A bird broke forth and sang" [Dickinson Electronic Archives](https://birds.adagia.org/project/about-overview). The tension between industrial violence and natural persistence would only deepen in the century after her death.

## III. The Farmer Poet (1900–1915)

Robert Frost came of age in the shadow of these New England writers. Born in San Francisco in 1874, he moved east as a young man and spent eight and a half years as a farmer on a thirty-acre lot in Derry, New Hampshire, where he wrote the poems that would make him famous. The Derry years — roughly 1900 to 1915 — were formative: Frost was broke, his farm was rocky and unproductive, and he was writing about the everyday labor of living on a piece of New England land that had already been farmed out and abandoned once [Harvard Thesis, Frost's Ecopoesis](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037d-ca0c-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content). It is the perfect biography for a poet whose subject would be the steady pressure of the real on the ideal.

Frost's New England was not Emerson's. It was not a temple of spirit. It was a place where people made choices — "Two roads diverged in a yellow wood" — and had to live with them. His woods were lovely, dark and deep, but they were also a practical problem: "Whose woods these are I think I know. / His house is in the village though." The nature in Frost's poetry is active, not passive; it pushes back against human intention. A wall falls down. Birches bend under ice. Snow fills a pasture. A mountain dominates the horizon, indifferent to the people who live at its foot. Nature, for Frost, was not a backdrop but an adversary — the sort you respect precisely because you cannot defeat it.

Critics have sometimes treated Frost as a minor poet of regional charm. The assessment has aged poorly — as most condescensions do, once they have had time to ripen into embarrassment. As scholar Priscilla Paton has noted, Frost's best poems refuse the pastoral tradition's easy idealization: "Frost rarely extols the breath-taking vista. The poem refigures the unprofitable New England landscape as a moralized scene of limits" [Paton, "Robert Frost and the American Landscape"](https://files01.core.ac.uk/download/pdf/61163255.pdf). Frost himself described his method in a letter to Walter Prichard Eaton in 1915, the year his breakout collection *North of Boston* appeared: he wanted a poem that "ends in a momentary stay against confusion" [Frost, Letters](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037d-ca0c-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content).

What Frost was describing was a new relationship between observer and observed. If Emerson had dissolved the self into nature, and Dickinson had watched nature like an experimental subject, Frost was the first New England poet to stand *beside* nature — to treat it as a neighbor, difficult and worth knowing. The literary scholar Astrid Galbraith has called this "the New England poetic landscape" — a place where the land itself becomes a conversation partner [Galbraith, *New England as Poetic Landscape* (Peter Lang, 2010)](https://www.ijfmr.com/papers/2023/5/7876.pdf).

## IV. The Daily Walk (1964–2019)

Mary Oliver moved to Provincetown, Massachusetts, in 1964 and lived there for fifty years, at the very tip of Cape Cod, where the land frays into shifting dunes and the Atlantic meets the bay in a collision of currents and light. She walked every morning, usually before dawn, carrying a hand-bound notebook. Over the course of her life, she filled more than 130 of these notebooks — small, unlined books that she kept during her daily rambles, recording what she saw: the arrival of migratory birds, the condition of the tide, the behavior of a single grasshopper or bear or whale [Library of Congress, Mary Oliver Papers](https://hdl.loc.gov/loc.mss/eadmss.ms024014.3). She understood what most writers discover only too late — that the body must be in motion before the mind will consent to do its work.

The Library of Congress, which holds her papers, describes these notebooks as the wellspring of her published work: "over 130 small, bound notebooks, some handbound, Oliver kept during her daily walks on Cape Cod." They contain her birding sight lists, her observations of the natural world, and "various trial lines and general musings on the natural and spiritual world." She was, in a very literal sense, a poet who composed by walking — a practice that connected her directly to Thoreau's sauntering, Emerson's wandering, and the long tradition of New England poets who understood that the body must be in motion before the mind can settle into attention.

Oliver's poetry was widely loved — she became the best-selling poet in the United States, a fact that bemused her and continues to puzzle critics who prefer difficulty to accessibility. In a review for the Poetry Foundation, one critic described her work as "easy to digest, smooth and slightly sweet like pap" — a dismissal that misses the radical discipline underneath the ease [Poetry Foundation, Mary Oliver and the Nature-esque](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/69656/mary-oliver-and-the-nature-esque). Oliver was not writing comfort poetry. She was writing a sustained argument that attention is the highest form of devotion. "I could not be a poet without the natural world," she wrote. "Someone else could. But not me. For me the door to the woods is the door to the temple" [Cape Cod Times, Oliver Obituary](https://www.capecodtimes.com/story/news/2019/01/18/person-in-community-people/6252584007/).

In a 2016 essay for the Boston Globe, Oliver looked back at the Provincetown she had known since the 1960s — a fishing town that had been transformed by tourism, overfishing, and climate change. "The great rafts of fish began to diminish," she wrote. "The satisfaction of a day's work also began to vanish." The essay concluded with a quiet benediction: "I don't know if I am heading toward heaven or that other, dark place, but I know I have already lived in heaven for fifty years. Thank you, Provincetown" [Boston Globe, "In 'Provincetown,' poet Mary Oliver recalls quiet fishing town"](https://www.bostonglobe.com/lifestyle/2016/10/20/provincetown-poet-mary-oliver-recalls-quiet-fishing-town/AsfB3MJmRbCA7Vf0G9VziN/story.html). It is hard to imagine a more graceful exit from a tradition that had, by then, become indistinguishable from a life.

## V. The Ecomyths (2020–Present)

The poets now writing in New England are working in the wake of a difficult knowledge. They inherit Emerson's demand for "an original relation to the universe," Thoreau's practice of close observation, Dickinson's interrogative stance, Frost's farmer's realism, and Oliver's daily devotion — but they also inherit a landscape that is visibly changing in their own lifetimes. They are the first generation of New England nature poets for whom the nature in question has begun to send its regrets.

Megan Grumbling, who lives in Portland, Maine, published *Persephone in the Late Anthropocene* in 2020, a book that reframes the ancient myth of the goddess of spring for an era when spring no longer arrives on schedule. Persephone, in Grumbling's telling, "now comes and goes erratically, drinks too much, and takes a human lover in our warming, unraveling world" [Acre Books, Persephone in the Late Anthropocene](https://acre-books.com/titles/persephone-in-the-late-anthropocene/). The book interweaves "extreme weather, industrialized plenty, and ecological collapse" with the voices of its mythological figures. Grumbling won the Robert Frost Foundation Award for her earlier work, and teaches at Southern Maine Community College — a living connection between the tradition and its transformation.

Elizabeth Bradfield, a naturalist-poet on Cape Cod who lives in the same town where Mary Oliver walked for decades, has spent the last several years compiling a "feel guide" for the Gulf of Maine bioregion — a book that blends poetry, art, and natural history for the region from Cape Cod to Nova Scotia. The Gulf of Maine is one of the fastest-warming bodies of water on the planet, and Bradfield's project is an attempt to register what is disappearing. "Anyone who looks out from the inscape of the self and asks how they are connected to the world — air, trees, water, concrete, beetles, stars, seasons — in a full way is writing ecopoetry," she said in a 2025 interview [Terrain.org, Elizabeth Bradfield Interview](https://www.terrain.org/2025/interviews/inscape-elizabeth-bradfield/).

David Crews's *Mônadenok* project — the 408-line poem, the Abenaki names, the Emerson echo — is perhaps the most explicit act of literary archaeology in this new generation. Crews describes his method as a "revision of one Ralph Waldo Emerson published about the mountain in 1847," and the poem's sources include Thoreau's bird lists, the botanical surveys of Mount Monadnock, and the Pennacook and Cowasuck histories of the region. He is, in a sense, writing the same poem Emerson wrote, but with the additional knowledge of what arrived in the intervening centuries — Indian removal, industrialization, climate change, extinction. The poem occupies the same form. The world it describes does not [David Crews, Mônadenok](https://www.davidcrewspoetry.com/monadenok).

There is, too, the national movement of Poets for Science — born in 2017 when Jane Hirshfield wrote "On the Fifth Day" after climate information was removed from the White House website. The movement has since produced the *Dear Human at the Edge of Time* anthology (2023), which accompanies the U.S. Fifth National Climate Assessment, and the *Nature of Our Times* anthology (2025), published alongside the U.S. National Assessment of lands, waters, and wildlife. In November 2026, the Wick Poetry Center will convene a three-day Poets for Science Gathering, bringing together scientists and writers to ask what the humanities can contribute to the scientific understanding of ecological crisis [USA Today, "Finding hope in troubling times with poetry and science"](https://www.usatoday.com/story/lifestyle/columns/2026/05/24/merging-science-humanities-christensen/90173749007/).

## VI. The Forest That Taught Them (2100)

The ecological data from New England's forests is not ambiguous. The USDA Forest Service's *New England Forest Ecosystem Vulnerability Assessment* — a collaboration of more than thirty scientists and resource managers — projects that by the end of this century, the mean annual temperature across the region will rise by 3 to 8 degrees Fahrenheit. Boreal and northern species such as balsam fir, red spruce, and black spruce will lose suitable habitat and biomass. Southern species, including red maple, northern red oak, and black cherry, may expand their ranges northward [USDA Forest Service, NRS-173](https://www.fs.usda.gov/nrs/pubs/gtr/gtr_nrs173.pdf).

A separate assessment by Mass Audubon of forest carbon offset projects in central and western Massachusetts found that by 2050, half the trees in the study area will be vulnerable to dying from climate change, pests, or disease. The health of the surviving forest will depend largely on just two species — northern red oak and red maple — neither of which is reproducing adequately under current deer-browsing pressure [Mass Audubon, Assessment of Forest Vulnerabilities](https://massland.org/sites/default/files/resources/forest_vulnerability_assessment_mass_aud.pdf).

A 2024 study in *Frontiers in Forests and Global Change* modeled the distance tree species would need to migrate to find compatible climates: an average of 222 kilometers by the 2030s, 358 kilometers by the 2050s, and 662 kilometers by the 2090s — roughly three seed zones north for every species, every century [Frontiers, "Identifying climatically-compatible seedlots" (2024)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/forests-and-global-change/articles/10.3389/ffgc.2024.1449340/full).

This is not merely an ecological story. It is a literary one. The sugar maple that turns the hills of Vermont red in October — the tree that Frost saw outside his window in Derry, that Dickinson watched from her window in Amherst, that Thoreau counted in the Concord woods, that Emerson used as a figure for the soul's relation to God — is projected to lose suitable habitat across much of its southern New England range. The red spruce that grows on the upper slopes of Mount Monadnock — the tree Emerson addressed in his poem, the tree Crews measures his lines against — is one of the species most vulnerable to warming temperatures.

When Crews stands on Monadnock and tries to see what Emerson saw, he is also trying to see what Emerson could not have seen: the end of the conditions that made his seeing possible. The "ecomyth," as the University of Maine honors thesis on Frost's poetry defines it, is "typically (1) oral and/or fluid in its telling, (2) saying something unknowable in terms of something knowable, (3) creating a momentary stay against confusion to therefore help us better understand the world around us, and (4) (attempting to) identify or represent a vital force as found in nature" [University of Maine, Frost's Ecomyth](https://digitalcommons.library.umaine.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1934&context=honors).

The tradition from Emerson to Crews is a tradition of creating such stays. And the question now is whether the staying will hold.

## VII. The Stubborn Song

The Dickinsonian scholar Martha Nell Smith once wrote that in Dickinson's poetry, "birdsong is arguably the most constant evanescent natural sound she recorded through writing in an age before the technologies of recording had been invented." The birds of the Connecticut River Valley, whose arrivals and departures Dickinson tracked for forty years, were "a waymark in an evanescent geography" [Dickinson's Birds, Unsilent Springs](https://manifold.umn.edu/read/a66e67c0-b299-47a5-9505-1ffe7f0ee1fe/section/a134e17a-e62b-4493-9899-91f0503a3a65).

Every poet in this tradition has been a recorder of evanescent geographies. Emerson recorded the feeling of spirit dissolving the boundaries of the body. Thoreau recorded the precise depth and temperature of Walden Pond. Dickinson recorded the date of the robin's return. Frost recorded the sound of a mowing scythe in a New Hampshire field. Oliver recorded the sight of a bear in the Truro woods. And now Grumbling records the erratic returns of spring, Bradfield records the warming water of the Gulf of Maine, and Crews records the mountain that he can see, as Emerson could, but that he might outlive in a form he would recognize.

Tradition is not inheritance. Tradition is something you choose, and then re-choose, every generation. The New England poets chose, again and again, to pay attention to the actual physical world in front of them — not the world of ideas, not the world of symbols, but the particular landscape of this specific place, with its specific mix of trees and birds and weather. That choice is now being re-made by poets who must attend to a world that is simultaneously the one they love and the one they are losing.

"We may trust a good deal more than we do," Thoreau wrote in *Walden*. "Nature is as well adapted to our weakness as to our strength."

We may need to trust it more now than ever. And New England's poets — the ones who came before, the ones writing now, the ones who will come after — will be the ones showing us how.
