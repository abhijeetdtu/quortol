# The Catskills' Next Act: $2.6 Billion, 300 Miles of Trail, and a Region Reimagining Itself

## 1. The View from the Ledge

The trail to the summit of Slide Mountain begins in silence — a long, steady climb through hemlock and yellow birch, past stone walls that mark the boundary of farms that have not existed for a century, through a forest that has been growing back ever since. At 4,180 feet, the highest point in the Catskills, the trees thin out and the world opens. To the east, the Hudson Valley spreads like a painted scroll. To the west, the headwaters of the Neversink River glint through the gaps between peaks. On a clear October afternoon, when the maples are on fire and the air carries the first edge of winter, the view extends into three states.

John Burroughs, the naturalist and writer who made this mountain famous, stood somewhere near this spot in the 1880s and wrote: "Here the works of man dwindle." [NYSDEC — Slide Mountain Wilderness](https://dec.ny.gov/places/slide-mountain-wilderness)

He was not wrong about the dwindling. But he could not have imagined what would come next. The Catskills — six thousand square miles of mountains, forests, rivers, and valleys stretching across four counties less than a hundred miles from Times Square — have spent the last forty years dying, reviving, and transforming into something Burroughs would not recognize.

In 2024, visitors to the Catskills spent $2.6 billion. That is more than visitors spent in the Adirondacks, more than the Finger Lakes, more than the Capital-Saratoga region. It is a number that seemed impossible in the 1990s, when the great Borscht Belt hotels stood abandoned, when boarded-up storefronts lined Main Streets, when the region appeared to have been left behind by the American century — which, in truth, had never much noticed it in the first place. [Empire State Development — 2024 Tourism Economics, Catskills regional report](https://esd.ny.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/Catskills-2024-EconomicImpactofTourisminNewYork.pdf)

## 2. What the Mountains Remember

To understand what the Catskills are becoming, you have to understand what they were — and what they have been, for much longer than most people realize.

The Catskill Forest Preserve was created on May 15, 1885, when Governor David B. Hill signed a law requiring that state lands in the Catskills and Adirondacks "be forever kept as wild forest lands." Nine years later, New York's Constitution was amended to include the "forever wild" clause — Article XIV, Section 1 — which remains one of the strongest legal protections for wild land anywhere in the world. The trees on these 288,000 acres cannot be sold, cut, or removed. They stand under the protection of the state constitution, which gives them a degree of legal security that most citizens could only envy. [NYSDEC — Birth of the Blue Line](https://dec.ny.gov/nature/forests-trees/forest-preserve/birth-of-blue-line-in-adirondack-catskill-parks)

The Catskill Park itself — the area within the "blue line," encompassing both public and private land — was established in 1904. Today, it contains roughly 700,000 acres, of which about 60 percent is privately owned. The remaining 288,000 acres of Forest Preserve are crossed by over 300 miles of trails, dotted with 700 individual campsites, and crowned by 98 peaks over 3,000 feet. [NYSDEC — Welcome to the Catskills](https://dec.ny.gov/nature/forests-trees/forest-preserve/welcome-to-the-catskills); [NYSDEC — Catskill Forest Preserve](https://dec.ny.gov/nature/forests-trees/forest-preserve)

For more than a century, the "forever wild" lands provided a quiet, largely unnoticed public good. The people who used them were mostly locals — hunters, fishermen, hikers who knew the unmarked trails. Then, in the 1920s, something else began to grow alongside the second-growth forest.

The Borscht Belt — the Jewish resort era that turned Sullivan and Ulster Counties into a summer playground for millions — was not born of tourism boards or economic development plans. It was born of exclusion. When Jews were barred from hotels and beaches elsewhere, Jewish entrepreneurs built their own. It is an old story: forbid a people from entering your garden, and they will grow a more beautiful one of their own. At its peak in the 1950s and 1960s, more than 500 hotels and 50,000 bungalow colonies dotted the Catskill landscape. Grossinger's had its own airstrip and post office. The Concord Resort Hotel, with 1,500 rooms, was the largest hotel in the eastern United States. Entertainers who began in the Catskills — Woody Allen, Joan Rivers, Jerry Seinfeld, Sid Caesar — reshaped American comedy. The Borscht Belt was not merely a vacation destination; it was a self-contained world, a Jewish Eden in the mountains. [Borscht Belt Historical Marker Project](https://borschtbelthistoricalmarkerproject.org/the-borscht-belt); [Catskills Borscht Belt Museum](https://www.borschtbeltmuseum.org/history)

Then it ended. Cheap airfare, air conditioning, the decline of antisemitism, and the assimilation of younger generations conspired against the old resorts. By the 1980s, most of the grand hotels stood abandoned. Grossinger's filed for bankruptcy in 1990. The Nevele, which outlasted most of its competitors, finally closed in 2009. The bungalow colonies were boarded up or bulldozed. The economy that had sustained the region for half a century collapsed. [Tablet Magazine — Back to the Borscht Belt](https://www.tabletmag.com/sections/community/articles/back-to-borscht-belt-jewish-catskills-revival)

For a time, the Catskills seemed destined to become what post-industrial regions across America became: a collection of dying towns, aging populations, and crumbling infrastructure, remembered mostly for what they used to be — which, in the manner of all elegies, was more impressive in retrospect than it had ever seemed at the time.

But mountains do not stop being beautiful because the hotels close. And the "forever wild" lands were still there, waiting. They had been waiting for quite a while.

## 3. What the Region Offers

The Catskills' revival did not begin with a plan. It began with a trail.

In 1923, a group of hikers and conservationists created the New York-New Jersey Trail Conference and began building the Long Path, a 358-mile footpath that runs from New York City to the Catskill Escarpment. Other trails followed: the 23-mile Devil's Path, described by guides as "extremely strenuous" and beloved by people who consider stairmasters insufficiently punishing; the Burroughs Range Trail, which traverses three of the Catskills' highest peaks; the Escarpment Trail, which follows the eastern edge of the mountains and offers views that stretch to the Berkshires on clear days. [NYSDEC — Kaaterskill Wild Forest](https://dec.ny.gov/places/kaaterskill-wild-forest); [Catskill Mountain Club](https://www.catskillmountainclub.org/news/more-hike-suggestions)

The waters beneath the trails are just as storied. The Catskills are the birthplace of American fly fishing. The Beaverkill, the Willowemoc, the Esopus, the Neversink — these are names that carry the same weight among anglers that Fenway Park carries among baseball fans. The Beaverkill is the preeminent American trout stream. The Willowemoc is where the art of casting a fly over wild brown trout was refined into something close to a religion — which, like most religions, involves a great deal of standing in cold water and waiting. [NYSDEC — Balsam Lake Mountain Wild Forest](https://dec.ny.gov/places/balsam-lake-mountain-wild-forest); [NYSDEC — Willowemoc Wild Forest](https://dec.ny.gov/places/willowemoc-wild-forest)

In winter, the snow arrives. Hunter Mountain and Windham Mountain draw skiers from the city. Belleayre, owned and operated by the state, crosses the summit into Ulster County. Plattekill, smaller and fiercely independent, is the kind of mountain where locals still outnumber tourists on a good powder day — a distinction that matters more to the locals than to the tourists, who have not yet discovered that they are outnumbered. Cross-country skiers and snowshoers have their pick of hundreds of miles of ungroomed trails through frozen forest. [I Love NY — Catskills Attractions](https://www.iloveny.com/places-to-go/catskills/attractions/)

And then there is the culture. Woodstock has been an artists' colony since 1902, and it still is — though the artists now share the town with visitors who come for the name and stay for the galleries, the restaurants, the weekend farmers' market, the sense of having arrived somewhere that has not been entirely sanitized. The Borscht Belt Museum opened permanently in Ellenville in 2025, housing artifacts from the half-century when the Catskills were the summer capital of American Jewish life. The Catskills Visitor Center, opened in 2015 in Mount Tremper, serves as the gateway to the park and the region. [Catskills Visitor Center](https://catskillsvisitorcenter.org/outdoor-recreation/); [Borscht Belt Museum](https://www.borschtbeltmuseum.org)

The towns have rediscovered themselves, too. In Hudson (technically the Hudson Valley, but close enough that the distinction matters mostly to locals), antique shops and farm-to-table restaurants line Warren Street. In Phoenicia, the diner still serves pancakes the size of dinner plates. In Livingston Manor, the Catskill Fly Fishing Center and Museum keeps the angling tradition alive. In Andes and Roxbury and Margaretville and Callicoon, the old storefronts are filling up again — with coffee roasters, bookshops, galleries, distilleries, cider houses, and the kind of small-batch everything that marks a region in the middle of a cultural renaissance, or at least a very well-curated one. [Tablet Magazine](https://www.tabletmag.com/sections/community/articles/back-to-borscht-belt-jewish-catskills-revival)

## 4. The Revival, in Numbers

What happened next was not gradual.

In 2024, the Catskills region drew $2.6 billion in visitor spending — a 7.1 percent increase over 2023. Ulster County led the region with $1.1 billion, followed by Sullivan County at 39 percent of the total. Tourists spent $520 million on seasonal second homes alone — one-fifth of all tourism spending, which is to say that a considerable portion of the region's visitors would prefer to own the place rather than merely rent it. [Empire State Development — 2024 Catskills report](https://esd.ny.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/Catskills-2024-EconomicImpactofTourisminNewYork.pdf)

![Catskills region visitor spending by county, 2020–2024, showing growth from $1.18 billion in pandemic-era 2020 to $2.6 billion in 2024.](/api/blog/images/catskills-next-act_visitor_spending.png)

The tourism industry now supports 18,552 jobs — 15.8 percent of all employment in the four-county Catskills region. In Sullivan County, tourism wages account for 17.2 percent of all personal income. In Greene County, the figure is 13.2 percent. The industry generated $283 million in state and local taxes. [Empire State Development — 2024 Catskills report](https://esd.ny.gov/sites/default/files/media/document/Catskills-2024-EconomicImpactofTourisminNewYork.pdf)

Statewide, New York State Parks recorded 88.4 million visits in 2024 — a record, and the 12th consecutive annual increase. Attendance has climbed 66 percent over the past two decades. [Office of the New York State Comptroller — State Parks a Growing Attraction](https://www.osc.ny.gov/reports/state-parks-growing-attraction); [Governor Hochul — 2024 record attendance announcement](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-announces-record-attendance-state-parks)

Within the Catskill Park itself, the numbers are even more striking. At Kaaterskill Falls, the most visited single destination in the park, the Catskill Center's stewards counted 52,405 visitors between May and October 2024 — a 25 percent increase over the previous year. Fire tower visitation more than doubled. Across all monitored sites, the center greeted over 90,000 visitors during the season, an increase of more than 24,000 from 2023. [Catskill Center — 2024 Stewards End-of-Season Report](https://catskillcenter.squarespace.com/s/20250325-Catskill-Park-Advisory-Committee-Agenda.pdf)

Since 2012, annual visits to the Catskill Park have more than doubled. In 2021, the park welcomed more visitors than the Grand Canyon, Yosemite, and Yellowstone combined — which is either a triumph of regional tourism or a warning that wilderness cannot be loved to death without consequences, and possibly both. [Catskill Mountainkeeper — 2023 Budget Testimony](https://nyassembly.gov/write/upload/publichearing/001345/004415.pdf)

The visitors have brought real estate buyers with them. In Sullivan County, median home prices rose from $144,900 in 2019 to $350,000 in 2025 — a 141.5 percent increase. Greene County homes went from $196,000 to $379,000 over the same period, a 93.4 percent jump. Ulster County rose from $248,000 to $450,000. [Hudson Valley Pattern for Progress — 2025 Q4 & Annual Housing Report](https://www.pattern-for-progress.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2025-Q4-Annual-Housing-Report-.pdf)

The FHFA's All-Transactions House Price Index for Sullivan County rose from 211.83 in 2021 to 317.80 in 2025 — a 50 percent increase in the index value in just four years. [FRED — All-Transactions HPI for Sullivan County](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/ATNHPIUS36105A)

![Median home prices for Sullivan, Greene, and Ulster Counties, 2019–2025, showing the pandemic-era surge. Sullivan County prices more than doubled.](/api/blog/images/catskills-next-act_home_prices.png)

The short-term rental market exploded alongside the real estate market. In Greene County, Airbnb and VRBO bookings totaled $67 million in 2021, an 80 percent increase from 2019, with average nightly rates rising from $403 to $549. In the Town of Catskill, active Airbnb listings grew 46.9 percent in a single year through early 2026, while revenue continued to climb. [The Real Deal — Hudson Valley rental markets](https://therealdeal.com/new-york/tristate/2022/02/07/these-hudson-valley-rental-markets-surged-most-during-pandemic/); [AirROI — Town of Catskill Airbnb Data 2026](https://www.airroi.com/airbnb-data/united-states/new-york/town-of-catskill)

The migration was not just vacationers. Between 2019 and 2021, an estimated 80,000 people moved from New York City to the Hudson Valley, including the Catskills region. Greene County led all of New York State with 18 net in-migrants per 1,000 residents in 2020-2021 — a rate that placed it in the top decile of all U.S. counties. USPS data showed a 204 percent increase in New York City address changes to Columbia County; Ulster County saw a 180 percent increase. [Hudson Valley Pattern for Progress — Migration Report 2023](https://pattern-for-progress.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/PfP-Migration-Report-2023.pdf); [Empire Center — NYC out-migration](https://www.empirecenter.org/publications/nycs-out-migration-fueled-ny-states-record-population-drop/)

## 5. The Demand for Paradise

The sudden popularity of the Catskills has created something the region has never had to manage before: demand. It is a peculiar problem — to be loved so well that one can no longer breathe.

The U.S. Forest Service, in partnership with the NYS DEC, conducted a social assessment of the Catskill Forest Preserve in 2023 and 2024, interviewing visitors at high-use areas to understand who was coming, why, and what they expected. The study was prompted by a simple observation: the number of people recreating in the preserve had grown far faster than the infrastructure designed to support them. [USDA Forest Service — Responsible Recreation in the Catskill Forest Preserve](https://research.fs.usda.gov/nrs/projects/catskill-recreation)

At Kaaterskill Falls, where visitation increased 25 percent in a single year, the pressure is visible: parking lots overflow onto narrow mountain roads, trails erode under the weight of foot traffic, and the DEC has spent years studying how to manage a waterfall that has become, essentially, a natural tourist attraction with the carrying capacity of a small national park — and the constitutional protections of a cathedral. A 2022 report from the Catskill Advisory Group stated plainly that the entire park needed "intensive management." [Times Union — Catskills hot spots more visitors 2024](https://www.timesunion.com/hudsonvalley/catskills/article/catskills-visitors-kaaterskill-falls-platte-clove-19997835.php)

The tension is not unique to the Catskills, but it is sharper here than in many places because the legal framework is so strict. Article XIV's "forever wild" clause means there are hard limits on what can be built, where, and how. You cannot pave a trail through a constitutionally protected wilderness. You cannot expand parking lots into old-growth forest. You cannot add restrooms and visitor centers without navigating a permitting process that was designed, intentionally, to make development difficult — which is, of course, precisely what made the place worth protecting in the first place. [NYSDEC — State Land Classifications](https://dec.ny.gov/nature/forests-trees/dec-land-stewardship/state-land-classifications)

The result is a paradox: a place that more people want to visit than the infrastructure can comfortably accommodate, protected by laws that make it difficult to build the infrastructure that would accommodate them. It is the sort of dilemma that would amuse the gods, if gods concerned themselves with parking lots.

## 6. The People Who Live Here

The Catskills' year-round residents have watched the revival with a complicated mixture of relief and unease — the relief of the patient who has finally recovered enough to worry about the cost of the medicine.

Relief, because the economic numbers are undeniable. In Sullivan County, where Resorts World Catskills has generated over $300 million in gaming tax payments since opening in 2018, room occupancy taxes rose 340 percent between 2017 and 2023. The casino supports an estimated 1,486 jobs and $287 million in annual economic output. [Sullivan County — Economic Impact of Downstate Casinos report](https://www.sullivanny.gov/sites/default/files/departments/BOL/The%20Impact%20of%20New%20Downstate%20Casinos%20on%20Sullivan%20County%20and%20RWC%204-21-25.pdf)

Unease, because prosperity has a price. In the village of Hunter, only 31 percent of housing units are occupied by year-round residents. The rest are second homes, short-term rentals, or seasonal properties — places that exist, for much of the year, as a kind of beautiful absence. In Shandaken, the figure is 55 percent. Across the six-town Overlook region in northern Ulster and southern Greene Counties, the median home value has risen to $351,767 — above the national median of $340,200 — while the poverty rate sits at 13.2 percent, slightly above the U.S. average. It is a peculiar kind of inequality: the locals cannot afford the towns the visitors have made valuable. [The Overlook — By the Numbers](https://theoverlooknews.com/by-the-numbers-a-data-driven-look-at-the-overlook-region/)

In the village of Fleischmanns, the population dropped from 351 in 2010 to 210 in 2020, before regaining about 20 permanent residents after the pandemic. In the town of Delaware in Sullivan County, nearly 500 residents have left since 2010, a 17 percent decrease. The vacant houses did not stay empty — many became second homes or short-term rentals. The people left, but the buildings stayed, and they are now occupied by people who do not need to be here year-round. [Times Union — Catskills villages face population decline](https://www.timesunion.com/projects/2025/shifting-new-york/catskills/)

The Catskills' four-county population (Delaware, Greene, Sullivan, Ulster) stands at roughly 352,000 — lower than it was in 2010. Projections from Cornell's Program on Applied Demographics suggest it could fall to 332,000 in the coming decades. [Cornell PAD — Demographic trends in the Catskills](https://pad.human.cornell.edu/presentations/slides/CERM22/CERM22.pdf)

But there are glimmers of a different future. The village of Franklin in Delaware County had 273 residents in 2024 — up from 258 in 2020. The increase is modest, but it represents something significant: people who choose to live in the Catskills not because they have to, but because the internet allows them to keep their city jobs while living in a mountain village. "The pandemic opened the door to virtual work," Franklin's town supervisor told the Times Union. "Remote work has allowed people to live in a rural community without worrying about a commute or sacrificing their jobs." [Times Union — Catskills villages](https://www.timesunion.com/projects/2025/shifting-new-york/catskills/)

The IRS migration data confirms the trend: households moving into the Hudson Valley are bringing higher incomes than those moving out. Remote work has enabled a segment of the workforce to keep metro-area salaries while enjoying upstate housing costs — creating a new class of residents who are neither tourists nor traditional locals, but something in between: people who have discovered that the paradise they once visited can now be lived in, at least on weekdays. [Hudson Valley Pattern for Progress — Migration Report](https://pattern-for-progress.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/PfP-Migration-Report-2023.pdf)

## 7. The Future, Under the Blue Line

The challenge ahead is one of balance — and the Catskills have been here before. That is the advantage of having lived through several deaths: one learns how to survive a revival.

The "forever wild" clause was written in response to a deforestation crisis that left the Catskills' hillsides denuded and its rivers choked with silt. The Borscht Belt rose from the ashes of an agricultural economy that could not sustain the families who worked the thin mountain soil. The current revival emerged from the collapse of the resort era and the long, slow decline that followed.

Each time, the region reinvented itself by rediscovering what it already had — which is the Catskills' special talent, and perhaps its curse. The place is always being found again by people who think they are the first to see it.

In 2024, the state committed to expanding the Catskill Park's trail network, improving access at Kaaterskill Falls, and funding additional stewardship positions. The Catskill Center's steward program, which placed trained staff at high-use areas to educate visitors and manage impacts, proved so successful that the DEC has considered expanding it to other regions. The Forest Service's social assessment will inform management decisions for years to come. [USDA Forest Service — Catskill Recreation](https://research.fs.usda.gov/nrs/projects/catskill-recreation); [Catskill Center Stewards Report](https://catskillcenter.squarespace.com/s/20250325-Catskill-Park-Advisory-Committee-Agenda.pdf)

The future of Resorts World Catskills is uncertain — downstate casino expansion threatens its customer base, and a single new casino in the New York City area could reduce the resort's gross gaming revenue by 26 to 76 percent, according to a county-commissioned analysis. [Sullivan County casino impact report](https://www.sullivanny.gov/sites/default/files/departments/BOL/The%20Impact%20of%20New%20Downstate%20Casinos%20on%20Sullivan%20County%20and%20RWC%204-21-25.pdf)

But the Catskills have survived the loss of larger industries. What they have that cannot be taken away is the land itself — the 288,000 constitutionally protected acres, the 300 miles of trails, the 98 peaks, the trout streams that have been flowing since long before anyone thought to give them names. A casino may falter. A hotel may close. But a mountain is a more durable investment.

## 8. The Sound of Running Water

In the Catskills, the water is always moving. It comes off the peaks in a thousand rivulets, gathers into streams, becomes creeks, becomes rivers that will, eventually, reach the Atlantic. The Esopus Creek runs cold and clear through the valley below Slide Mountain. The Beaverkill flows past the ruins of the old hotels, past the fly fishermen standing thigh-deep in the current, past the towns that are learning to live with a second life.

On a summer evening, standing on the bank of the Willowemoc — the river that made the Catskills famous among people who care about trout — you can hear the water moving over the stones. It is the same sound John Burroughs heard. The same sound the Borscht Belt visitors heard, when they came up from the city to escape the heat. The same sound the new arrivals hear now, as they sit on their cabin porches, laptops open, working from paradise — which, if one is honest about it, has always required a certain amount of maintenance.

The trees are older than any of them. The constitution says they will remain. And the water, as ever, has the last word.
