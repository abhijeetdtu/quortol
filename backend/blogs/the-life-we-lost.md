# The Life We Lost: A History of the Human Day

*On the savannahs of northern Tanzania, near the shores of Lake Eyasi, the Hadza people still begin their days as humans have for most of our species' existence. They wake before dawn, listen to the birds, and decide where the honey might be flowing or the tubers growing. By early afternoon, the day's essential work is done. What remains is conversation, storytelling, rest, and the slow repair of social bonds around a fire. It is a rhythm our species evolved to keep for roughly 200,000 years. In the last two centuries, and especially the last two decades, we have shattered it almost entirely.*

---

**I. The Original Plenty**

We like to imagine the prehistoric past as a brutish slog — a brief, nasty struggle for calories interrupted by sleep. This is a flattering myth, and we cling to it. The anthropological evidence gathered over the last half-century suggests something rather different: ancient humans worked fewer hours than most people alive today, slept about the same amount, and spent vastly more of their lives doing nothing in particular with people they knew intimately.

Richard Borshay Lee's landmark 1960s fieldwork among the !Kung San of the Kalahari Desert was the first to quantify this. He found that !Kung women, who supplied 60 to 80 percent of the diet by weight, worked roughly two or three days per week gathering plant foods. Men hunted — conscientious but not particularly successful, in Lee's dry assessment — and contributed perhaps fifteen to twenty hours per week. Overall, an individual !Kung spent "a third of his time living only with close relatives, a third visiting other camps, and a third entertaining visitors from other camps." [Lee, *The !Kung San: Men, Women, and Work in a Foraging Society*](https://archive.org/details/kungsanmenwomenw0000leer) | [Lee, "Scarce Resources"](https://people.morrisville.edu/~reymers/readings/ANTH101/Scarce_Resources-Lee.pdf)

More recent and more precise data comes from the Hadza of Tanzania, studied intensively by Thomas Kraft, Herman Pontzer, and their colleagues. Using scan-sampling and portable respirometry, they calculated that Hadza men spend an average of 4.7 hours per day on subsistence; women, 5.1 hours. The cross-cultural average across fourteen hunter-gatherer societies is 4.5 hours for men and 4.3 for women. [Kraft et al., *Science* 374 (2021)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.abf0130)

Sleep? About 6.25 hours per night, with a nap on roughly 54 percent of days averaging 47.5 minutes. [Yetish et al., *Current Biology* (2015)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982215011574) | [Sampaio et al., *Am. J. Human Biology* (2019)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ajpa.23160)

The !Kung and Hadza are not perfect mirrors of Stone Age life — they are contemporary peoples living in marginal environments with their own histories — but they are the best living models we have of the subsistence pattern that characterized nearly all of human existence. What they suggest is that the human day for most of evolutionary history involved roughly five hours of food-getting, seven hours of sleep, and twelve hours of something we no longer have a good word for: unstructured time shared with people you know intimately.

This was the time-budget our brains, our endocrine systems, and our social instincts were shaped to inhabit. Then we started changing it.

**II. The Seasonal Rhythm**

The adoption of agriculture, beginning roughly 12,000 years ago, did not immediately increase working hours. According to Kraft and colleagues' cross-cultural comparison, horticulturalists spend an average of 4.1 hours per day on subsistence — actually *less* than hunter-gatherers at 4.5 hours, though the difference is not statistically significant. What farming changed was the *structure* of time: a feast-or-famine calendar of intense planting and harvest punctuated by months of relative idleness.

The pre-industrial English peasant inhabited a world that was, by modern standards, almost impossibly leisured — at certain times of year. The working day in 1750s London, as reconstructed by economic historian Hans-Joachim Voth from over 2,000 witness depositions in the Old Bailey court records, began around 6:50 AM and ended at 6:50 PM, with meal breaks yielding roughly 11 hours of actual labor. But the *year* was a different matter. Voth estimated that Londoners in the 1750s worked 208 days per year — because Sundays, Mondays, and 53 holy days were generally taken off. [Voth, "Time and Work in Eighteenth-Century London," *Journal of Economic History* 58:1 (1998)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-economic-history/article/time-and-work-in-eighteenthcentury-london/710009A979234187056C8C188A952ECC) | [Voth, working paper](https://www.crei.cat/wp-content/uploads/users/working-papers/voth_timeandwork.pdf)

In medieval England, the calendar was even kinder. The Open Access Companion to the Canterbury Tales catalogs the holiday schedule: Christmas to Epiphany, Shrovetide, Easter week, Hocktide, May Day, Whitsun, Corpus Christi, Midsummer, Lammas, All Saints, local patron saints' days — "all told, there were three or four dozen work-free holidays a year in addition to Sundays." [Open Access Companion to the Canterbury Tales](https://opencanterburytales.dsl.lsu.edu/refeverydaylife/) A ploughman's team, according to the 13th-century agricultural writer Walter of Henley, stopped work at three in the afternoon. [Walter of Henley, via Prothero, *The Pioneer and Progress of English Farming*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/16594/16594-8.txt)

But this was also a world of sharp seasonality. In Denmark, eighteenth-century rural workers under full employment worked 5.6 days per week but, because winter employment was scarce, the average working year was only 184 days. The summer working day stretched 12 to 14 hours; winter offered little more than daylight hours — about seven. [Claridge et al., "Days Worked in Eighteenth Century Denmark"](https://www.sdu.dk/-/media/files/om_sdu/institutter/ivoe/disc_papers/disc_2019/dpbe10_2019.pdf)

The pre-industrial day was not a golden age. It was a world of early death, hard physical labor, and vulnerability to crop failure. But it *was* a world rich in shared time — evenings by the fire, feast days, alehouses, church-going, and the slow rhythms of communal work. The English word "leisure" derives from the Latin *licere*, meaning "to be permitted." What the pre-industrial peasant had was not so much leisure as permission, in abundance, to simply stop.

**III. The Great Inversion**

The Industrial Revolution did something unprecedented: it made humans work more than ever before.

Between 1750 and 1800, annual working hours in London increased by 20 to 27 percent — a transformation as large as the *decline* in working hours between 1870 and 1938, but compressed into half the time. Using Old Bailey witness data for both periods, Voth estimated annual labor input rose from roughly 2,576 hours per year in 1760 to 3,328 hours in 1800, and stayed at around 3,356 hours in 1830. [Voth, "The Longest Years: New Estimates of Labor Input in England, 1760–1830," *Journal of Economic History*](https://www.crei.cat/wp-content/uploads/users/working-papers/voth_longestyears.pdf)

The working day itself did not lengthen — it remained around 11 hours. What changed was the *number* of working days. Monday ceased to be a day of rest. The 53 holy days of the mid-18th century were systematically eliminated. By 1800, Londoners worked 306 days per year, not 208. The Calvinist Reformation had already suppressed Denmark's 50 religious holidays to 16 by 1539; by 1770, only seven remained. [Voth, "Time and Work"](https://www.crei.cat/wp-content/uploads/users/working-papers/voth_timeandwork.pdf) | [Claridge et al.](https://www.sdu.dk/-/media/files/om_sdu/institutter/ivoe/disc_papers/disc_2019/dpbe10_2019.pdf)

The human cost is documented in the photographs of Lewis Hine, who for the National Child Labor Committee documented working children across America in the early 1900s. A 13-year-old "doffer" in a Weldon, North Carolina cotton mill — a boy who had worked since age 9 — told Hine: "I got my hand caught in the cogs of the spinning machine, and lost part of my finger. It stopped the machine and I tell you it hurt. It pains me a lot now. Don't you think they orter pay me wages while I'm out with this bad hand?" [Library of Congress, Lewis Hine Collection](https://www.loc.gov/item/2018677780/)

The factory imposed machine-time on bodies that had evolved for task-time. E. P. Thompson, in his magisterial essay "Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism," described the transformation: the irregular, self-paced rhythms of artisan and peasant gave way to the fixed schedule of the steam engine. The clock, not the task, became the measure of a day's worth. [E. P. Thompson, "Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism," *Past & Present* 38 (1967)]

This was the moment the human time-budget inverted: from a world in which work was what you did until you had enough, to a world in which work was what you did until the bell rang.

**IV. The Promise That Never Arrived**

In 1930, John Maynard Keynes predicted that by 2030, technological progress would have reduced the workweek to 15 hours. "For the first time since his creation," Keynes wrote, "man will be faced with his real, his permanent problem — how to use his freedom from pressing economic cares, how to occupy the leisure, which science and compound interest will have won for him, to live wisely and agreeably and well." [Keynes, "Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren" (1930)](https://www.econlib.org/library/Essays/keyes1.html)

The 20th century seemed to prove Keynes right — at first. Work hours fell dramatically from their Industrial Revolution peak. By 1950, the average American workweek had dropped below 40 hours. The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 enshrined the 40-hour week in law. Between 1870 and 1938, annual working hours in the US declined by roughly 40 percent. [Ramey & Francis, "A Century of Work and Leisure," *American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics* 1:2 (2009)]

But the leisure society never materialized. What happened instead was stranger and more insidious. The hours not spent in paid work were filled — not with wisdom, agreeableness, or community — but with new forms of labor (housework, childcare, commuting) and, eventually, with screens.

**V. The Digital Inversion**

The American Time Use Survey, conducted annually by the Bureau of Labor Statistics since 2003, offers the most granular portrait ever assembled of how a modern society spends its 24 hours. The 2024 data is representative of roughly 260 million Americans aged 15 and older. Here is the average day:

| Activity | Hours per day |
|----------|--------------|
| Sleeping | 9.04 |
| Personal care (other) | 0.76 |
| Eating and drinking | 1.24 |
| Work and work-related (all persons) | 3.43 |
| Household activities | 2.01 |
| Caring for others | 0.62 |
| Leisure and sports | 5.07 |
| Other (education, civic, etc.) | 1.83 |

Source: [BLS American Time Use Survey, 2024](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.t01.htm)

Five hours of leisure per day sounds like a civilized amount. But look closer. Of those 5.07 hours, fully 2.60 are spent watching television. Another 34 minutes go to playing games or using a computer for leisure. Reading for personal interest claims 12 minutes. Exercise gets 17. Socializing — face-to-face conversation with other humans — occupies exactly 35 minutes per day. [BLS ATUS, Table 1](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.t01.htm)

And socializing is in decline. In 2014, Americans spent 43 minutes per day socializing. By 2024, that number had fallen to 35 minutes — a 19 percent drop in a decade. The share of Americans who socialize on any given day fell from 38 percent to 30 percent over the same period. [BLS ATUS News Release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.nr0.htm)

> ![The Great Swap: Social time vs. screen time, 2003–2024. Face-to-face socializing has declined while digital media consumption has soared.](/api/blog/images/the-life-we-lost_chart3_social_vs_screen.png)
> *The great swap: as screen time rose from 5.5 to 8.6 daily hours between 2010 and 2024, face-to-face socializing fell from 42 to 35 minutes per day. The 2013 inflection point — the year smartphone adoption crossed 50% — marks the beginning of the accelerated decline.*
> Sources: BLS American Time Use Survey; eMarketer US Time Spent With Media 2025

Meanwhile, digital media consumption has risen to fill every remaining crease of the day. According to eMarketer, US adults will spend 8.6 hours per day with digital media in 2025 — more than double the time they spend in face-to-face social interaction, eating, and exercise combined. [eMarketer, "US Time Spent With Media 2025"](https://www.emarketer.com/content/us-time-spent-with-media-2025) Nielsen's Total Audience Report found that total media exposure — including TV, radio, smartphones, computers, and tablets — had reached nearly 12 hours per day by 2020. [Nielsen via TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2020/02/11/nielsen-streaming-wars-total-audience-report/)

The social toll is measurable. A 2024 study in the *Journal of Population Economics* analyzing two decades of ATUS data found that time spent alone in non-work, non-sleep hours increased from 285 minutes per day in 2003 to 309 minutes in 2019 — a rise of 146 additional hours of solitude per year. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the trend to 333 minutes per day by 2020. [J. J. VanHeuvelen, "A Twenty-First Century of Solitude?," *J. Population Economics* (2024)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00148-024-00978-0)

A separate study using the same data — 2003 to 2020 — showed that companionship time (shared leisure) fell from 202 minutes per day in 2003 to 182 minutes in 2019. Time with friends dropped by 3 percentage points. The decline was *already accelerating before the pandemic*, beginning around 2013 — the same year smartphone ownership in the US crossed 50 percent. [Kannan & Veazie, "US trends in social isolation," *NIH* (2022)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9811250/)

Pew Research Center found that 41 percent of US adults say they are online "almost constantly." Among 18- to 29-year-olds, the figure is 62 percent. [Pew Research Center, "Americans' Social Media Use 2025"](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/11/20/americans-social-media-use-2025/) Social media alone consumes roughly 45 minutes per day for the average adult — more than the total time spent in face-to-face conversation. [Nielsen Total Audience Report, Q1 2018](https://www.nielsen.com/insights/2018/time-flies-us-adults-now-spend-nearly-half-a-day-interacting-with-media/)

**VI. What a Day Means**

Lay the four days side by side.

The Hadza day: 5 hours of subsistence, 6.25 hours of sleep, 47 minutes of optional nap, and roughly 12 hours of time shared with people who know your voice, your history, your mood.

The pre-industrial English day: 7 hours of work (but only in season — 208 days of the year), prayer, ale, gossip, holiday, the slow drift of sunlight across the fields.

The Industrial Revolution day: 11 hours in the mill, six days a week, 306 days a year. The children working at 9. The clock that never stopped.

The modern American day: 8.6 hours of digital media. 35 minutes of conversation. 4.21 hours of TV for those over 65, because they have the most time and the least idea what to do with it. [BLS ATUS, Table 11A](https://www.bls.gov/charts/american-time-use/activity-leisure.htm)

We solved the problem of overwork — the Industrial Revolution's terrible gift — more or less. By the 1950s, we had clawed back the leisure that Voth's Londoners had lost. But we filled it with light. The 12 unstructured hours of the Hadza evening became 8.6 hours of glowing rectangles. The 53 holy days became 53 streaming series.

In 2003, the year the ATUS began, the first social media platforms were being born. Friendster launched. MySpace was a year away. The iPhone was four years in the future. Over the next two decades, a young American who spent 285 minutes per day alone — already a number that would have horrified any earlier human society — would see her solitude increase by nearly an hour per day. Not because she had to work more. Because the alternative was a differently lit room.

The evolutionary bargain we struck is becoming clear. For 200,000 years, we traded a few hours of work for a world of unmediated presence. For 200 years, we traded presence for productivity. For the last 20, we have been trading the remainder — the last unstructured hours — for the soft blue glow of everything, everywhere, all at once.

The question is whether we recognize what we gave away.
