# The Emergent Value Paradox: Why the Meaning of a Novel Shouldn't Matter, and Why It Does

*If physics has no room for value, then minds that emerge from physics should have no room for value either — and yet they are full of it, crowded with it, weeping over it. Novels that emerge from minds should carry no moral weight; they are, after all, only paper and vanity. And meanings that emerge from novels — those fragile, reader-born phantoms — should be the emptiest of all. Yet here we are, weeping over fiction. What gives?*

---

**Cambridge, 1903.** A forty-year-old philosopher sits in his study at Trinity College, putting the finishing touches on a book that will, he hopes, settle the foundations of ethics once and for all. The room is quiet. A gas lamp throws light across a desk stacked with notes on pleasure, on desire, on the good. George Edward Moore is trying to solve a puzzle that has troubled philosophers since Socrates: what *is* goodness?

His answer, when it comes, is not what anyone expected. Goodness, Moore concludes, is a simple, indefinable, non-natural property. You cannot break it down into pleasure, or evolutionary fitness, or anything else that science can measure. Any attempt to do so commits what he calls "the naturalistic fallacy" — the mistake of confusing a natural property with a normative one. Just because something *is* the case does not mean it *ought* to be. The gap between fact and value is not a crack you can leap across. It is a chasm. And the whole history of moral philosophy, Moore suggests, has been spent stumbling into it. [Moore, Principia Ethica, 1903](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781107282115)

The book made Moore famous. It also, perhaps without meaning to, set the stage for a much deeper and more unsettling question — one that reaches far beyond ethics into the very structure of reality. Philosophers, like everyone else, are at their most dangerous when they are doing precisely what they did not intend.

## I. From Physics to Mind

The intuition that consciousness arises from physical matter has become, in the twenty-first century, a scientific orthodoxy so settled that to question it is to confess oneself a mystic or a bore. But *how* it arises — what the exact relationship is between a configuration of neurons and the felt experience of seeing blue — remains the hardest problem in science, and therefore the most entertaining.

The leading formal account comes from Integrated Information Theory (IIT), developed by Giulio Tononi and his colleagues at the University of Wisconsin. IIT starts not from the brain but from experience itself. It identifies five essential properties of every conscious experience: it is *intrinsic* (it exists for the experiencer), *specific* (it is this experience and not another), *unitary* (it cannot be subdivided), *definite* (it has a border), and *structured* (it has composition). From these axioms, IIT derives postulates about physical systems: a substrate of consciousness must have cause-effect power that is for itself, specific, unitary, definite, and structured. The quantity of consciousness in a system is measured by **Φ** (phi) — the amount of integrated information it generates above and beyond what its parts generate separately. [Albantakis et al., IIT 4.0, PLOS Computational Biology, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011465)

![G. E. Moore in 1914. Photograph by Ray Strachey. Public domain via Wikimedia Commons.](/api/blog/images/emergent-value-paradox_ge_moore.jpg)
*G. E. Moore, photographed in 1914 — the year after Principia Ethica's eleventh reprint. He had spent the decade defending a simple, unsettling claim: that goodness cannot be found in nature. Ray Strachey. Public domain via Wikimedia Commons.*

This is emergence in its most rigorous form. The mind is dependent on the brain — change the neurons, and you change consciousness — but it is also autonomous in a meaningful sense. The cause-effect structure of a conscious system is not reducible to the sum of its parts. As Todd Feinberg argues in his theory of Neurobiological Emergentism, sentience emerges from complex nervous systems in three evolutionary stages: from single-celled sensing organisms (non-sentient, 3.5 billion years ago), through animals with simple nervous systems (presentient, 570 million years ago), to animals with complex central nervous systems (sentient, 560-520 million years ago). Each stage is a genuine novelty, not merely a rearrangement of what came before. [Feinberg, Neurobiological Emergentism, Frontiers in Psychology, 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1528982)

The philosophical implications are immediate and severe. If mind is a genuine emergence from physics, then it is one layer removed from fundamental reality. The particles that compose your neurons do not, themselves, have a point of view. They do not suffer or rejoice. They simply *are*. The experiencer, the "what it is like to be" that Nagel identified as the essential character of consciousness, is an additional ontological layer — real, causally efficacious, but not fundamental. [Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?", The Philosophical Review, 1974](https://doi.org/10.2307/2183914)

So the first link in the chain is forged: *minds arise from physics*, and in that arising, something genuinely new enters the universe — subjectivity, sensation, the raw material of experience. The universe, having spent billions of years doing nothing in particular, suddenly found itself blushing.

## II. From Mind to Novel

Once you have a mind — a conscious, experiencing, self-aware system — you have the capacity for symbolic representation. And once you have symbolic representation, you have the capacity for narrative. This is not a small step. It is the step that turns a creature that merely exists into a creature that can imagine existing otherwise.

Joseph Carroll, in his evolutionary account of literary meaning, argues that fictional narratives engage three sets of minds simultaneously: those of authors, characters, and readers. Meaning in fiction, he writes, "consists of experiences in individual minds: sensations, emotions, perceptions, and thoughts." The novel is not a passive container of information but an active cognitive technology — a tool for modeling possible worlds, simulating social interactions, and rehearsing emotional responses. [Carroll, "Minds and Meaning in Fictional Narratives," Evolutionary Psychology, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/gpr0000104)

Cognitive narratology has deepened this insight. David Herman argues that "both language generally and narrative specifically can be viewed as tool-systems for building mental models of the world." When a reader picks up a novel, she does not simply decode text. She performs a complex cognitive operation: constructing a storyworld, tracking the intentional states of characters, making inferences about causality, and responding emotionally to events that she knows perfectly well never happened. [Herman, "Narrative Theory and the Cognitive Sciences," Narrative Inquiry, 2001](https://doi.org/10.1075/ni.11.1.01her)

Mario Garcia's neural model of literary narrative proposes that narrative is "the result of an unconscious computation in the brain, a computation that arises from the dynamical interaction of specific innate and representational lower-order neuronal circuits and mappings." On this view, the novel is not a cultural artifact that happens to be processed by the brain. It is a direct expression of the brain's own cognitive architecture — a structured output of the same neural machinery that constructs our everyday experience of reality. [Garcia, "Literary Narrative as a Cognitive Structure in the Brain," Narrative Inquiry, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1075/ni.25.1.02gar)

The second link, then: *novels arise from minds*. And like the first link, this is a genuine emergence. A novel is not reducible to the neural firings that produced it, any more than a mind is reducible to the particles that compose it. Each level brings something ontologically new — which is a philosopher's way of saying that a good sentence is worth more than the electricity it cost to think it up.

## III. From Novel to Meaning

But a novel sitting on a shelf is just paper and ink, bound in cloth, gathering dust. The *meaning* of a novel — the felt significance, the interpretive resonance, the thing that makes you close the book and stare at the wall for ten minutes — does not exist in the text itself. It is enacted in the interaction between text and reader. Like a party that requires at least two guests before anything interesting happens, meaning is a social event pretending to be a property of objects.

Inna Livytska, writing from the perspective of global semiotics, argues that narrative discourse is a "self-organizing entity with the purposeful (telic) character of all its constituent parts which turn a static text into a dynamic whole in the process of reading." Meaning is not extracted from the text like a fossil from rock. It emerges through the interaction between the text's structural affordances and the reader's embodied subjectivity. [Livytska, "Narrative Discourse as an Emergent Phenomenon," Journal of Narrative and Language Studies, 2021](https://nalans.com/index.php/nalans/article/view/245)

Yanna Popova's enactive approach goes further. She argues that stories "do not happen in individual minds, either those of tellers or readers, but in the dynamic interaction between them." Narrative understanding is a form of "participatory sense-making" — a process in which meaning is not found but *made*, co-constructed between teller and reader through the irreducibly intersubjective nature of human cognition. [Popova, "Narrativity and Enaction," Frontiers in Psychology, 2014](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00895)

This is where the chain reaches its most precarious point. If meaning is an emergent property of the reader's interaction with a text — if it requires a conscious mind, a symbolic artifact produced by another conscious mind, and an active interpretive engagement between the two — then meaning is not merely one layer removed from fundamental reality. It is *three* layers removed. Physics begets mind. Mind begets novel. Novel begets meaning. Each step is a genuine emergence, each layer ontologically dependent on the ones below and irreducible to them.

Which brings us to the question that Moore, sitting in his Cambridge study, never quite got around to asking — perhaps because, like most philosophers, he was too busy answering questions no one had posed.

## IV. The Value Question

Moore argued that you cannot derive *ought* from *is*. If "good" is analyzed as "pleasure," he asked, is the question "but is pleasure good?" still open? Of course it is, he answered — and so goodness cannot be identical to any natural property. The open question argument became the foundation of non-naturalism in ethics. [Moore, Principia Ethica, 1903](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781107282115)

David Hume had anticipated the argument by more than a century. In his Treatise of Human Nature, he observed that moralists routinely slide from descriptive statements ("this is the case") to prescriptive ones ("this ought to be the case") without explaining how the transition is justified. "This small attention," he wrote, "would subvert all the vulgar systems of morality." [Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, 1739](https://doi.org/10.1093/oseo/instance.00046233)

![David Hume, painted by Allan Ramsay in 1766. National Galleries of Scotland. Public domain.](/api/blog/images/emergent-value-paradox_hume.jpg)
*David Hume, portrait by Allan Ramsay, 1766. The philosopher who first noticed that morality's foundation — an "ought" derived from an "is" — seemed to rest on an unspoken step. National Galleries of Scotland. Public domain.*

The standard reading of Hume's law — that you cannot derive normative conclusions from descriptive premises — is itself a normative claim. It tells us something about how we *ought* to reason. And this creates a curious self-referential problem: if the fact-value distinction is itself a value, then what grounds it? The law that forbids deriving an ought from an is turns out to be an ought itself, which is precisely the sort of irony that would have delighted Hume and infuriated Moore in equal measure.

But the problem sharpens considerably when we layer emergence on top of the is-ought gap. If mind is emergent from physics, and novels are emergent from mind, and meaning is emergent from the interaction of mind and novel, then *every normative claim about meaning* is separated from fundamental reality by at least three levels of ontological dependence. Each level, by the logic of emergence, brings something genuinely new. But each level also inherits the question: where does the normativity come from?

Emergent moral non-naturalism, recently defended by Umut Baysan, proposes that moral properties are emergent properties that confer *noncausal* powers on their bearers. On this view, moral properties are real and irreducible, dependent on descriptive base-level properties but not identical to them. The dependency relation is not one of metaphysical necessity but of contingent normative bridge principles. [Baysan, "Emergent Moral Non-Naturalism," Philosophy and Phenomenological Research, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/phpr.70057)

This is a sophisticated response to the problem. It accepts the reality of emergent normativity while acknowledging its metaphysical contingency. But it does not answer the question of why we should *care*. And that, for a theory of value, is rather the point.

## V. The Privileging Problem

Here is the argument in its most distilled form:

1. Minds emerge from physics.
2. Novels emerge from minds.
3. Meanings emerge from the interaction of novels and minds.
4. Therefore, any meaning — and any value that attaches to meaning — is at least three layers of emergence removed from fundamental reality.
5. Fundamental reality, as described by physics, contains no intrinsic values.
6. If no value exists at the foundation, then all values are emergent.
7. If all values are emergent, then no value is more "grounded" in reality than any other.
8. Therefore, there is no non-arbitrary criterion for privileging one emergent value over another.

This is the emergent value paradox. It does not claim that values are unreal — only that their claim to authority is structurally indistinguishable from any other emergent product of the physics-to-mind-to-novel-to-meaning chain. The moral landscape, it turns out, is as flat as the physical one.

The reductionist might simply accept the conclusion: values are illusions, useful fictions, evolutionary byproducts with no genuine authority. This is the position of the error theorist, most forcefully defended by J. L. Mackie, who argued that moral values are "queer" entities, unlike anything else in the natural world, and that our belief in them is a widespread mistake. [Mackie, Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong, 1977](https://doi.org/10.1093/0198245535.001.0001)

James Harold, writing about the intrinsic/extrinsic value distinction, identifies a subtler way out. He argues that the traditional dichotomy — values are either intrinsic (grounded in the object itself) or extrinsic (dependent on something else) — forces us into a foundationalist picture of value justification that excludes the possibility of *mutual support*. A coherence theory of value, he suggests, would allow values to justify each other in a network of reciprocal dependence, without requiring any single foundational value. [Harold, "Between Intrinsic and Extrinsic Value," Journal of Social Philosophy, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9833.2005.00260.x)

This is attractive. It rescues the meaning of a novel from the demand that it be grounded in fundamental physics. On the coherence picture, the value of *Anna Karenina* does not depend on a chain that terminates in quarks and leptons. It depends on its place in a web of human practices: the practice of reading, the practice of moral reflection, the practice of shared attention to the suffering of fictional characters. These practices justify each other. The value is real because the web is real.

But the coherence answer raises a question of its own. If the web of human practices *itself* emerges from minds, which emerge from physics, then the entire web is contingent. It could have been otherwise. It could collapse. In a thousand years, if no one reads Tolstoy, the value of *Anna Karenina* will not survive. The coherence of the web is not a foundation; it is a temporary pattern, a local fluctuation in the probability distribution of human culture.

And the same is true of every value we hold. Which is not to say that our values are worthless, but only that they are as fragile as the minds that hold them, and as precious as anything that can be lost.

## VI. The Human Scale

None of this, of course, stops us from caring. The heart has its reasons of which metaphysics knows nothing, and it does not particularly care to be corrected.

The reader who closes *Anna Karenina* with tears on her face is not making a metaphysical error. She is participating in the most complex emergent phenomenon we know — a meaning enacted between a text and a mind, three layers removed from fundamental reality, and none the less real for that distance. The distance, in fact, is what makes it interesting. If value were written into the fabric of the universe, we would not need to create it; we would merely discover it, like a fossil or a tax bill.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on emergent properties notes that the concept of emergence mediates "between extreme forms of dualism, which reject the micro-dependence of some entities, and reductionism, which rejects macro-autonomy." Emergence is not magic. It is the recognition that the universe generates genuine novelty at multiple levels of organization — and that the reality of these novel levels does not require them to be traceable, in any practical sense, to the level below. [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Emergent Properties," 2021](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/properties-emergent/)

Shelly Kagan, in his re-examination of intrinsic value, argued that we should leave open the possibility that value as an end might depend on relational properties, not just intrinsic ones. A painting's value might depend on the existence of beings who can appreciate it. A novel's meaning might depend on a reader who can enact it. This is not a sign of deficiency. It is a sign that value — like mind, like meaning — is an emergent phenomenon, dependent on the relationships between things, not just on the things themselves. [Kagan, "Rethinking Intrinsic Value," 1998](https://campuspress.yale.edu/shellykagan/files/2015/04/Rethinking-Intrinsic-Value-1fann69.pdf)

Moore was right about one thing: you cannot reduce goodness to a natural property without losing something essential. But he may have been wrong to conclude that goodness therefore belongs to a non-natural realm. The alternative — the one the emergentist tradition offers — is that goodness is as natural as consciousness, as natural as narrative, as natural as the meaning that flickers into existence when a mind meets a text. Goodness is not a visitor from another world; it is a native of this one, and it speaks with the local accent.

If physics has no room for value, it has no room for mind either. And we know, with the certainty of immediate experience, that mind exists. The mistake is not in valuing what emerges. The mistake is in thinking that what is fundamental is all that is real. It is the error of the man who stares so intently at the foundations of a house that he forgets to live in it.

The meaning of a novel is not less real because it depends on a reader. Consciousness is not less real because it depends on a brain. And value is not less real because it depends on minds, and narratives, and the strange, fragile act of making sense together. If dependence were a disqualification, nothing would be real at all, and we should have to give up weeping over fiction — which, of all the sacrifices philosophy has demanded, is surely the most unreasonable.

On a shelf in Cambridge, a first edition of *Principia Ethica* sits gathering dust. It is worth something to a collector. It is worth something else to a philosopher who reads it and feels the force of Moore's argument. And it is worth something else again to anyone who has ever wondered whether the things they care about are real.

The answer, on the emergentist view, is simpler than Moore imagined: they are as real as you are. And you are as real as the universe that made you. That is not a foundation, perhaps, but it is enough. It is, when one thinks about it, rather more than enough.
