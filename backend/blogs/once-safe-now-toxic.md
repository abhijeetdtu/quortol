# The Magic Mineral and the Morning Sickness Pill

## From radium-laced health tonics to the "forever chemicals" in your non-stick pan — a look at the materials we trusted, the ones that betrayed us, and the ones we are only beginning to suspect.

---

**Scene**

In 1903, the public queued in Germany and Austria for the privilege of drinking radium-infused water. The health spas promised that this radioactive elixir would cure arthritis, restore vitality, and — that most optimistic of medical claims — "rejuvenate the aging." By 1918, a best-selling tonic called Radithor, consisting of bottled water laced with radium-226 and radium-228, was being sold in American drugstores with the endorsement of physicians who recommended it for everything from impotence to high blood pressure. The logic was elegantly simple: if a little is good, more is better, and therefore any amount is not nearly enough.

In 1932, the industrialist Eben Byers died a death spectacular enough to make front-page news — his skull crumbling, his jaw rotting from within, his body consumed by bone cancer. He had consumed roughly fourteen hundred bottles of Radithor over four years. His passing forced the fledgling Food and Drug Administration to finally regulate radioactive patent medicines. The lesson, however, diffused more slowly than radium.

The history of human civilization is, to a remarkable degree, the history of materials we loved for their usefulness and only later discovered were killing us. This is a tour through that graveyard of good intentions, with a glance at the plots we are currently digging.

**The Magic Mineral**

No substance better illustrates this tragic arc than asbestos. The ancient Greeks noted its curious resistance to fire and called it "amiantos" — undefiled. Pliny the Elder wrote of a "linum vivum," a living linen that could be thrown into flames and emerge unscathed. Charlemagne is said to have astonished his guests by casting his asbestos tablecloth into the fireplace after a feast and retrieving it unblemished, as if the laws of nature had agreed to make an exception for his dinner party.

But asbestos entered its golden age in the late nineteenth century, when industrialization developed an insatiable appetite for fireproof insulation, cheap building materials, and durable brake pads. The Hatschek machine, invented in 1900, enabled the mass production of asbestos-cement sheets and opened a floodgate. By 1910, world production had exceeded 80,000 metric tons — a 300 to 400 percent increase from 1900. The United States alone consumed 55 percent of the global supply.

[USGS Circular 1255KK — Mineral Commodity Profiles: Asbestos](https://pubs.usgs.gov/circ/2005/1255/kk/Circ_1255KK.pdf)

The asbestos industry embraced its own mythology with enthusiasm. Trade publications called it the "magic mineral." It was described as "the most important of the non-metallic mineral products of the world — and certainly one of the most wonderful." By 1958, asbestos was employed in roughly three thousand different applications — pipe insulation, ceiling tiles, floor tiles, roofing shingles, brake linings, gaskets, textiles, and even cigarette filters, as if the tobacco industry felt its product was not already hazardous enough.

[USGS Circular 1298 — Worldwide Asbestos Supply and Consumption Trends from 1900 through 2003](https://pubs.usgs.gov/circ/2006/1298/c1298.pdf)

World production peaked in 1977 at 4.8 million metric tons, produced by twenty-five countries and used by roughly eighty-five.

The health warnings had been present since the 1920s, when early studies linked asbestos exposure to asbestosis — a progressive scarring of the lung. But it was a landmark study by Dr. Irving Selikoff in the 1960s that conclusively proved the link between asbestos and lung cancer, and later mesothelioma, a cancer so aggressive and so intimately tied to asbestos that it serves as the disease's signature. By the time the EPA began issuing bans in the 1970s, millions of tons of asbestos were already embedded in the walls, ceilings, pipes, and brake pads of the American built environment.

[USGS Circular 1298 — Health impact section](https://pubs.usgs.gov/circ/2006/1298/c1298.pdf)

As late as 2019, the EPA had to issue a Significant New Use Rule to prevent companies from resuming discontinued uses of asbestos without review. By then, U.S. consumption had dropped to 450 metric tons — approximately 0.01 percent of the peak. Some forty countries have now banned asbestos outright. The United States has not. The "magic mineral" remains legal, still in limited use, and still killing approximately 255,000 people globally each year.

[EPA SNUR 2019](https://www.epa.gov/assessing-and-managing-chemicals-under-tsca/asbestos-significant-new-use-rule-snur)

**The Poison in the Pipeline**

Lead has a longer and more tangled history than asbestos. The Romans used it in their water pipes, their cooking vessels, and even as a sweetener for wine. Some historians have speculated — controversially — that lead poisoning contributed to the decline of the Roman Empire. Whether or not this is true, the Romans certainly managed to achieve levels of exposure that would impress a modern toxicologist.

Modern civilization repeated the error at a larger scale and with better data. In the early 1920s, tetraethyl lead was added to gasoline as an anti-knock agent. The lead industry — led by the Ethyl Corporation and General Motors — insisted the additive was safe. Public health advocates raised concerns almost immediately, but the industry argued successfully against limits for decades. The corporate objection to regulation is, after all, the most timeless of human institutions.

A 1960s study found that lead levels in air were highest in heavy traffic zones and that blood lead levels increased with air lead levels. The report nonetheless concluded that the general population showed no evidence of "abnormal" exposure — a finding that must have been a considerable comfort to anyone not yet convinced that traffic was a normal part of life.

[CDC — Control of Lead Sources in the United States, 1970-2017](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/78545)

Lead paint was an even larger source. Before 1940, interior paints contained roughly 50 percent lead by weight. Children ate the sweet-tasting paint chips. They breathed the dust from painted windows and doors. By the time the Lead-Based Paint Poisoning Prevention Act was passed in 1971 — which defined lead-based paint as anything with more than 1 percent lead — an estimated 23 million housing units still contained lead-based paint hazards, including 3.6 million homes with children under six.

The phaseout of leaded gasoline, which began in 1973 and was finally banned for road vehicles in 1995, produced one of the great public health triumphs of the twentieth century. Between 1976 and 2016, the geometric mean blood lead level of the U.S. population aged one to seventy-four dropped from 12.8 mcg/dL to 0.82 mcg/dL — a decline of 93.6 percent.

[CDC — Lead data 1976-2016](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/78545)

![Blood lead decline](/api/blog/images/once-safe-now-toxic_lead_decline.png)

But that statistic conceals an uglier distribution. Communities with older housing stock, particularly Black and low-income neighborhoods, still carry significantly higher exposure levels. The water crisis in Flint, Michigan — where corrosive water leached lead from aging pipes into the drinking supply of 100,000 residents — was a stark reminder that the lead problem has not been solved. It has merely been buried, pipe by pipe, in the infrastructure we have not yet troubled to replace.

[ATSDR — Toxicological Profile for Lead](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK589542/)

**The Girls Who Glowed in the Dark**

The radium dial painters of the 1910s and 1920s were mostly young women — some as young as fifteen — employed to paint luminous numbers on watch faces and instrument dials. The paint contained radium-226 and radium-228, which had the useful property of glowing in the dark and the less advertised property of emitting radiation into anything that came near it. The women were instructed to "point" their brushes by drawing them to a fine tip between their lips — a technique that produced a sharper point and a more precise application, and that also guaranteed a direct oral dose of radium with every brushstroke.

The company literature assured them the paint was harmless. Some women, in a spirit of youthful exuberance that now reads as tragic irony, painted their teeth and fingernails with it as a party trick, laughing as they glowed in the dark. By the early 1920s, the first symptoms appeared: fatigue, toothaches, jaw infections that would not heal. The company dentist diagnosed "pyorrhea" — gum disease, entirely unrelated to their work. The professional instinct, when faced with an uncomfortable truth, is always to find a more comfortable name for it.

[Argonne National Laboratory — Radium in Humans: A Review of U.S. Studies](https://publications.anl.gov/anlpubs/1994/11/16311.pdf)

It took the determination of Dr. Harrison Martland, a New Jersey pathologist, to prove what was actually happening. In a landmark 1925 paper, Martland and his colleagues detected gamma rays emanating from the bodies of living dial painters and measured radon in their breath. The radium had lodged in their bones, where it continuously emitted radiation. The women developed a distinctive constellation of diseases: osteosarcomas of the bone, carcinomas of the mastoid and paranasal sinuses, and "radium jaw" — a gruesome necrosis in which the jawbone rotted from within, as though the body were finally confessing what it had swallowed.

The cases were not limited to New Jersey. At the Waterbury Clock Company in Connecticut, dial painters were also falling ill. The company deliberately concealed the illnesses, and it took middle-class women reformers — allied with the New Jersey victims — to expose the cover-up in 1925. The instinct to hide what one has done is almost as reliable as the instinct to do it in the first place.

[University of Illinois — Managing an Epidemic: Industrial Radium Poisoning and the Waterbury Clock Company](https://doi.org/10.5406/26395991.64.2.04)

![Radium Girls](/api/blog/images/once-safe-now-toxic_recognition_gap.png)

The radium tragedy gave medicine its first clear understanding of radiation-induced bone cancer. It also gave the labor movement a cause célèbre that helped establish workplace safety laws. The women who painted dials with their lips did not know they were human canaries in a radium mine. But their deaths changed how we think about industrial toxins — a cold comfort, but a comfort nonetheless.

**The Pesticide That Would Not Go Away**

DDT — dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane — was first synthesized in 1874, but it was not until 1939 that the Swiss chemist Paul Hermann Müller discovered its insecticidal properties. During World War II, DDT was sprayed on soldiers to control typhus and malaria with an effectiveness that seemed almost miraculous. Müller won the Nobel Prize in 1948, which is one way to measure success; the environmental devastation that followed is another.

After the war, DDT was released for agricultural use. It was cheap, persistent, and devastatingly effective against crop pests. Farmers sprayed it liberally on fruit, vegetables, cotton, and grain. Suburban homeowners applied it to lawns and gardens. It was sprayed from airplanes over entire neighborhoods to control mosquitoes. What could possibly go wrong with releasing a persistent toxin into the general environment in vast quantities?

The very property that made DDT so effective — its persistence — was also its poison. DDT does not break down easily. It accumulates in soil, water, and living tissue. It travels up the food chain, concentrating in predators at the top. By the 1950s, bald eagles, peregrine falcons, and other birds of prey were experiencing catastrophic population declines because DDT caused their eggshells to thin to the point of breakage. The symbol of American majesty had been undone by a pesticide, which is the sort of irony that nature specializes in and that humans consistently fail to anticipate.

[EPA — DDT Regulatory History: A Brief Survey (to 1975)](https://www.epa.gov/archive/epa/aboutepa/ddt-regulatory-history-brief-survey-1975.html)

Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring*, published in 1962, synthesized decades of scientific research into a devastating indictment of indiscriminate pesticide use. The chemical industry attacked Carson personally — calling her hysterical, unscientific, a "bird lover" who would rather see children die of malaria than tolerate a few dead robins. The personal attack is always the first refuge of an industry that has run out of scientific arguments. But the evidence held.

In 1972, EPA Administrator William Ruckelshaus issued the final cancellation of remaining crop uses of DDT, effective December 31, 1972. His decision was based on "findings of persistence, transport, biomagnification, toxicological effects and on the absence of benefits of DDT in relation to the availability of effective and less environmentally harmful substitutes."

[EPA — DDT: A Review of Scientific and Economic Aspects of the Decision to Ban Its Use as a Pesticide](https://www.epa.gov/archive/epa/sites/production/files/documents/DDT.pdf)

But DDT never really went away. It is still used for malaria control in parts of Africa and Asia. And it persists in the global environment — DDT and its metabolites can still be detected in Arctic ice cores, decades after its heaviest use. The substance that was supposed to be controlled ended up as a permanent record in the planet's most remote archive.

**The Pill That Broke 10,000 Babies**

Thalidomide was introduced in West Germany in 1957 as a sedative and treatment for morning sickness. It was marketed as completely safe — so safe, indeed, that it was sold without prescription. By 1960, it had become Germany's most popular sleeping tablet. It was sold in 46 countries under at least 37 different brand names. The number of names is itself a sort of confession: when a drug needs that many identities, one suspects it is trying to be difficult to associate with any particular disaster.

At 7:12 on a Tuesday in November 1961, a German pediatrician named Widukind Lenz telephoned the manufacturer Chemie Grünenthal to report that he had identified a cluster of newborns with phocomelia — a rare condition in which the long bones of the arms and legs are severely shortened or absent. All of the mothers had taken thalidomide during early pregnancy. On the same day, independently, the Australian obstetrician William McBride published a letter in *The Lancet* making the same connection. The coincidence suggests that the truth was not so much discovered as it was finally impossible to ignore.

[NEJM — The Saga of Thalidomide: Neuropathy to Embryopathy](https://www.nejm.org/doi/abs/10.1056/NEJM196212062672305)

The drug was withdrawn from the German market in November 1961. But the damage was done. The most conservative estimate is that 10,000 children were born with severe malformations. Thousands more pregnancies ended in miscarriage. The "thalidomide babies" grew up without arms, without legs, with missing ears, with heart defects, with malformed intestines — each a living monument to a sedative that was supposed to be completely safe.

The United States was spared the worst of the tragedy because of one person: Dr. Frances Kelsey, an FDA medical officer who refused to approve the drug despite intense pressure from the manufacturer Richardson-Merrell. Kelsey had concerns about reports of peripheral neuropathy — nerve damage — in thalidomide users, and she insisted on more safety data. Her caution saved hundreds, possibly thousands, of American babies. She received the President's Award for Distinguished Federal Civilian Service from John F. Kennedy in 1962. The medal was given for the unfashionable virtue of saying no.

[Science — Thalidomide coverage, 1962](https://www.science.org/doi/pdf/10.1126/science.136.3517.683)

The thalidomide disaster led to sweeping reform of drug regulation worldwide. In the United States, the Kefauver-Harris Amendments to the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act were passed in 1962, requiring manufacturers to prove the efficacy and safety of drugs before marketing. It was the single most important reform in American pharmaceutical law — and it required ten thousand damaged children to achieve it.

**The Contenders**

The pattern is clear enough: a material is introduced because it offers real benefits. Its risks are unknown, underestimated, or suppressed. Decades pass. Accumulating evidence forces a reckoning. By the time the material is banned or restricted, it is already embedded everywhere. The pattern is so consistent that one begins to suspect it is not a flaw in our system but a feature of it.

Which brings us to the question: what are we currently getting wrong?

**PFAS — The Forever Chemicals**

Perfluoroalkyl and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) were developed in the 1940s and 1950s for their ability to repel water, oil, and grease. They are used in non-stick cookware, waterproof clothing, stain-resistant carpets, food packaging, and firefighting foam. They are called "forever chemicals" because the carbon-fluorine bond is so strong that they do not degrade in the environment. The name is meant to describe their chemistry, but it also describes their social behavior: they will outlast the companies that made them, the regulators who permitted them, and quite possibly the civilization that found them so useful.

Between 1999 and 2004, PFAS compounds were detected in 95 to 100 percent of blood samples from the U.S. general population. The primary routes of exposure are drinking water, food, and indoor dust. Communities near PFAS manufacturing facilities have been found with dramatically elevated levels in their water supplies.

[ATSDR — Public Health Statement for Perfluoroalkyls](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/toxprofiles/tp200-c1-b.pdf)

The health effects under investigation include increased cholesterol levels, liver damage, reduced fertility, increased risk of high blood pressure in pregnancy, thyroid dysfunction, and certain cancers. The EPA has issued health advisories for PFOA and PFOS — two of the most studied PFAS compounds — at levels of 0.004 parts per trillion for PFOA and 0.02 parts per trillion for PFOS. These are concentrations so vanishingly small that they are almost philosophical: the agency's assessment is that there is no safe level of exposure.

[EPA — Our Current Understanding of PFAS](https://www.epa.gov/pfas/our-current-understanding-human-health-and-environmental-risks-pfas)

In 2024, the EPA designated two of the most common PFAS compounds — PFOA and PFOS — as hazardous substances under the Superfund law. But there are over 12,000 known PFAS compounds, and only a handful have been studied for toxicity. The rest are, legally speaking, presumed innocent until proven guilty — which is an admirable principle in criminal law but a rather unsettling one in toxicology.

**Microplastics — The Invisible Invasion**

Plastic production has increased from 2 million metric tons per year in 1950 to over 400 million metric tons today. A significant portion of that plastic breaks down into particles smaller than 5 millimeters — microplastics — or smaller than 100 nanometers — nanoplastics. These particles have now been detected everywhere: in the deepest ocean trenches, in Arctic snow, in the air we breathe, in the food we eat. We set out to make a material that would last forever, and we succeeded beyond our wildest expectations.

In humans, microplastics have been found in blood, lungs, liver, kidneys, placenta, breast milk, and testicular tissue. A review of 91 studies published in 2023 confirmed that microplastic exposure is "ubiquitous" and that particles have been detected in every human biological matrix tested to date. We are not merely surrounded by plastic; we are becoming it.

[Environmental Research — Human biomonitoring of microplastics and health implications](https://www.ovid.com/journals/envres/pdf/10.1016/j.envres.2023.116966)

The health effects are not yet fully understood. In vitro and animal studies suggest that microplastics can trigger inflammation, oxidative stress, and DNA damage — the three horsemen of chronic disease. A 2024 review in *Environmental Research* found that exposure potentially leads to respiratory disorders, cardiovascular disease, and cancer.

[ScienceDirect — The potential impact of nano- and microplastics on human health, 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0013935124004390)

The World Health Organization, in a 2024 technical report, acknowledged that the available data are insufficient for a full quantitative risk assessment but called for urgent research. The pattern is familiar: we are in the "presumed safe" phase, and the evidence is only beginning to accumulate. We have been here before.

**Glyphosate — The Most Widely Used Herbicide**

Glyphosate was introduced by Monsanto in 1974 as the active ingredient in Roundup. It is now the most widely used herbicide in the world, applied to crops, gardens, and public spaces in vast quantities. Its use increased roughly fifteenfold after the introduction of genetically engineered glyphosate-resistant crops in the 1990s. The solution to the problem of too much herbicide was, naturally, more herbicide.

In March 2015, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) — the cancer agency of the World Health Organization — classified glyphosate as "probably carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2A). The classification was based on limited evidence of cancer in humans (specifically non-Hodgkin lymphoma) and sufficient evidence of cancer in experimental animals, supported by strong evidence of genotoxicity.

[IARC — Glyphosate Monograph, 2015](https://www.iarc.who.int/featured-news/media-centre-iarc-news-glyphosate/)

The reaction was immediate and polarized. Bayer, which acquired Monsanto in 2018, has maintained that glyphosate is safe. Other regulatory agencies, including the EPA in the United States and the European Food Safety Authority, have reached conclusions different from IARC's — classifying glyphosate as not likely carcinogenic to humans. The divergence stems from differences in which studies are considered and how weights are assigned. Science, it turns out, is like any other human endeavor: what you find depends on where you look, and where you look depends on who is paying for the lamp.

The World Health Organization itself has been caught in the crossfire. An internal WHO document leaked in 2016 suggested that a scientist working on the IARC evaluation may have pre-determined the outcome. Independent reviews have since vindicated the IARC process, but the controversy illustrates how difficult it becomes to assess risk once large economic interests are at stake. When money enters the room, the truth tends to excuse itself.

**The Shape of the Pattern**

What unites these stories is not malice — at least not always. Asbestos was genuinely useful. DDT saved lives from malaria. Lead improved engine performance and made paint more durable. Thalidomide helped pregnant women sleep. PFAS makes pans non-stick and jackets waterproof. Glyphosate makes farming more efficient. Microplastics are an unintended consequence of a material we designed to be indestructible. The road to the hospital is paved with good intentions, and also with quite useful industrial chemicals.

What unites them is a failure of foresight — and a system that places the burden of proof on the victims rather than the producers. A material is introduced. It is assumed safe until proven dangerous. It takes decades, often generations, for the evidence to accumulate. And by the time it does, exposure is already universal. The precautionary principle is an excellent idea, but it has never been popular with anyone who had a product to sell.

The question is not whether we are getting something wrong today. We almost certainly are. The question is which of the chemicals we now use casually — which of the materials in our kitchens, our cosmetics, our water bottles, our clothes — will be the subject of a future article like this one, fifty years from now. What are we currently mistaking for safety, simply because the evidence has not yet arrived?

The pattern is older than the radium girls. It is older than leaded gasoline. It is embedded in the structure of industrial civilization. We discover the magic mineral. We fall in love with it. And then, one day, we realize it has been in our bones all along — sometimes literally.
