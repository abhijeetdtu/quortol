# The Great Reinvention: How Small-Town Pacific Northwest Learned to Make a Living From Paradise

**By Staff** · June 8, 2026

CATHLAMET, Wash. — Wahkiakum County has no Starbucks, no McDonald's, and — most conspicuously — no stoplight. The Columbia River glides past town at a pace that appears to have infected its human inhabitants: slow, deliberate, serenely indifferent to the century pressing in upon it.

On a June morning, Craig Sears, 77, pilots his 1952 MGTD down Main Street — the wood-framed roadster he rebuilt himself over forty-seven years, which is roughly the same span in which the local economy has been patiently falling apart. The county courthouse, a modest brick building with a clock tower, surveys a main drag where the *Wahkiakum County Eagle* has been published since 1891, and where the Pioneer Center, a former church built in 1895, now houses the public library. The town contains fewer than 500 people, making it the seat of Washington's third-least-populous county. And it is, in miniature, the story of how the Pacific Northwest small town is being reinvented — not by the people who grew up here, but by the people who found it after the old economy stopped working.

There is a kind of poetry in the numbers. Between the late 1990s and 2024, the number of logging jobs in Wahkiakum County fell from 160 to just 30, according to state employment data. During the same period, the county's labor force shrank by nearly 40 percent — from 1,957 workers to 1,242. The timber and fishing economy that built this corner of Washington did not decline gradually; it fell, and it did not get back up.

![Faceted bar chart showing the decline of logging jobs from 160 to 30, the shrinking labor force from 1,957 to 1,242, and the rise of retirement-age population from 25% to 45% in Wahkiakum County, Washington.](/api/blog/images/pnw-reinvention_resource_decline.png)

What rose in its place is harder to name. The percentage of retirement-age residents jumped from one-quarter of the population in 2000 to nearly 45 percent by 2024. Home prices have roughly doubled within the last decade to a median of $467,500. The share of adults with bachelor's degrees rose from 15.3 percent in 2010 to 22.3 percent in 2023. And 59 percent of all income earned by county residents in 2020 came from jobs held outside Wahkiakum County — a figure made possible by the same remote-work revolution that has rearranged downtowns from Seattle to San Francisco. The town no longer produces much of anything. It simply is. And for the moment, being is enough.

The small towns of the Pacific Northwest are undergoing their most profound economic transformation since the railroads arrived in the 1880s. A resource-extraction economy built on timber, fishing, and farming — industries that demanded physical presence, tolerated danger, and rewarded endurance — is being replaced by something else: a lifestyle economy built on scenery, solitude, and the willingness of outsiders to pay for it.

But the transaction is not a simple exchange. The new money brings new tensions. And the question quietly asked from Cathlamet to the Methow Valley to the Oregon Coast is whether a town can reinvent itself without losing its people.

## What Was Lost

The story of the Pacific Northwest small town in the twentieth century is a story of extraction. Timber built Aberdeen, Forks, and Port Angeles. Fishing built Astoria, Ilwaco, and Cathlamet. Agriculture built Walla Walla, Hood River, and the Yakima Valley. These were not genteel economies. They were dangerous, cyclical, and physically punishing. But they supported families, funded schools, and anchored communities to specific places with specific purposes — which is more than can be said for an economy whose chief product is a photograph.

By the late 1990s, that model was breaking. Automation, consolidation, and the exhaustion of easily accessible old-growth forests reduced timber employment across the region. In Wahkiakum County, logging jobs dwindled to a tenth of their former strength. On the Oregon Coast, the pattern repeated: between 2003 and 2021, the proportion of personal income derived from traditional employment in Tillamook County dropped by nearly 10 percentage points, according to a study commissioned by the Oregon Coast Visitors Association.

What filled the gap was not a new industry in the traditional sense. Transfer payments — Social Security, Medicare, income maintenance programs — rose from 22 percent of total personal income on the Oregon Coast in 2003 to 37 percent by 2021. Coastwide, income from these programs more than doubled in inflation-adjusted terms, from $1.8 billion to $4.8 billion.

![Grouped bar chart showing the shift in Oregon Coast personal income sources from 2003 to 2021: net earnings declining from 53% to 46%, investment income from 23% to 17%, and transfer payments rising from 24% to 37%.](/api/blog/images/pnw-reinvention_income_shift.png)

"The continued absolute and proportional growth of transfer payments and the continued decline of proportional net earnings" was the study's central finding — a clinical description of what happens when a working-age population ages out and is not replaced.

In Jefferson County, home to Port Townsend, the timber industry that once dominated employment gave way first to marine trades and then to tourism. By 2023, the county's largest employment sector was government. The accommodation and food services sector provided 12 percent of county jobs. The old world — pulp mills, logging crews, commercial fishing boats — had receded to the margins. It had not so much been replaced as outlived.

## What Arrived

If the old economy was about what you could take from the land, the new economy is about what you can see, breathe, and photograph.

The numbers are staggering. In 2024, Washington residents and visitors logged an estimated 437 million days of outdoor recreation — 66 percent of them on public lands — generating $14.6 billion in trip-related spending on lodging, restaurants, gas stations, grocers, and gear shops. The Washington Recreation and Conservation Office's 2025 economic assessment, based on mobile device data and statewide surveys, found that outdoor recreation supported tens of thousands of jobs, many in the small gateway communities that surround the state's national parks, forests, and coastlines.

Olympic National Park alone drew nearly 3 million visitors in 2023, who spent $279 million in gateway communities like Port Angeles, Forks, and Port Townsend — the last a Victorian seaport that has reinvented itself as a destination for artists, boaters, and cultural tourists. By 2023, direct visitor spending in Jefferson County had reached $159.9 million, a 26 percent increase from 2019, generating $14.1 million in state and local tax revenue.

The Oregon Coast tells a similar story. A 2022 economic analysis found that outdoor recreation supported $16 billion in spending across the state, contributing $7.5 billion to Oregon's GDP and supporting 192,000 full- and part-time jobs. Travel tourism alone accounted for 18 percent of net earnings in coastal counties, according to the 2021 Oregon Coast Sources of Income Study — more than fishing (10 percent), agriculture (4 percent), and timber (6 percent) combined. The visitor has become the economy's most valuable crop.

But the most transformative force to arrive in small-town PNW may not be tourism at all. It is remote work.

In 2024, 27.5 percent of Washington workers and 25.2 percent of Oregon workers teleworked at least some hours, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics — both well above the national average of 22.8 percent. The U.S. Census Bureau's American Community Survey found that 13.8 percent of American workers usually worked from home in 2023, more than double the 5.7 percent who did so in 2019. And while the overall telework rate has declined from its pandemic peak, it remains structurally higher than before COVID-19 — a permanent shift in where and how work happens. The office, it turns out, was never the point. The point was the paycheck that let you live somewhere else.

The implications for small towns are profound. In Wahkiakum County, 59 percent of all income in 2020 was earned by residents whose jobs were physically located elsewhere. In the Methow Valley, the remote-worker influx has been even more dramatic: a 2021 economic study by the nonprofit TwispWorks found that remote workers in the valley had a median wage of $202,000 — more than three and a half times the $57,779 median income of families who both lived and worked locally.

This is the reinvention in its purest form. The small town no longer needs to produce anything. It just needs to be beautiful.

## The Price of Paradise

The gap between the old economy and the new one is measured not just in dollars but in access.

In Port Townsend, the median home price reached $543,400 in 2024 — an 8.1 percent increase from the previous year. The median household income, by contrast, was $62,606. More than 13 percent of the population lives below the poverty line. A 2023 analysis by the Washington Center for Real Estate Research found Jefferson County ranks 38th out of 39 Washington counties in first-time home buyer affordability. The city has just 378 multi-family housing units for a population of 10,678.

The rental vacancy rate hovers between zero and 1 percent. The first new multi-family apartment building built in Port Townsend in 14 years — a project called 7th Haven — completed construction recently. Before that, nothing.

"What people are starting to understand is that their local coffee shop is closed on Sundays or they can't go out to dinner after 8 p.m. because of a lack of staffing," Liz Revord, director of the grassroots Housing Solutions Network, told The Urbanist in 2023. "And that lack of staffing isn't because nobody wants to work anymore. It's because nobody can afford to live in Port Townsend."

The Methow Valley presents an even starker divide. More than 30 percent of the valley's population derives at least part of its income from outside the area. About 30 percent of jobs are in retail or recreation services — sectors that pay a fraction of what remote tech workers earn. Housing prices increased 14.7 percent from 2020 alone. Between 2018 and 2020, the average home price jumped by $105,000 to $499,000.

Sierra Golden, a spokesperson for a local health care provider, told the Seattle Times in 2022 that many staff could not afford to live in the area. Some workers sleep in their cars during the summer and on friends' couches in the winter. There is no emergency shelter in the valley.

In Wahkiakum County, the math is perhaps cruelest. The median home price is $467,500. The average annual salary, at about $43,000, is the lowest of any county in Washington, according to state data from 2024. That means the typical home costs more than ten times the typical annual earnings.

![Horizontal bar chart comparing average weekly wages across Washington counties in Q1 2025. King County towers at $2,675 while Wahkiakum County bottoms out at $903, well below the national average of $1,589.](/api/blog/images/pnw-reinvention_county_wages.png)

"We feel sorry for some of the young folks that are trying to come up and want to live here," Dan Cothren, a longtime county commissioner and former logger, told the Columbian in 2025. Most young people, he said, must commute to Longview or Portland to find work that can support a family.

## The Uneven Reinvention

Not every small PNW town is struggling to manage the transition. Some are thriving.

Ridgefield, a small city in Clark County just north of Portland, saw its population soar 45.5 percent between 2020 and 2023, from 10,330 to 15,027 — one of the fastest growth rates in Washington. The town has managed to absorb newcomers while maintaining its small-town character, benefiting from its proximity to the Portland metro area's job market and its own developing downtown.

Cathlamet is cautiously optimistic. The Elochoman Slough Marina, run by Wahkiakum Port District No. 1, has diversified beyond fishing to offer cabins, yurts, and 45 RV campsites with power and water. New restaurants have opened along the riverfront, including Salt and Tomato and River Mile 38 Brewing, which draw yachters from as far as Portland.

Garrett Radcliffe moved to Cathlamet from San Jose, California, during the pandemic, seeking "peace, tranquility, get away from the rat race." Now he works half a dozen paid and unpaid gigs — for the chamber of commerce, local schools, restaurants, and the fire department. His wife works remotely for LinkedIn. They found a community eager to welcome them.

"We don't have one stoplight in Wahkiakum County," said longtime Realtor David Nelson. "You have to create your own entertainment and love what you see."

Port Townsend is pursuing a different reinvention strategy, leaning into its identity as a creative economy hub. The city has the highest share of creative-sector GDP in a state that itself leads the nation in creative economy output, though the share of creative jobs in Port Townsend has slipped 1.1 percent in the last five years. In 2025, the city brought the Port Townsend Creative District under its management, partnering with the Washington State Arts Commission to support artists, galleries, and cultural institutions.

The Port Townsend comprehensive economic development plan, adopted in 2025, makes the tension explicit: "The focus of the strategy is maintaining and enhancing sustainable year-round opportunities for visitation. Reducing the 'seasonality' of the tourist industry could reduce the fluctuation in income, employment, and tax revenues."

The plan also acknowledges a harder reality: many residents feel "peak season tourist volumes in Port Townsend are at or near the saturation point."

Forks, the gateway town to Olympic National Park's Hoh Rain Forest, learned just how dependent it had become on tourism when the only road to the rainforest washed out in December 2024. Businesses panicked. A scramble for funding involving the governor's office, the state economic development reserve account, and a last-minute GoFundMe raised $27,240 from local businesses and individuals — enough to unlock $623,000 in state emergency funds.

"Forks had the busiest year in history this past year, and now we're looking at what to expect," Lissy Andros, executive director of the Forks Chamber of Commerce, told SFGATE in early 2025. "We wouldn't be business owners if we weren't optimistic in nature ... but if we didn't have the park, we would cease to exist."

## The Question That Remains

The Pacific Northwest small town has always reinvented itself. Port Townsend was a bustling seaport in the 1880s, then a sleepy backwater for a century, then a destination for retirees and tourists. Forks was a logging town, then a Twilight-themed tourist stop, then a national park gateway. Cathlamet fished and logged, then it didn't, and now it's figuring out what comes next. The only constant is that nothing is constant — a truth small towns have understood longer than the tech economy has been inventing disruption.

What is different this time is the scale and speed of the change, and the precariousness of the people caught in the middle.

Nationally, small towns with fewer than 5,000 residents grew by just 0.1 percent between 2024 and 2025, according to Census Bureau Vintage 2025 estimates released in May 2026 — barely a pulse. In the West, the rate was 0.2 percent. The USDA Economic Research Service found that despite an overall nonmetro population increase, 51 percent of nonmetro counties still lost population between 2020 and 2024. The growth is concentrated in recreation-dependent counties adjacent to metro areas — places like the ones profiled here — while remote rural counties continue to empty out.

The towns that are growing are growing because they are beautiful. But beauty, in the new economy, is a commodity with a complex price tag.

In Cathlamet, Brandi Beedle, the county's emergency resource coordinator and a native of nearby Puget Island, finds hope in small signs of renewal. Former classmates are returning home for county jobs after college. The civic spirit that built the library, the pool, the skatepark decades ago is stirring again. She has two sons, ages 2 and 6.

"My son, he's only 6," she told the Columbian. "So who knows what he'll want to do in 20 years."

The question — hanging over Cathlamet, Port Townsend, the Methow Valley, the Oregon Coast, and every small town remaking itself in the image of its own landscape — is whether a place can offer its children a future without asking them to leave.
