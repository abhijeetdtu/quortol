# The $53 Million Summer: New England's Agricultural Reckoning

---

**Roque Bluffs, Maine** — On a July morning in 2025, Lisa Hanscom walked out onto her family's blueberry barren and saw the berries falling. Not being picked, not being raked — falling. The drought had descended with such speed that the fruit shriveled on the bush before she could get her crew into the field. "I ended up raking only four acres," she said later. "That was all I could rake before they were no longer there." Twenty-eight acres of wild blueberries — the fruit that has grown on this coast since the last glacier retreated — never got harvested at all. [Maine Public, November 7, 2025](https://www.mainepublic.org/climate/2025-11-07/maine-apple-blueberry-harvest-devastated-by-drought)

Hanscom, who co-owns Welch Farm and chairs the Wild Blueberry Commission of Maine, was not alone. Across the state, growers brought in 54.9 million pounds of wild blueberries in 2025 — roughly 30 million pounds less than the previous year. The Wild Blueberry Commission estimates the industry lost $30 million. [Bangor Daily News, November 7, 2025](https://www.bangordailynews.com/2025/11/07/hancock/maine-blueberry-industry-losses-joam40zk0w/)

Add in $10 million in lost apples from Maine orchards, and at least $15.9 million in agricultural losses across Vermont, and you arrive at a rough number for a single growing season: roughly $56 million in agricultural damage across northern New England, concentrated in the states that depend most on the land for their identity and their livelihoods. [Vermont Agency of Agriculture, 2025 Drought Survey](https://agriculture.vermont.gov/agency-agriculture-food-markets-releases-2025-agricultural-drought-survey-report-dashboard); [Drought.gov, November 20, 2025](https://www.drought.gov/drought-status-updates/drought-status-update-northeast-2025-11-20)

![Agricultural losses from extreme weather in northern New England by event, 2023–2025, in US$ millions. Dark orange bars show drought losses; blue bars show flood losses for context.](/api/blog/images/the-53-million-dollar-summer_agricultural_losses.png)
The money alone does not capture what happened. The money never does.

## The Berry that Defines a State

Maine produces 99 percent of the nation's wild blueberries — not the plump cultivated kind that roll around in plastic clamshells at the supermarket, but the smaller, more intensely flavored fruit that grows in the rocky, acidic soil of Washington County and points east. The industry is not large by national agricultural standards, but it is foundational to coastal Maine: a web of multi-generational family farms, processors, and seasonal workers that has operated, in one form or another, since the Passamaquoddy and Wabanaki first harvested these barrens centuries ago.

The 2025 growing season began with a problem that would have been hard to imagine five years earlier: too much water. Wet conditions during the June pollination period interfered with bees, reducing the initial fruit set. Then the rain stopped. A flash drought — the kind that arrives so fast it barely leaves time to adapt — set in just as the berries began to ripen. [Inside Climate News, May 11, 2026](https://insideclimatenews.org/news/11052026/maine-wild-blueberry-farms-climate-change/)

Eric Venturini, executive director of the Wild Blueberry Commission, described it as a double disaster: yields down nearly 35 percent from 2024, at a moment when farm profit margins had already been negative for three consecutive years. The cost of production doubled. Active blueberry acreage under management has declined by more than 20 percent in the last five years. [Maine Legislature Testimony, January 2026](https://legislature.maine.gov/legis/bills/getTestimonyDoc.asp?id=196252)

In response, Senator Susan Collins asked the USDA to include wild blueberry growers, apple orchards, and hay farmers in any emergency funding the department releases. The Wild Blueberry Commission began exploring whether it could provide direct relief to growers — an extraordinary step for a commodity board. [Bangor Daily News, November 7, 2025](https://www.bangordailynews.com/2025/11/07/hancock/maine-blueberry-industry-losses-joam40zk0w/)

## Three Catastrophes in Three Years

In Vermont, the story was both different and disturbingly similar. The 2025 drought did not arrive in isolation. It followed catastrophic flooding in July 2023 — roughly $16 million in agricultural losses — and another round of flooding in 2024 that cost farms more than $15 million. [VTDigger, January 14, 2026](https://vtdigger.org/2026/01/14/drought-cost-vermont-farmers-15-9m/)

Abbey Willard, who manages the agriculture development division at the Vermont Agency of Agriculture, Food & Markets, describes the pattern bluntly: "These impacts build." The 2025 drought is now the third consecutive growing season in which Vermont farmers have faced extreme weather that threatened their viability. [VTDigger, January 14, 2026](https://vtdigger.org/2026/01/14/drought-cost-vermont-farmers-15-9m/)

The state's drought impact survey, released in March 2026, is a document no one would wish to read and no Vermonter can afford to ignore. Of 198 agricultural businesses that responded — representing all 14 Vermont counties — 85 percent reported lower crop yields or total crop failure. Sixty-three percent faced feed shortages heading into winter. Thirty percent purchased or hauled water for the first time in their lives. Nearly 60 percent said this was the worst drought they had ever seen. [Vermont Agency of Agriculture, 2025 Drought Survey Report](https://agriculture.vermont.gov/agency-agriculture-food-markets-releases-2025-agricultural-drought-survey-report-dashboard)

The total: more than $15.4 million in estimated losses across 78,000 acres in 109 towns. [Vermont Business Magazine, March 9, 2026](https://vermontbiz.com/news/2026/march/09/2025-agricultural-drought-survey-report-and-dashboard-published)
![Share of Vermont agricultural businesses reporting each drought impact: 85% lower crop yields, 63% feed shortages, 59% worst drought ever, 51% insufficient pasture, 30% purchased water for first time.](/api/blog/images/the-53-million-dollar-summer_vt_impacts.png)

Some losses resist quantification. The Vermont maple syrup industry — which depends on healthy sugar maple stands that were stressed by drought — faced an uncertain spring harvest. Apple orchards, already battered by two flood years, saw fruit stressed by water deficit. Dairy farmers, the backbone of Vermont's agricultural economy, scrambled to find hay after pastures failed. The accountant's ledger and the farmer's memory tell different stories, and both are true.

## When the Wells Run Dry

The agricultural crisis sat atop a deeper, less visible catastrophe. By September 2025, groundwater levels across northern New England had fallen to record or near-record lows. The U.S. Geological Survey reported that dozens of monitoring wells in New England measured water levels below the 5th percentile — meaning the water table was lower than 95 percent of all historical readings for that time of year. [USGS New England Water Science Center](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/new-england-water-science-center/news/provisional-data-detects-record-low-streamflows)

In New Hampshire alone, 92 public water systems imposed outdoor watering restrictions, affecting approximately 200,000 people. Lake Winnipesaukee sat nearly 8 inches below its normal September level. Lake Sunapee was 17.5 inches below full pond. [Drought.gov, September 30, 2025](https://www.drought.gov/drought-status-updates/drought-status-update-northeast-2025-09-30)

Private well owners fared worse. Hundreds of dry wells were reported across Maine and New Hampshire after the 2025 growing season. Well drillers became so backlogged that many stopped taking new customers until spring. Some households entered winter without a reliable water supply. State agencies in Vermont and New Hampshire issued warnings against drinking untreated water from roadside springs — an act of desperation that public health officials felt compelled to name as a risk. [Drought.gov, November 20, 2025](https://www.drought.gov/drought-status-updates/drought-status-update-northeast-2025-11-20)

## Climate Whiplash and the Flash Drought Era

What happened in 2025 was not an anomaly. It was, by multiple accounts, a classic example of what some researchers call "climate whiplash" — the rapid oscillation between extreme wet and extreme dry that characterizes a warming atmosphere's effect on the hydrologic cycle.

The Northeast United States has the most rapidly increasing frequency of extreme precipitation in the country. [Jong et al., 2024, Earth's Future](https://doi.org/10.1029/2023ef004370) GFDL climate models project that very extreme precipitation events — those exceeding 150 millimeters in a single day — could be six times more likely by the end of the century. [GFDL/NOAA, SPEAR Model Research](https://www.gfdl.noaa.gov/research_highlight/increases-in-extreme-precipitation-over-the-northeast-united-states-using-high-resolution-climate-model-simulations/)

And yet the same region that is getting more extreme rain is also suffering more frequent, more severe drought. The Northeast experienced historic dry periods in 2000, 2016, 2020, and 2022 — a frequency that climatologists say has no precedent since at least the 1960s. [Drought.gov, Northeast DEWS](https://www.drought.gov/dews/northeast) The U.S. Geological Survey documented the 2022 drought as a short but extreme event that turned parts of southern New England into a federal disaster area by August. [USGS Scientific Investigations Report 2023-5016](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/sir20235016/full)

The mechanism is simple enough: a warmer atmosphere holds more water vapor, which means it can produce both heavier downpours and more efficient evaporation. The same heat that fuels extreme rain events also desiccates the soil. When the rain stops — and in New England, it can stop abruptly — the ground dries out faster than it used to. The window between "plenty of water" and "not nearly enough" shrinks.

David Calderwood, a Maine blueberry industry analyst, called the 2025 season "a classic example of climate whiplash." [Inside Climate News, May 11, 2026](https://insideclimatenews.org/news/11052026/maine-wild-blueberry-farms-climate-change/) The phrase captures something important: the problem is not simply that New England is getting drier. It is that the region is becoming less predictable, and the old rhythms — wet spring, settled summer, reliable fall — no longer hold.

## The Groundwater Trap

The most troubling signal for the 2026 growing season came not from the crops but from the water table. By April 2026, the National Weather Service office in Gray, Maine, was reporting something unusual. The spring recharge — the annual refilling of groundwater from snowmelt and spring rain — had arrived two to four weeks early, then stalled. [NWS Gray, Drought Information Statement, April 28, 2026](https://www.weather.gov/media/gyx/DGT/DGT_GYX_04282026.pdf)

The early melt meant that water ran off before the ground had fully thawed, failing to penetrate the deep aquifers that sustain wells through the summer. As soils warmed, moisture was drawn downward from the topsoil to fill deeper voids — essentially robbing the surface layer of its recent gains. And once vegetation emerged for the growing season, plants would begin actively pumping water from the soil, a demand that risked rapidly reducing whatever topsoil moisture remained.

The National Weather Service put it plainly in its April assessment: "If the deep groundwater isn't replenished before the plants start drawing from it, the drought conditions could worsen quickly once the summer heat arrives." [NWS Gray, April 28, 2026](https://www.weather.gov/media/gyx/DGT/DGT_GYX_04282026.pdf)

By May 2026, some improvement had been registered. The May 26 U.S. Drought Monitor showed soaking rains across parts of the Northeast, with drought categories reduced in Pennsylvania, Maryland, and West Virginia. But northern New England saw less of that precipitation. Small areas of degradation appeared in Rhode Island and eastern Massachusetts. [U.S. Drought Monitor, May 28, 2026](https://droughtmonitor.unl.edu/) The seasonal outlook from the Climate Prediction Center, issued May 2026, offered a cautious note: drought improvement was forecast for the Northeast, but only if summer precipitation arrived on schedule. [Climate Prediction Center, Seasonal Drought Outlook, May 2026](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/expert_assessment/sdo_summary.php)

## The Recovery Question

Emergency loans from the USDA have been made available to farmers in designated counties across Maine, New Hampshire, and Vermont. The Livestock Forage Disaster Program can compensate some producers for grazing losses. The Maine Farmers Drought Relief Fund exists, though its capacity relative to the scale of need is an open question. [USDA Farm Service Agency, November 21, 2025](https://www.fsa.usda.gov/news-events/news/11-21-2025/usda-designates-maine-counties-primary-natural-disaster-areas)

But disaster assistance, as Vermont farmers have now learned three years running, does not prevent the next disaster. It only helps clean up after the last one. The government arrives with a mop after the flood, when what the farmer needed was a roof.

The deeper question is what happens to an agricultural economy built on a climate that no longer exists. Maine's wild blueberry industry has lost a fifth of its active acreage in five years. Vermont dairy farmers face a third consecutive season of weather-related stress. Maple syrup producers wonder if the sugar maples can survive repeated drought years on top of warming winters. Ski resorts worry about having enough water to make snow. [Drought.gov, November 20, 2025](https://www.drought.gov/drought-status-updates/drought-status-update-northeast-2025-11-20)

These are not separate problems. They are symptoms of a single, underlying condition: a region that has historically counted on abundant, reliable water is discovering that abundance and reliability are not the same thing, and that neither can be taken for granted anymore.
