# The Forty-Nine Stones

**What the oldest games in the world tell us about the people who played them — and what they cannot.**

![The Royal Game of Ur](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1d/British_Museum_Royal_Game_of_Ur.jpg/800px-British_Museum_Royal_Game_of_Ur.jpg)
*The Royal Game of Ur, on display at the British Museum. The board is made of wood inlaid with shell and lapis lazuli. The drawer at its thick end once held dice and playing pieces. Photograph by BabelStone, CC0 1.0 Public Domain.*

---

They are forty-nine stones, no bigger than the palm of your hand, painted in colours that have no business surviving five thousand years. Green, red, blue, black, white still cling to the carved surfaces — the flank of a miniature pig, the snout of a dog, the sloping face of a pyramid, the smooth curve of a bullet. A few are shaped like nothing at all: round, oblong, abstract. They were discovered in 2012, gathered in a single cluster inside a grave at **Başur Höyük**, a low mound in southeastern Turkey that overlooks the Tigris River valley (Haluk Sağlamtimur, cited in *Discovery News*, 15 August 2013; [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/these-complex-beautiful-board-game-pieces-are-5000-years-old-127645/)).

They are almost certainly the oldest figurative game pieces ever discovered. They are also, in a profound sense, useless. The board that should have accompanied them — probably made of wood, probably laid in that same grave — has completely decayed. "Unfortunately, we could not find the board of this game set," Haluk Sağlamtimur, the archaeologist who led the excavation, said later. "It was probably inside the grave, but it decayed" ([*Hürriyet Daily News*](https://www.hurriyetdailynews.com/ancient-playing-pieces-unearthed-in-siirt-to-be-redesigned-as-3d-game-73566), 28 October 2014).

No one knows how to play the game the forty-nine stones were made for.

---

### 1. The lost game

To hold a game piece without knowing the game is to hold half an object. You can admire the craftsmanship — each stone was shaped with care, painted with intention — but you cannot use it. You cannot sit across from another person and move the pig, or the dog, or the bullet across the board, because the board is gone and the rules went with it.

The Başur Höyük pieces date to between 3100 and 2900 BC, placing them at the very dawn of urban civilization in Mesopotamia and the Fertile Crescent. The site itself had been inhabited since roughly 7000 BC — meaning people lived there for four thousand years before the game pieces were buried. The grave belonged to someone of high status, possibly a ruler, judging by the metal artifacts and hundreds of pottery vessels found alongside the stones (radio carbon dating, cited in [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/sciencemain/oldest-known-gaming-tokens-dung-bronze-age-turkish-graves-6c10920354), 14 August 2013; [*New Scientist*](https://www.newscientist.com/article/dn24060-ancient-pawns-pieces-from-5000-year-old-board-games/), 16 August 2013).

The pieces are organized around the number four: four dog heads, four pigs, four pyramid shapes, four bullet shapes, four circular tokens of white shell topped with black stone. "It's a unique finding, a rather complete set of a chess-like game," Sağlamtimur told reporters. "We are puzzling over its strategy" ([*Archaeology Magazine*](https://archaeology.org/news/2013/08/15/130815-turkey-bronze-age-gaming-tokens-found/), 15 August 2013).

Scholars, naturally, pushed back. Ulrich Schädler, director of the Swiss Museum of Games, observed that early games were generally simple race affairs, not multi-piece strategic contests. The pyramids, he noted, "resemble the dice of the Royal Game of Ur" ([*New Scientist*](https://www.newscientist.com/article/dn24060-ancient-pawns-pieces-from-5000-year-old-board-games/), 16 August 2013). Perhaps the forty-nine stones represent several different games, bundled together.

We do not know. That is the condition of the earliest board games: we have the equipment, but the instructions are missing. The people who made these objects are long gone. The language they spoke is lost. The rules that seemed so obvious to them that no one thought to carve them into stone — those rules have evaporated, like the wooden board in the grave.

It is a strange kind of survival. The stones endured. The play did not.

---

### 2. The found game

One of the oldest game boards in the world sits behind glass in the British Museum. It measures 301 by 110 millimetres — roughly the size of a hardcover novel held sideways. It is made of wood that survived only because Mesopotamia was dry, inlaid with twenty squares of shell and lapis lazuli. Five of the squares bear rosettes. At the thick end of the board there is a small drawer. "When they found the original it was made of wood and all the wood had rotted, but they could see that there had been a drawer in this thick end where all the dice and pieces were," said **Irving Finkel**, a curator at the British Museum ([Irving Finkel, Tom Scott vs Irving Finkel: The Royal Game of Ur, British Museum](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WZskjLq040I), 2017).

The board came from the Royal Cemetery of **Ur**, in modern-day Iraq, where the British archaeologist **Sir Leonard Woolley** excavated it between 1922 and 1934 ([British Museum, collection number ME 120834](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1928-1009-378); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Game_of_Ur)). Ur was one of the great cities of ancient Sumer, the birthplace of writing, of law, of bureaucracy. It was also, it turns out, a place where people played games.

Woolley found five game boards in the Royal Cemetery. Each came with tetrahedral dice — four-sided pyramids of stone or lapis lazuli, with two corners marked and two unmarked — and with pieces: seven white and seven black, marked with five dots each. What they did not come with was instructions. For decades, no one knew how the Royal Game of Ur was played.

Then, in the early 1980s, Irving Finkel — who had been obsessed with board games since childhood, who had once built a wooden replica of the Ur board as a boy and forced his sister Angela to play it endlessly — found a clay tablet in the British Museum's collection of 130,000 cuneiform tablets. It changed everything.

Written by a Babylonian scribe named **Itti-Marduk-balāṭu** on the third of November, 177–176 BC, the tablet described the rules of a board game. It was a late text — Babylonian civilization was approaching its end — but it had been copied from an earlier source. Finkel recognized that the game on the tablet matched the board from Ur, at least in its later form. He had found the world's oldest surviving rulebook (Finkel, "On the Rules for the Royal Game of Ur," 2007; [Great Big Story](https://greatbigstory.com/oldest-board-game-in-the-world/), 2023).

"I found this after about two years," Finkel recalled, "and I worked on it because I've always liked reading things which are difficult, and this tablet was very difficult, and I worked on it and worked on it and worked on it" ([The British Museum, "Deciphering the world's oldest rule book," 2015](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wHjznvH54Cw)).

The tablet described a race game for two players. They moved their pieces along a track of twenty squares, using the tetrahedral dice to determine their moves. Landing on a rosette was good. Landing on an opponent's piece in the middle of the board sent it back to the start. The first player to get all seven pieces off the board won.

Finkel's reconstruction made the Royal Game of Ur playable again after an interruption of roughly two thousand years. In 2017, the British Museum filmed a match between Finkel and the YouTuber **Tom Scott**. Finkel won. (He had been practicing since he was nine.)

The tablet also revealed something stranger: the game was used for fortune-telling. The cuneiform text assigned predictions to certain squares. Landing on one might mean "fine beer" or "success with women"; another might mean "starvation" or "enough meat." The game was simultaneously a pastime, a gambling device, and an astrological instrument. People in 177 BC were betting on the rosettes (Finkel, "On the Rules for the Royal Game of Ur," 2007; [*Time*](https://content.time.com/time/specials/2007/printout/0,29239,1815747_1815707_1815665,00.html), 2008).

It took a dead civilization's written language, deciphered by a bearded Englishman with a childhood obsession, to bring the game back. But it came back. You can buy a reproduction of the Royal Game of Ur at the British Museum gift shop today, for about £40. It comes with a board, pieces, dice, and a rulebook. The instruction that cost Itti-Marduk-balāṭu a day of careful writing, that lay buried in a clay tablet for two thousand years, that sat misidentified in a museum drawer for another century, has been printed on a sheet of paper and boxed in cardboard.

### 3. The persistent game

But there is another lineage. Not every ancient board game has to be excavated and revived. Some never went away.

**Go** – or *weiqi*, as it is called in Chinese – has been played for at least 2,500 years. The earliest written reference appears in the *Zuo Zhuan*, an annal from about 548 BC, documenting a game played by a Chinese nobleman named **Mu Shu** and a minister named **Bo Wei** (as recorded in the *Zuo Zhuan*, cited in [Wikipedia: History of Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Go); [*GoBase*](https://gobase.org/reading/history/china/)). Confucius mentioned it, warily, in the *Analects*: it was, he thought, a step up from gluttony and idleness, but barely.

Legend goes further. The fourth-century AD scholar **Zhang Hua** wrote, in his *Bo Wu Zhi* (Record of the Investigation of Things): "Yao invented go in order to instruct his son Dan Zhu." Then he added, with dry mockery: "Others say [Emperor] Shun regarded his son Shang Jun as stupid and invented go to instruct him" ([Zhang Hua, c. 270–290 AD, cited in *GoBase*](https://gobase.org/reading/history/china/?sec=part-2)).

The story is almost certainly mythical. Emperor Yao is a semi-mythical figure from the 23rd century BC. But the myth is worth something: it tells us that the Chinese of Zhang Hua's time already thought of Go as unimaginably old, as something that had always been there.

The game is played on a grid. Originally 17×17 lines (289 intersections); later standardized to 19×19 (361). Two players take turns placing stones — one black, one white — on the intersections. The goal is to fence off more territory than the opponent. No dice, no cards, no random elements. The game is pure strategy, and its complexity is astronomical: there are more possible Go positions than atoms in the observable universe.

Today, according to a 2016 survey by the International Go Federation, over 46 million people know how to play Go, and over 20 million play it regularly, most of them in East Asia ([International Go Federation, 2016, cited in Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Go_(game))). It has been played continuously for longer than any board game in existence, which means that if you learn Go today, you are joining a chain of players that stretches back past the Han Dynasty, past Confucius, past the Warring States, to a time when the game was already old.

Go is the counterpoint to the Başur Höyük stones. Both games are ancient. Both originated in civilizations that rose and fell. But the rules of Go were never lost. They were passed from hand to hand, generation to generation, across thousands of years, without interruption. The forty-nine stones have no one to move them. Go has twenty million.

|------|--------|----------|--------|
| Mehen (Egypt) | c. 3600–3200 BC | Spiral snake boards, lion pieces & marbles | Rules lost |
| Başur Höyük pieces (Turkey) | c. 3100–2900 BC | 49 painted stone pieces | Rules lost; board decayed |

| Go / Weiqi (China) | Earliest ref. 548 BC | Textual references, archaeological boards | Continuously played |
| Chess (India → Persia → Europe) | c. 600 AD | Variants in Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic | Continuously played |

---

The arc of ancient board games is not a straight line from primitive to sophisticated. The earliest games — Mehen, Senet, the Royal Game of Ur — had rules as elaborate as anything played today, at least in their developed forms. The forty-nine Başur stones were found with dice, suggesting a game of comparable complexity. The impulse to play did not evolve gradually; it was there from the beginning, fully formed.

What was lost was not the capacity to design games, but the memory of them. Without writing, the rules died with the players. With writing, some survived — but only if the text survived, and only if someone could read it.

Itti-Marduk-balāṭu wrote his tablet in the second century BC, copying from an older source. It was unearthed around 1880 in the ruins of Babylon, sold to the British Museum, and sat unread for nearly a hundred years before Finkel recognized it for what it was. The tablet nearly vanished again when a second copy — held in the private collection of Count Aymar de Liedekerke-Beaufort — was handed to a photographer just before the First World War. The photographer's studio was destroyed, with the tablet inside. Only the photograph survived (Finkel, "On the Rules for the Royal Game of Ur," 2007; [*Academia.edu*](https://www.academia.edu/15173145/On_the_Rules_for_the_Royal_Game_of_Ur)).

The survival of any ancient game is a series of near-misses.

---

### 4. What the board cannot tell us

The forty-nine stones from Başur Höyük now reside in the **Batman Museum**, in the city of Batman in southeastern Turkey — a name that reliably generates headlines, though the museum itself is a serious institution with an important collection. The stones sit in a glass case, labeled as the world's oldest figurative game pieces. Visitors pause, read the label, and move on.

There is no way to reconstruct the game they were made for. The board is gone — carbonized, probably, like the poorly preserved wooden fragments found alongside the stones. The rules were never written down. The language of the people who played it is unknown. The culture that produced it — a Bronze Age society living along the Tigris, trading with Mesopotamia and Egypt — has left no textual record.

"We are puzzling over its strategy," Sağlamtimur said, years after the discovery. His team never solved it.

And yet the stones survive. They are evidence of something otherwise invisible: that in the fourth millennium BC, while the first cities were rising and the first writing was being scratched into clay, people were already playing games. They were not merely surviving — they were passing the time, competing, laughing, losing.

The impulse to play did not wait for civilization. It was part of what made civilization possible. Before accountants began marking grain on clay tablets, people were sitting across from one another, moving pieces across a board, trying to win.

The game itself is gone. But the forty-nine stones remain, in a museum in a city called Batman, still painted green and red and blue and black and white, five thousand years after someone shaped them, painted them, used them for something that mattered, and buried them with a person who mattered more.
