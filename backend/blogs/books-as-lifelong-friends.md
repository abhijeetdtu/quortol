# The Friend Who Never Leaves: What Science Knows About the Companionship of Books

*She was three years into a divorce, raising two sons alone, when the pandemic emptied the world further. Emily Anderson, an English professor at USC Dornsife, retreated to her makeshift garage office — no libraries, no research materials, just the books already on her shelves. She started typing out essays, reflections on what she called "a life lived in books." What emerged was not a work of criticism but a kind of testimony: that reading, even in its most solitary posture, is an act of profound connection. "You're with these characters, you're with the author, you're in another world," she later said. We have been taught to think of reading as withdrawal. She discovered it was a form of arrival.*

*[USC Dornsife — "What's in a book? Often, a good friend" — April 29, 2025](https://dornsife.usc.edu/news/stories/reading-can-help-overcome-loneliness-and-make-connections/)*

**I. "Books Are Friends We Can Choose Without Restriction"**

The idea that a book can be a friend is ancient. But it found one of its most precise formulations in the nineteenth century, in the work of John Ruskin, the English art critic who made J. M. W. Turner famous, championed education for working people, and denounced greed as the main motivation of British life. "However intimate our friendships," Ruskin wrote, "however much we value them, circumstance will have restricted our choice of people we can know. Books are friends we can choose without restriction."

The thought passed from Ruskin to Marcel Proust, who began reading him in 1897. Proust's English was poor — he said he did not claim to know English but that, instead, he claimed to know Ruskin — and over the next decade he translated several of Ruskin's books into French. The encounter became a preparation for Proust's great novel, *À la recherche du temps perdu*, and for an insight about reading that Proust would refine into something stranger and more intimate than Ruskin had imagined. In the final volume, *Le temps retrouvé*, Proust wrote of his own readers: "It would even be inexact to say that I thought of those who read it as readers of my book. Because they were not, as I saw it, my readers. More exactly they were readers of themselves, my book being a sort of magnifying glass … by which I could give them the means to read within themselves."

The modern psychological restatement of this tradition comes from Keith Oatley, professor emeritus of cognitive psychology at the University of Toronto and a leading researcher on the psychology of fiction. In a 2012 essay for *Psychology Today*, Oatley traced the Ruskin-Proust line and argued that books offer a unique kind of relationship — not a substitute for friendship, but a distinct form of it. We expect our friends to arrive, to answer, to reciprocate. A book asks for nothing but attention, and gives back a mind. It does not compete or betray. It offers intimacy without demand, which is perhaps the only kind that lasts.

**II. The Attachment to Books**

But what is the *nature* of this bond? Do we merely say that books are like friends, or does the brain actually process them that way?

The most systematic attempt to answer this question comes from María Angélica Thumala Olave, a cultural sociologist at the University of Edinburgh, whose 2020 paper "Book love" in the journal *Poetics* analyzed 43 in-depth interviews and 60 written responses from the UK's Mass Observation project. Her conclusion: people form genuine attachments to books — attachments that are material, emotional, and even sacred. The word is not too strong. We treat books the way other cultures treat relics.

Thumala Olave found that readers "desire to be surrounded by print copies, experience pain when discarding books, and enjoy the active role reading affords." Books, she argues, function as "icons" — not in the religious sense, but in the cultural-sociological one. They do not simply communicate ideas or signal social status. They embody and enable the realization of what she calls "sacred cultural goods" — values, memories, ways of being that matter deeply to their owners. A book on the shelf is not decoration. It is a small altar to a former self.

This is not mere sentimentality. The study found that the material properties of books — their weight, their smell, the marginalia left by a previous owner, the inscription on the flyleaf — were central to the attachment. "Aesthetic immersion in books' materiality allows for these values to be realized," Thumala Olave wrote. The book is not a delivery mechanism for text. It is a physical object that carries the history of a relationship. We keep certain books not because we plan to read them again, but because throwing them away would feel like a small betrayal.

*[Thumala Olave, "Book love: A cultural sociological interpretation of the attachment to books," *Poetics* 81 (2020), 101440](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.poetic.2020.101440)*

**III. Bibliotherapy: Reading as Healing**

If books can function as friends, can they also function as therapists? The evidence suggests yes — and the effect sizes are embarrassingly large for a profession that charges by the hour.

A 2024 meta-analysis in *Innovations in Clinical Neuroscience* examined 29 studies of cognitive bibliotherapy for depression and found an effect size of Cohen's *d* = 0.77. Another meta-analysis of 13 bibliotherapy studies found *d* = 0.84 — with a *greater* effect in more severe illness. For context, a meta-analysis of general psychotherapy for depression published in *JAMA Psychiatry* found *d* = 0.75. Reading books, in other words, appears to be roughly as effective as talking to a therapist for mild to moderate depression. The paperback may be the most cost-effective medical device ever invented.

*[Correll, "Literary Prescriptions: Applying Bibliotherapy in a Psychotherapeutic Context," *Innovations in Clinical Neuroscience* 21(7-9), 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11424070/)*
*[Cuijpers et al., "Psychotherapy for depression across different age groups," *JAMA Psychiatry* 77(7), 2020](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2020.0164)*

The mechanism matters. Bibliotherapy is not simply reading. It is the structured use of reading materials — personal narratives, stories, guided self-help — to promote a "cognitive shift." The reader identifies with characters, reflects on their situations, and develops new tools for making decisions about their own lives. A 2021 systematic review in *Frontiers in Public Health* examined 13 high-quality studies and found that values such as autonomy and justice were "closely linked with positive results in bibliotherapy." Reading, the authors concluded, "leads those involved to reflect on themselves and their environment… It is an intrinsic character of literature to serve as therapy, catharsis, and cure for any conflict that disrupts our existence."

*[Monroy-Fraustro et al., "Bibliotherapy as a Non-pharmaceutical Intervention to Enhance Mental Health," *Frontiers in Public Health* 9 (2021), 629872](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8007779/)*

Research on bibliotherapy has grown exponentially. A scientometric analysis of the Scopus database identified 1,703 papers on bibliotherapy published between 1942 and 2023, with the majority appearing in the last decade. The field has moved from margin to mainstream — from a curiosity to a discipline, from a footnote to a library of its own.

*[Joseph & Jose, "A scientometric analysis of bibliotherapy: mapping the research landscape," *Library Hi Tech* (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1108/LHT-01-2024-0028)*

**IV. The Empathy Gap**

But bibliotherapy addresses the reader's own distress. What about the quieter, more transformative claim: that reading makes us better at understanding *other* people? That the novel is not merely comfort, but a moral education?

The research here is robust and growing. A 2013 experimental study by Bal and Veltkamp, published in the journal *PLOS ONE* and cited more than 1,180 times since, found that fiction reading influences empathy — but only under one condition: "emotional transportation" into the story. Readers who became absorbed in a narrative showed increased empathy. Those who remained detached did not. The story had to work its particular magic: the reader had to enter the world of the characters. You cannot read a novel with one foot out the door and expect to be changed by it.

*[Bal & Veltkamp, "How Does Fiction Reading Influence Empathy?" *PLOS ONE* (2013)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3559433/)*

More recent work has deepened the picture. A 2025 study in the journal *Literacy* examined how fiction reading supports empathy in adolescents, finding that readers experienced both "cognitive empathy" — understanding what a character thinks and feels — and "affective empathy" — sharing those feelings. Importantly, the empathy did not stay inside the book. Adolescents reported that feelings of empathy for fictional characters "transferred beyond fiction to real-life others."

*[Santi, "Reading and Empathy: Qualitative insights into adolescents' experiences with fiction books," *Literacy* (2025)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lit.70006)*

A 2022 integrative review of 21 studies found that "improved empathy levels obtained through reading fiction texts lead students to care more about their peers, as well as other people around them." Both the affective and cognitive components of empathy were strengthened, and the effects were observed across age groups.

*[Roza & Guimarães, "The relationship between reading and empathy: An integrative literature review," *Psicologia: Teoria e Pesquisa* 24(2), 2022](https://doi.org/10.5935/1980-6906/ePTPPE14051.en)*

What this suggests is that a book does not merely offer companionship — it trains the capacity for it. Reading fiction is a kind of social simulation, a safe rehearsal for the complex work of understanding other minds. We learn to feel for imaginary people so that we might, in time, feel more generously for real ones.

**V. The Loyalty of the Page**

"There is no friend as loyal as a book," Ernest Hemingway is reported to have said. The line is widely attributed to his posthumous memoir *A Moveable Feast* (1964), though scholars have not conclusively identified the exact passage. The attribution is itself instructive: the quote persists because it feels true, because it captures something about books that readers recognize from their own experience. Hemingway probably said it. If he did not, he should have.

A book does not gossip. It does not move away. It does not become someone you used to know. It sits on the shelf, waits, and is ready to resume the conversation exactly where it left off — even if decades have passed. This constancy is not trivial. In a world where relationships are increasingly conditional, curated, and contingent, the book offers a form of relationship that asks nothing except attention. It is the only friend that will never unfriend you.

The novelist and critic Tim Parks, writing in *The New Yorker*, once observed that "we read to feel less alone, but also to feel more alone — to discover that solitude is shareable." This paradox lies at the heart of the friendship with books. Reading is solitary. But what enters the solitude is the presence of another mind — the author's, yes, but also the accumulated presence of everyone who has read the same pages, underlined the same sentences, been moved by the same passages. We are never quite alone with a book; we are alone with everyone.

Emily Anderson, the USC professor who wrote *Shadow Work: Loneliness and the Literary Life*, put it differently. Her students, she notes, keep discovering that the mere act of carrying a book into public creates connection. "Someone recognizes the cover and says, 'I remember that book!'" The material object itself becomes a conversation starter, a signal of shared experience, a visible emblem of an invisible community. A book in the hand is a flag flown for a country whose borders are invisible.

*[Anderson, *Shadow Work: Loneliness and the Literary Life*, Columbia University Press, 2025](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/shadow-work/9780231218504/)*

**VI. The Friendship That Grows**

There is another dimension to the book-friend that distinguishes it from most human relationships: the friendship deepens over time. A novel read at sixteen is not the same novel at forty. The words are the same, but the reader has changed, and so the book reveals different things. Proust called this the "magnifying glass" of reading — a device for reading within oneself. At sixteen, you see the surface. At forty, you see the structure underneath. The book has not aged. You have.

C.S. Lewis, who is often misquoted as having said "We read to know we are not alone" (the actual line comes from screenwriter William Nicholson in the 1993 film *Shadowlands*), wrote something perhaps more true: "Those of us who have been true readers all our life seldom fully realise the enormous extension of our being which we owe to authors." The extension works in both directions. We bring more of ourselves to a book each time we return to it, and the book, unchanging, reveals how much we have changed. It is the one friendship where you are the variable.

*[Essential C.S. Lewis — "We Read To Know" — quotation fact-check](https://essentialcslewis.com/2015/09/05/we-read-to-know/)*
*[C.S. Lewis Foundation — Quotes Misattributed to C.S. Lewis](https://www.cslewis.org/aboutus/faq/quotes-misattributed/)*

This is the kind of friendship that does not stagnate. The book becomes a record of who you were at each encounter — a diary written in the margins, invisible to anyone but yourself. No human friend can do that. No human friend should be asked to.

**The Return**

On the savannahs of northern Tanzania, the Hadza people still begin their days as humans have for most of our species' existence. By early afternoon, the day's essential work is done. What remains is conversation, storytelling, rest, and the slow repair of social bonds around a fire.

The storytelling has migrated to other media — to books, to screens, to the silent communion of a reader alone in a room. What has not changed is the need it serves. We read to extend ourselves, to train the muscle of empathy, to find companionship in minds we have never met. We read because, as the research now confirms, it works. The effect sizes are comparable to therapy. The empathy transfers beyond the page. The attachment is real enough to measure. We have spent centuries defending reading as a pleasure; science has now confirmed it is also a medicine.

Proust said that his book was a magnifying glass by which readers could read within themselves. What he did not say — what no scientist has yet quite captured — is that the magnifying glass also works in the other direction. The book reads us, too. It reflects back what we bring to it, holds steady while we change, and stays on the shelf, waiting, whenever we are ready to resume the conversation. It is the only relationship in which being ignored does not damage the bond.

"Books are remote but reliable friends," Victor Hugo wrote. Remote, because they speak without voice. Reliable, because they never stop speaking. In an age of signal and noise, of notifications and algorithmic suggestion, the silence of the book — its patient, waiting presence — may be the most loyal thing we have left. The algorithm wants to know what we like. The book already knows who we are.
