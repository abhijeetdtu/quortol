# The House That Breakfast Built: New Hampshire's Bed and Breakfasts Through Two Centuries

**On the porch of the Philbrook Farm Inn, at the hour when the White Mountains turn from granite to gold, the rocking chairs face the Presidential Range as they have since 1861. The screen door creaks; someone inside is beating eggs. Nothing here has changed, which is, of course, the whole point — and also, quietly, the miracle.**

---

In a state where tourism generates $7.5 billion in annual visitor spending and supports more than 70,000 jobs, bed and breakfasts occupy a peculiar and outsized niche [NH Division of Travel & Tourism 2026 Economic Impact Report](https://www.visitnh.gov/getmedia/a10ac1f0-45aa-41df-9cd6-63577fe08043/2026EconomicImpact.pdf). They are not the largest accommodation segment — that honor belongs to the chain hotels and resorts that ring the interstate exits — but they are the most enduring, the most personal, and, in many ways, the most New Hampshire. Of the 208 B&Bs operating in the state as of 2026, 85.58 percent are single-owner properties, which is to say that roughly 178 of them are someone's home, someone's life savings, someone's magnificent second act [SmartScraper B&B Report for New Hampshire](https://rentechdigital.com/smartscraper/business-report-details/united-states/list-of-bed-and-breakfasts-in-new-hampshire). The modern B&B industry nationwide generates $2.0 billion in annual revenue, with an average of six rooms per property, and 95 percent are run by owners for whom hospitality is a second career [Kentley Insights B&B Market Report](https://www.kentleyinsights.com/bed-and-breakfast-inns-industry-market-research-report/)[VerticalIQ B&B Industry Report](https://verticaliq.com/product/bed-and-breakfast-inns/). New Hampshire's version of this story reaches back farther than most: some of the buildings housing B&B guests tonight were already old when Abraham Lincoln was a young lawyer, and one suspects they will be old long after the last chain hotel has been converted into something else entirely.

## The Farmhouse That Became an Inn

The origin story of the New Hampshire bed and breakfast is, in nearly every case, the origin story of a farmhouse. The **New London Inn** began in 1792 when Ezekiel Sargent built a farmhouse on what was then a rough road through the woods; it became a hotel called The Elms in 1870, when Herman Currier saw that the railroad might bring people who would pay for a bed [New London Inn History](https://www.thenewlondoninn.com/history). The **Inn at Pleasant Lake** was built in 1790 as a private residence by Caleb Segur and opened as an inn in 1868 by Colonel William Messer, who called it the Red Gables Inn — a name that would survive for more than 150 years of hospitality [Inn at Pleasant Lake History](https://www.innatpleasantlake.com/history). The **Wolfeboro Inn** started as an 1812 farmhouse built by Nathaniel Rogers; the railroad arrived in 1871, bringing tourists, and the building became a guesthouse in the 1920s, later known as the Carr House [Wolfeboro Inn Story](https://www.wolfeboroinn.com/about/our-story).

<!-- IMAGE: [/api/blog/images/new-hampshire-bed-and-breakfasts_inn_at_pleasant_lake.jpg] The Inn at Pleasant Lake, a white clapboard inn on the lakeshore. -->

The pattern is so consistent it functions almost as a law of regional economics: build a farmhouse in the late 18th or early 19th century, watch the railroad arrive in the 1870s, and retrofit the building for guests. The **Sugar Hill Inn** was built in 1789 as a farmhouse and now offers fourteen rooms and cottages [NH Magazine: Bed & Breakfast and Beyond](https://www.nhmagazine.com/bed-breakfast-and-beyond-2/). The **Pinestead Farm Lodge** in Franconia was built in 1852 by Henry Whitney and opened to the public in 1899 by Joe and Mary Sherburn, who met arriving guests at the train station with a horse-drawn carriage [Pinestead Farm Lodge History](https://www.pinesteadfarmlodge.com/history). It remains family-run today — the Sherburns' descendants are still the ones meeting the guests, though the carriage has been replaced by something with an engine and the horses by something with a reservation system.

The **Blue Bear Inn** in Francestown traces its roots to 1765, when Thomas Quigley settled the land. James Wilson, a Revolutionary War veteran, built the home in 1794, and the current brick structure dates to 1822. It served as a stop on the Underground Railroad before becoming the Inn at Crotched Mountain in the 1970s and, after a 2021 rebranding, the Blue Bear Inn [Blue Bear Inn History](https://bluebearinn.com/history/). The **Three Chimneys Inn** in Durham pushes the story back even further: its original homestead was built in 1649 by Valentine Hill, making it one of the oldest buildings in New Hampshire. It served as a merchant homestead, a Victorian estate, fell into ruin, and was restored in 1997 [Three Chimneys Inn History](https://www.threechimneysinn.com/history). One might say that in New Hampshire, a building has to try several lives before it discovers it was always meant to be an inn.

## The Philbrook Century

If there is a single property that embodies the New Hampshire B&B tradition, it is the **Philbrook Farm Inn** in Shelburne, and its story deserves the telling.

The building began as a farmhouse around 1834, in the Androscoggin Valley, with views of the Carter-Moriah and Presidential ranges — views that would prove worth more than any crop ever grown in their shadow. The Philbrook family began taking guests in 1861, the first summer of the Civil War, and never stopped. For more than 120 years, it was the oldest hotel in the United States operated by the same family at the same site, passing through five generations of Philbrooks before being listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1984 [NRHP Nomination: Philbrook Farm Inn](https://npgallery.nps.gov/NRHP/GetAsset/NRHP/84002804_text).

<!-- IMAGE: [/api/blog/images/new-hampshire-bed-and-breakfasts_philbrook_exterior.jpg] Philbrook Farm Inn exterior, a white clapboard farmhouse against the Presidential Range. -->

The Philbrook survived what killed most of the grand White Mountain hotels: the automobile. When guests could drive anywhere, the logic went, why would they stay in one place? The grand hotels of the nineteenth century — the Profile House, the Crawford House, the Glen House — burned, were demolished, or simply faded from memory. The Philbrook endured, not by competing with the new resorts but by refusing to become one. It remained small, personal, and unmistakably itself. Inside, it holds an extensive collection of White Mountain memorabilia — a museum of the landscape that surrounds it, curated by the family that has watched that landscape change and learned that the one thing a mountain cannot do is hurry.

The Philbrook's story is not unique in its broad shape, only in its duration. The same impulse — a farmhouse, a scenic view, an extra room, a paying guest — animates dozens of properties across the state. The **Wolfeboro Inn**, the **Inn at Pleasant Lake**, the **New London Inn**: each represents a variation on the same theme, a family deciding that hospitality is a better crop than hay. The lesson is one that commerce has somehow never quite learned: that the most reliable income is the one that arrives with a suitcase and leaves asking for the breakfast recipe.

## The Hospitality Virus

The path to becoming an innkeeper in New Hampshire rarely runs straight. National data shows that 95 percent of B&B owners enter the industry as a second career [VerticalIQ B&B Industry Report](https://verticaliq.com/product/bed-and-breakfast-inns/), and New Hampshire's innkeepers fit the profile with almost comedic precision.

**Steve Allen** ran a shipping business before buying the Sugar Hill Inn. He traded logistics for linens, spreadsheets for scones, and discovered that running an inn requires many of the same skills as running a supply chain — inventory management, scheduling, customer relations — plus the ability to make a perfect omelet [NH Magazine: Bed & Breakfast and Beyond](https://www.nhmagazine.com/bed-breakfast-and-beyond-2/).

**Denis Swords** arrived at the Follansbee Inn on Lake Sunapee with an even more dramatic career arc. He trained and worked as a geologist, then earned a law degree and practiced law, before deciding that what he really wanted to do was run a bed and breakfast. "I was looking for a change of pace," he said, with the magnificent understatement of a man who has changed everything except his capacity for understatement [Denis Swords, Innkeeper, Follansbee Inn](https://follansbeeinn.com/denis-swords-innkeeper-follansbee-inn-new-hampshire/).

<!-- IMAGE: [/api/blog/images/new-hampshire-bed-and-breakfasts_sugar_hill_inn.jpg] Sugar Hill Inn, a white farmhouse with guest cottages in autumn. -->

The pattern recurs so often it might as well be a recognized career path. It goes like this: corporate or professional career, followed by burnout or boredom, followed by the purchase of an inn in a scenic location, followed by the unnerving discovery that hospitality is harder than it looks, followed — for those who last — by the slow acquisition of a competence that resembles nothing they brought with them. The B&B industry is, in this sense, a vast machine for recycling the talents of lawyers, bankers, and shipping executives into breakfast service, reservation management, and the delicate art of telling the same guests the same directions to the same waterfall without betraying that you have told them a hundred times before. It is a profession that rewards the skills of middle management and the soul of a butler, and it attracts precisely the people who thought they had escaped both.

## The Regulatory Architecture

What distinguishes a bed and breakfast from any other lodging establishment in New Hampshire is, strictly speaking, a matter of law. The state defines a B&B as the owner's personal residence where breakfast is the only meal served, which exempts the property from certain commercial kitchen requirements [NH Admin Code He-P 2308.02](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-hampshire/N-H-Admin-Code-SS-He-P-2308.02). This is a regulatory distinction with real consequences: it means the eggs are cooked in a home kitchen, the dining room doubles as a family room, and the line between public accommodation and private residence is deliberately, legally blurred. No other industry has ever been so precisely defined by what it is allowed to cook for breakfast.

The **New Hampshire Bed and Breakfast Association**, formed on February 22, 2002, includes roughly 40 member properties across seven tourism regions [NHBBA About](https://www.staynh.org/About/). Membership requires a food service license, a New Hampshire Meals & Rentals Tax license, that the owner lives on the premises, and that breakfast is included in the room rate [NHBBA Membership Information](https://www.staynh.org/About/Membership-Information.html). These requirements codify the tradition: a B&B is not a hotel, not a short-term rental, not a restaurant with rooms. It is a house where someone lives, and where that someone feeds you breakfast. The law has succeeded in doing what philosophy has not: it has given a clear definition to the act of being made to feel at home.

The financial architecture is equally precise. New Hampshire's **Meals & Rentals Tax** applies at 8.5 percent to both rooms and meals, generating approximately $448.5 million in fiscal 2023, a figure that was trending 3.6 percent above estimate for fiscal 2025 [NHBR: Will Canada Chatter Cool NH's Tourism?](https://www.nhbr.com/will-canada-chatter-cool-nhs-tourism/). The Accommodation sector contributes **$2.16 billion** to the state's GDP [FRED: NH Accommodation GDP](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/NHACCOMDNGSP). For every dollar the state invests in tourism marketing, it receives $15 in return [NH Division of Travel & Tourism Economic Impact Report](https://www.visitnh.gov/getmedia/a10ac1f0-45aa-41df-9cd6-63577fe08043/2026EconomicImpact.pdf). The return on investment is extraordinary by almost any measure — though the B&B owner sees none of that $15 directly, only the guests it helps lure up the driveway. The economy, like a good innkeeper, works best when no one notices it working.

## The Chains, The Platforms, The Canadians

For all their charm, New Hampshire's bed and breakfasts operate in an increasingly competitive environment, and the forces arrayed against them have names.

**The chains.** After the 2008 financial crisis, hotels from the major chains — Hilton, Hampton, Best Western — began moving into the White Mountains, a region that had previously been the preserve of independent lodgings. Ed Butler of the Notchland Inn watched this happen with the uneasy sense that something fundamental was shifting: the same brands that lined the interstates were now appearing on scenic highways, offering predictable rooms and loyalty points that no B&B could match [NPR/StateImpact: White Mountains Innkeeper Struggles](https://stateimpact.npr.org/new-hampshire/2012/06/26/getting-by-getting-ahead-white-mountains-innkeeper-struggles-with-shifting-economic-landscape/). The modern traveler, it seems, prefers the comfort of knowing exactly what they will get over the adventure of discovering something they did not know they wanted.

**The platforms.** Airbnb and its competitors have redefined what a traveler expects from a short-term stay. The same guest who might once have sought out a B&B for its character and local knowledge can now rent an entire house through an app, never speak to a host, and feel no obligation to eat breakfast at a communal table. For B&Bs, this is an existential challenge: they must compete not only on price and location but on the increasingly intangible value of human interaction, at a moment when many travelers are voting, with their booking dollars, for its absence. Younger travelers — Millennials and Gen Z — say they value authentic local experiences and unique character, but they also expect smooth digital booking and the option to be self-guided with on-demand support [Frictionless Guest: What Gen Z and Millennials Expect](https://frictionlessguest.com/post/what-gen-z-and-millennials-expect-from-independent-lodging-experiences). The B&B, which has historically traded on the presence of the innkeeper, must now learn to offer that presence without making it feel like a requirement. It is a trick that would defeat most philosophers, yet the innkeepers are expected to manage it before check-in.

**The Canadians.** Canadian visitors have historically accounted for 5 to 10 percent of New Hampshire's tourism traffic. In 2025, border crossings dropped 17 percent, driven by tariff disputes and unfavorable exchange rates [WMUR: NH Tourism Summit, Canada Tariffs](https://www.wmur.com/article/new-hampshire-tourism-summit-canada-tariffs/64770284). For B&Bs in the northern parts of the state — the North Country, the Great North Woods, the lakes region near the border — this decline is not an abstraction. It is the reservation that did not come through, the empty room in August, the polite voicemail explaining that this year, sadly, they will be vacationing closer to home. International diplomacy, it turns out, has a direct line to the breakfast table.

Meanwhile, proposed budget cuts to the state's tourism marketing budget — reductions of 30 percent or more — could result in a $68 million loss in tax revenue, according to industry estimates [InDepthNH: Tourism Industry Concerns Over Budget Cuts](https://indepthnh.org/2024/11/14/new-hampshire-tourism-industry-voices-urgent-concerns-over-proposed-budget-cuts/). The math is straightforward: less marketing means fewer visitors means emptier rooms. And emptier rooms, for a B&B, means the difference between a property that survives and one that goes back to being just a house. The state, one might observe, has yet to discover the elegant economy of spending money to make money — a principle the innkeepers understand in their bones, because they have to.

## The Next Breakfast

What comes next for New Hampshire's bed and breakfasts depends on how well they can hold two contradictory ideas at once: that their strength lies in tradition, and that tradition alone will not save them. This is the sort of paradox that would paralyze a lesser industry, but innkeepers have been living inside contradictions since the first paying guest asked for an extra blanket.

The trends are not uniformly grim. The rise of remote work means that some guests now stay for weeks rather than weekends, treating a B&B as a temporary office with better views and superior baked goods. The demand for chef-driven, farm-to-table breakfasts — accommodating special diets, sourcing locally, serving meals that guests photograph — has elevated the culinary expectations of the industry and, in doing so, raised the barrier to entry for new innkeepers [NH Magazine: Bed & Breakfast and Beyond](https://www.nhmagazine.com/bed-breakfast-and-beyond-2/). The eggs have never been more scrutinized, and they have never been better.

But the challenges are structural. The average B&B has six rooms. The average owner is in their fifties or sixties, having arrived via a mid-life career change, which means that a wave of succession is coming — innkeepers who bought their properties in their forties and fifties are now approaching retirement, and the question of who takes over has no obvious answer. The industry's demographics mirror the state's: aging, experienced, and without a clear pipeline of younger owners ready to inherit the business of making strangers feel at home. The B&B, which has always been about the transfer of comfort, now faces a crisis in the transfer of itself.

And there is the deeper question of what a B&B is for in an age of frictionless accommodation. If a guest can rent a house, order groceries for delivery, and never speak to another human being, what is the value proposition of a place that requires conversation, a shared breakfast table, and the presence of the owner? The answer, for those who remain in the business, is that the value is precisely what cannot be automated: the innkeeper who knows which hike to recommend based on the guest's boots, who remembers that the couple from Boston prefers the blueberry pancakes, who sits on the porch at sunset and points out the peaks as the light drains from the sky. That is not a service. It is a way of life, and it cannot be scaled. It cannot even be franchised. It can only be lived, one breakfast at a time.

## The Porch at Dusk

The sun has finished its work on the Presidential Range. The peaks go dark, then go gray, then go quiet. On the porch of the Philbrook Farm Inn, the rocking chairs have been pulled inside for the night, but the view remains — the same view that five generations of Philbrooks have watched from this same hill, the same view that has greeted every guest since 1861, through wars and depressions and pandemics and the slow encroachment of a world that has less and less use for a rocking chair on a porch, and yet cannot quite bring itself to abandon one.

The screen door creaks. Inside, someone is setting the table for breakfast. The eggs will be fresh. The coffee will be hot. The innkeeper will ask how you slept, and will mean it. In an age of algorithms and automated everything, sincerity remains the one thing that cannot be outsourced.

In an industry measured in billions of dollars and millions of visitors, the bed and breakfast persists because it refuses to be measured in anything larger than the individual. Not the market. Not the segment. Not the channel. Just the room, the breakfast, the view, the kindness. Two hundred years of that, and still going. It is, when you think about it, a rather good return on investment — especially when you consider that the only capital required was a house, some eggs, and the nerve to open the door to a stranger.
