# The Great Preservation

## How the national park idea conquered the world — and what 150 years of data reveal about the cost, the return, and the unfinished work

The sulfur announces itself before the geyser does. A warmth through the soles of your boots. Then the column of steam — a plume that has been bursting from this patch of Wyoming earth for longer than any living thing has troubled to count. Around it, a landscape that the United States Congress, in a moment of legislative audacity few believed would hold, declared in 1872 would never be sold, never settled, never mined. "Reserved and withdrawn from settlement, occupancy, or sale under the laws of the United States," the Yellowstone National Park Protection Act read, "and dedicated and set apart as a public park or pleasuring-ground for the benefit and enjoyment of the people." [National Archives, Act of March 1, 1872](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/act-establishing-yellowstone-national-park)

It was the first such declaration anywhere on Earth. One hundred and fifty-two years later, in 2024, more than 331 million people visited America's national park units — a record. [NPS Statistical Abstract, 2024](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/DownloadFile/722329) Those visitors spent $29 billion in local communities, generating $56.3 billion in total economic output and supporting 340,100 jobs. [Flyr et al., 2024 NPS Visitor Spending Effects Report](https://doi.org/10.36967/2315417) The idea that began in Yellowstone has proliferated to more than 300,000 protected areas across every continent, covering 54.29 million square kilometers — 17.6% of the world's land and 8.4% of its oceans. [Protected Planet Report, 2024](https://digitalreport.protectedplanet.net/)

The national park is one of the most successful ideas humans have ever had about the non-human world. This is the story of how it happened, what the data say about whether it worked, and what the next six years will decide.

## The seed: from Yosemite to Yellowstone

The national park idea did not spring from the earth fully formed. It had a precursor, and that precursor was signed into law by Abraham Lincoln in the middle of the Civil War — as though the nation, tearing itself apart, needed one piece of ground it could agree to keep whole.

On June 30, 1864, with the Union armies pushing through Virginia and the reelection campaign unfolding around him, Lincoln signed the Yosemite Valley Grant Act, granting what is now Yosemite Valley and the Mariposa Big Tree Grove to the State of California "upon the express conditions that the premises shall be held for public use, resort, and recreation; shall be inalienable for all time." [Library of Congress, Today in History, June 30](https://www.loc.gov/item/today-in-history/june-30/) The Yosemite Grant, as it came to be known, was the first time a government had set aside land specifically for preservation and public enjoyment — what California State Parks now calls "the nation's first state park." [California State Parks, 150th Anniversary](https://www.parks.ca.gov/?page_id=27478)

Frederick Law Olmsted, the landscape architect who designed Central Park and served on Yosemite's first board of commissioners, recognized almost immediately that the act of preservation and the act of public access would exist in creative tension. In his 1865 report on the park's management — a document that never reached the state legislature — Olmsted warned that "the slight harm which the few hundred visitors of this year might do, if no care were taken to prevent it, would not be slight, if it should be repeated by millions." [Library of Congress, Olmsted Papers](https://www.loc.gov/resource/mss35121.mss35121_037_0386_0553/?sp=47) He was writing about Yosemite. He was describing the central management problem of every national park that would follow — and proving that the first person to foresee a paradox is seldom the last to wrestle with it.

Eight years later, the Yellowstone National Park Protection Act went further. Rather than granting land to a state, the federal government retained ownership of a territory larger than some states. "The Yellowstone act of 1872 set a precedent and popularized the idea of preserving sections of the public domain for use as public parks," the National Park Service writes. [NPS, President Grant and the Yellowstone National Park Protection Act](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/president-grant-and-the-yellowstone-national-park-protection-act.htm) Yellowstone's enabling legislation drew directly on the Yosemite Act, using strikingly similar language about "public park or pleasuring-ground for the benefit and enjoyment of the people." [Library of Congress, Yellowstone Research Guide](https://guides.loc.gov/act-establishing-yellowstone/introduction)

Yet Yellowstone was not immediately followed by a cascade of new parks. The United States did not create a second national park until 1875 (Mackinac Island, later transferred to Michigan), and it would take until 1890 for Yosemite, Sequoia, and General Grant (now Kings Canyon) to join the system. [NPS, The National Parks: Shaping the System](https://npshistory.com/publications/shaping-the-system-2005.pdf) The idea spread faster across oceans than across state lines — as great ideas often do, once they discover that their home country is not yet ready for them.

## The spread: a global idea in seventeen years

Within fifteen years of Yellowstone's establishment, three other nations had national parks of their own. Australia created Royal National Park — originally simply "The National Park" — just south of Sydney in 1879. [Frost & Laing, "From Yellowstone to Australia and New Zealand," Global Environment, 2013](https://www.environmentandsociety.org/sites/default/files/key_docs/ge12_frost-laing.pdf) Canada followed in 1885 with Rocky Mountain National Park, now Banff, set aside around the hot springs discovered during construction of the transcontinental railway. [The Canadian Encyclopedia, National Parks Timeline](https://thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/en/timeline/national-parks) New Zealand created Tongariro National Park in 1887, after the Ngāti Tūwharetoa chief Horonuku Te Heuheu Tukino IV gifted three sacred mountains to the Crown on the condition that they never be sold. [EBSCO, National Parks System of Australia and New Zealand](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/national-parks-system-australia-and-new-zealand)

None of these were exact copies of the American model. Australia's early national parks were conceived primarily as recreation reserves — public pleasure grounds for the urban populations of Sydney and Melbourne — rather than the monumental wilderness preserves of the American West. New Zealand's parks were shaped from the beginning by indigenous land claims and the gift relationship between Māori iwi and the Crown. The Canadian parks were tied to railway tourism and commercial development from the outset. As Frost and Laing write, "the second wave laid the foundations for the internationalisation of the national parks concept" — by demonstrating that the idea could be adapted, not merely replicated. [Frost & Laing, 2013](https://www.environmentandsociety.org/sites/default/files/key_docs/ge12_frost-laing.pdf)

Europe's first national parks arrived in Sweden, where the Riksdag established nine parks in 1909, including Abisko and Sarek in the far north. In Switzerland, the Swiss National Park (established 1914) offered an alternative model: stricter protection, fewer visitors, and a research orientation that stood in deliberate contrast to the American emphasis on mass access. [Berghahn Books, Kupper, "Creating Wilderness: A Global History of the National Park"](https://www.berghahnbooks.com/downloads/intros/KupperCreating_intro.pdf) Europe, characteristically, decided that a pleasure-ground could be improved by making it slightly less pleasurable to visit.

The spread accelerated after World War II. Africa's first national park had been Virunga in the Belgian Congo (1925), created to protect mountain gorillas, followed by Kruger in South Africa (1926). [BBC Travel, "Birth and spread of the world's national parks", 2012](https://www.bbc.com/travel/article/20120426-travelwise-birth-and-spread-of-the-worlds-national-parks) India's first national park — Hailey, now Jim Corbett — was established in 1936 to protect Bengal tigers. The postcolonial era saw a surge: by the 1960s and 1970s, dozens of newly independent nations were establishing their own park systems, often drawing on the Yellowstone model while adapting it to local ecologies and political realities. The empire had made its maps; the newly free redrew them with green.

## The numbers: visitation and economic impact

The U.S. National Park Service has tracked visitation since 1904, the year before Theodore Roosevelt — a president who would quadruple the system's acreage via the Antiquities Act — began his full term. That year, 120,690 people visited the areas that would eventually become the National Park System. [NPS, Visitation Numbers](https://www.nps.gov/aboutus/visitation-numbers.htm) One hundred and twenty thousand people, who had found their way to something the government had decided to keep.

By 1930, the number had climbed to 3 million. By 1950, 32 million. By 1970, 168 million. By 2024, 331.9 million — more than the entire population of the United States, drawn to 433 units covering 85 million acres. [NPS, National Park System](https://www.nps.gov/aboutus/national-park-system.htm); [NPS Statistical Abstract, 2024](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/DownloadFile/722329) The growth curve is not perfectly linear — the Great Depression, World War II, and the COVID-19 pandemic each produced sharp dips — but the long-term trajectory is unmistakable: a 275,000% increase over 120 years. There are few human endeavors whose popularity has grown quite so steadily. There are fewer still that deserved to.

![From 120,000 to 332 million: the quietest revolution in American leisure.](/api/blog/images/the-great-preservation_visitation.png)

The economic multiplier is equally striking. In 2024, the National Park Service released its annual peer-reviewed Visitor Spending Effects report, prepared by agency economists Matthew Flyr, Evan Stockmoe, Catherine Cullinane Thomas, Lynne Koontz, and Christopher Huber. The findings: visitors spent $29.0 billion in local gateway regions, supporting 340,100 jobs, generating $18.8 billion in labor income, $33.7 billion in value added to GDP, and $56.3 billion in total economic output. [Flyr et al., 2024 NPS Visitor Spending Effects Report](https://doi.org/10.36967/2315417) The lodging sector led all categories, with $11.1 billion in direct output, followed by restaurants at $5.7 billion. It turns out that what people want, once you give them a landscape they cannot own, is a place to sleep and someone else to cook dinner.

These are not small numbers. Adjusted for inflation, 2024 visitor spending was the highest in more than a decade — 10% above 2023. [USA Facts, via NPS data](https://usafacts.org/articles/how-do-national-parks-affect-the-economy/) Even after accounting for the 31.8% pandemic-era collapse in 2020, the system has recovered to set consecutive records in 2023 and 2024. The hunger for protected places, it seems, is only sharpened by being denied them.

## The quiet sibling: America's state parks

Less visible in the national conversation — but quantitatively larger — are America's state parks. The National Association of State Park Directors, drawing on the Annual Information Exchange database maintained by Utah State University, reports 9,817 state park units across 20.1 million acres. In 2024, they received an estimated 867 million visits. [NASPD, About Us](https://www.stateparks.org/about-us/); [Leung & Smith, AIX Statistical Reports, USU](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/extension_curall/1988)

That is more than double the NPS visitation figure, on less than a quarter of the acreage. State parks are the workhorses of the American outdoor recreation system — closer to population centers, more numerous, and, for millions of Americans, the first (and only) encounter with a protected landscape. Visitation to state parks has risen steadily since systematic record-keeping began in 1984, when the system logged 642.6 million visits. [Smith & Leung, 2017 Outlook Letter, USU](https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2827&context=extension_curall) The 2016 peak of 791.4 million visits has since been surpassed.

Yet state park systems face chronic underfunding. Operating expenditures have not kept pace with visitation growth in most states. The Resources for the Future backgrounder on state parks noted that while the systems vary widely across states, "current challenges and the outlook for the future" revolve around a persistent gap between what parks are asked to provide and what they are given to provide it. [RFF, "Parks and Recreation: State Park Systems", 2009](https://www.rff.org/publications/issue-briefs/parks-and-recreation-in-the-united-states-state-park-systems/) It is the oldest story in public life: the things people use most are the things they fund least.

## The planetary moment: the 30×30 target

The national park idea has always had a numerical dimension — how much land is enough? The first global target for protected areas was the 2010 goal of conserving 10% of the Earth's surface, set under the Convention on Biological Diversity. In 2010, the Aichi Biodiversity Target 11 raised the bar to 17% of terrestrial and 10% of marine areas by 2020. [Geldmann, "The World Could Reach 30% Protection by 2030," Conservation Letters, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/con4.70030) The targets grew more ambitious, as targets do when nothing less than the truth will do.

The world missed the Aichi deadline, but just barely: 16.4% of land was protected by 2024, according to the World Bank, and 17.6% when inland waters are included, per the Protected Planet Report. [World Bank, Terrestrial Protected Areas Indicator](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/ER.LND.PTLD.ZS); [Protected Planet Report, 2024](https://digitalreport.protectedplanet.net/)

In December 2022, 196 parties to the Convention on Biological Diversity adopted the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework. Target 3 — the "30×30" target — commits the world to effectively conserve and manage at least 30% of terrestrial, inland water, and marine and coastal areas by 2030. [CBD, 2030 Targets](https://www.cbd.int/gbf/targets)

![Seventeen-point-six percent of land, eight-point-four percent of oceans. The gap between ambition and achievement.](/api/blog/images/the-great-preservation_protected_area.png)

As of 2024, the gap is substantial: 12.4 percentage points on land, 21.6 points in the ocean. But the pace is accelerating. Since 2020, the global network has added 629,262 km² of terrestrial protection — an area roughly the size of France. Marine protection has grown by 1.77 million km² — larger than the Gulf of Mexico. Fifty-one countries and territories have already met the 30% threshold. [Protected Planet Report, 2024](https://digitalreport.protectedplanet.net/)

Yet a growing body of research warns that area alone is an inadequate metric. A 2026 Conservation Letters analysis by Jonas Geldmann and colleagues found that "if area continues to dominate reporting, the global community could meet 30×30 and still fail to conserve biodiversity." Representativeness, connectivity, management effectiveness, and equitable governance matter as much as acreage. [Geldmann, Conservation Letters, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/con4.70030) It is possible to protect the wrong land perfectly — and the right land hardly at all. The map is not the mountain.

## The forest that stores the carbon

Protected areas do more than host tourists. They store carbon. A 2021 study published in the Chinese Journal of Applied Ecology estimated that China's 881 national forest parks — covering 5.8% of the country's forest area — stored 3.56 petagrams of carbon (Pg C), accounting for 11–12.2% of the total carbon stored in China's forest ecosystems. The average carbon density in these parks was 255.18 t C·ha⁻², higher than the national average. [Li et al., "Spatial-temporal variations of carbon storage in China's national forest parks", 2021](https://www.cjae.net/EN/10.13287/j.1001-9332.202103.015)

In the Western United States, a 2020 study in Ecological Applications modeled the potential of high-productivity, low-vulnerability forests — many of them within protected areas — to sequester up to 5,450 Tg CO₂ equivalent by 2099, representing roughly 20% of the total mitigation potential previously identified for all temperate and boreal forests globally. [Buotte et al., Ecological Applications, 2020](https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/eap.2039)

These findings confirm something conservation biologists have long suspected: parks are not luxuries. They are carbon infrastructure. A nation may build a park for pleasure and discover, a century later, that it has built a climate solution instead — as though nature had quietly been doing the accounts while everyone was looking at the geysers.

## The body: what parks do to human health

The most recent frontier in parks research is not ecological but medical. A growing body of evidence — including randomized controlled trials — suggests that time in protected natural environments produces measurable improvements in cardiovascular and mental health. The wilderness, it turns out, has been a physician all along, requiring only that we submit to its attendance.

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis by Nguyen and colleagues at Monash University, published in The Lancet's affiliated journals, identified 92 studies on "nature prescription" programs — interventions in which health or social professionals prescribe time in nature. Pooled results showed statistically significant reductions in systolic blood pressure (–4.82 mmHg), diastolic blood pressure (–3.82 mmHg), moderate-to-large effects on depression (SMD –0.50) and anxiety (SMD –0.57), and an increase in daily step counts averaging 900 steps per day. [Nguyen et al., "Effect of nature prescriptions on cardiometabolic and mental health," 2022](https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/lcdocs/other/20796/Monash%20University%20-%20Paper%207%20-%20received%2017%20December%202024.pdf) The prescription is simple: go outside. The cure is free. The only difficulty is persuading a species that has spent ten thousand years building walls to finally step beyond them.

![From blood pressure to daily steps: the measurable body of evidence for nature as medicine.](/api/blog/images/the-great-preservation_health.png)

A 2026 randomized controlled trial published in Scientific Reports examined the psychophysiological effects of a 30-minute walk in an urban forest versus an indoor setting. The outdoor group showed significantly greater improvements in directed attention, greater reductions in salivary cortisol, and increased parasympathetic activity (heart rate variability). The authors concluded that "green exercise exerts synergistic benefits across cognitive, affective, and physiological domains." [Yan et al., Scientific Reports, 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-60002-1)

Japan's practice of shinrin-yoku — "forest bathing" — has accumulated its own evidence base. A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis of shinrin-yoku studies found short-term improvements in cardiovascular function and mental health, recommending it as a complementary approach for hypertension and chronic stress. The review noted that "the increasing acceptance of shinrin-yoku by the medical community has led to the implementation of healthcare programs that prescribe nature." [Szkaradowska et al., Frontiers in Psychology, 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1707829/full) The Japanese, who have long understood that a forest is a kind of temple, have now produced the clinical trials to prove it.

The implication is clear: national and state parks are not merely recreation amenities. They are public health infrastructure. What we once called a "pleasuring-ground" turns out to have been a hospital in disguise.

## The continuity of the idea

The National Park Service Organic Act, signed by Woodrow Wilson on August 25, 1916, created a federal bureau charged with a dual mandate: "to conserve the scenery and the natural and historic objects and the wildlife therein and to provide for the enjoyment of the same in such manner and by such means as will leave them unimpaired for the enjoyment of future generations." [NPS, Organic Act](https://www.nps.gov/aboutus/national-park-system.htm)

That tension — conservation and enjoyment, preservation and access — has never been resolved. It is not meant to be. It is the generative contradiction at the heart of the national park idea. Yellowstone drew 4.5 million visitors in 2024. [NPS, Annual Park Ranking Report, 2024](https://npshistory.com/publications/visitation/annual-park-ranking/2024.pdf) That is forty times the annual visitation the park received in the 1950s. Each of those visitors arrived, in some sense, to see the same thing the Washburn Expedition saw in 1870: a landscape held apart from the ordinary processes of ownership and exchange. The difference is that now, 152 years later, there are 331.9 million of them — standing on boardwalks, pointing at geysers, asking each other what time Old Faithful is scheduled to erupt. They have come to see the uncontrolled, and they want to know when it will happen.

The park idea has proliferated because it works, in a way that few human institutions do. It simultaneously preserves something ancient — the functioning of an ecosystem, the shape of a mountain, the migration path of a wolf — and produces something modern: a $56 billion economic return, a 4.82 mmHg reduction in blood pressure, a 30% global conservation target that has reshaped the environmental policy of every nation on Earth.

What began as a single legislative act — signed by a president who never visited the park he protected — has become the dominant framework through which humans organize their relationship with the natural world. The great preservation is not complete. The 30% target is not yet met. The health evidence is still accumulating. But the trajectory is legible, and it has been legible for more than a century: what we set aside, we keep. What we keep, we measure. What we measure, we understand. And what we understand, we have a chance of not destroying.
