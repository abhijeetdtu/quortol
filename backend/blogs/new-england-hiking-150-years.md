# The Art of Sauntering: 150 Years of Walking in New England

*In 1876, a handful of Boston gentlemen founded the Appalachian Mountain Club. A century and a half later, 16.9 million people walk the trails they built — and the philosophy that started it all has never been more urgently needed.*

---

On a Saturday morning in July 2025, a young woman from Somerville, Massachusetts, parked her Subaru at the Lincoln Woods trailhead in New Hampshire's White Mountain National Forest, laced her boots, and began walking. She followed the East Branch of the Pemigewasset River for 2.7 miles, crossed a suspension bridge, and started climbing. The trail steepened. Roots crossed the path like knuckles. The air thinned. By the time she broke treeline on Mount Flume, the temperature had dropped fifteen degrees and the view had opened across the Pemigewasset Wilderness — ridge after ridge of northern hardwood and spruce-fir, folding into a hazy blue distance that suggested, with the persuasive dishonesty of all beautiful views, that the world had no end.

She was, in one sense, merely one person walking in a forest. In another, she was the latest expression of a tradition of organized hiking in New England that is, in 2026, exactly 150 years old.

The Appalachian Mountain Club, the oldest outdoor recreation and conservation nonprofit in the United States, celebrates its sesquicentennial this year. Founded on January 8, 1876, in a lecture room at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, the AMC began as a gathering of "thirty or forty gentlemen" interested in "exploring the mountains of New England and the adjacent regions, both for scientific and artistic purposes" [AMC History](https://www.outdoors.org/about/history/). Their first outing, that July, drew 175 people to the White Mountains via the Crawford Notch train. They climbed Mount Willard, admired the view of the notch, and returned to Boston with the satisfied air of people who had invented something whose name would not arrive until they needed it.

They had invented, without quite realizing it, the American framework for mass participation in wilderness recreation. A century and a half and an almost incomprehensible increase in scale later, the full shape of their invention has finally come into view.

## I. The Boston Gentlemen Who Walked Into History

The AMC was not, at first, a conservation organization. Its early members were amateur scientists, topographers, and mountain enthusiasts — the sort of late-Victorian New Englanders who believed that a proper education demanded both the ability to identify a schist formation and the moral fortitude to climb a peak before breakfast.

The club's first major project was building trails and huts in the White Mountains, which had become a summer destination for Boston's elite via the newly completed railroad through Crawford Notch. In 1888, the AMC built its first hut at Madison Spring — a stone shelter at 4,800 feet that could accommodate a dozen hikers [AMC Hut History](https://www.outdoors.org/about/history/). It was the beginning of a system that now includes eight huts along a 56-mile stretch of the Appalachian Trail in New Hampshire, capable of sleeping nearly 1,000 hikers per night — a lodging network that has somehow made the wilderness feel both pristine and almost civilized.

But the club's most consequential early act was not building — it was buying. In the 1890s, logged-over hillsides and clearcut slopes had become common sights in the White Mountains, and the AMC, unlike many of its contemporaries, recognized that scenic beauty could not survive market logic. In 1893, the club established a New Hampshire Forestry Fund and began purchasing land. One of its first acquisitions: thirty-six acres along Snyder Brook in Randolph, New Hampshire, protecting a remnant of old-growth forest from imminent logging [AMC Land Trust History](https://www.outdoors.org/resources/amc-outdoors/conservation-and-climate/in-land-we-trust/).

This was, in retrospect, the moment the AMC ceased to be merely a walking club and became something more consequential. A group of people who loved a landscape had decided to pay real money to stop other people from destroying it — a concept almost too simple to mention, and so radical that it is still being litigated, a hundred and fifty years later, in courtrooms across the country.

## II. The First Footpath

In 1909, a Vermont educator named James P. Taylor found himself on the summit of Stratton Mountain, waiting for the clouds to clear. He was, by most accounts, lost — a condition that has given rise to more useful ideas than clarity ever has. Somewhere between the fog and the frustration, an idea formed: a footpath running the length of the Green Mountains, from the Massachusetts line to the Canadian border — a "long trail" that would make "the Vermont mountains play a larger part in the life of the people" [VTDigger](https://vtdigger.org/2021/06/20/then-again-james-taylor-father-of-the-long-trail-had-plenty-of-help/).

On March 11, 1910, Taylor convened a meeting at the Van Ness Hotel in Burlington. Twenty-three people attended. The Green Mountain Club was founded. Its treasury held $100. The goal: clear a 270-mile path through some of the most rugged terrain in New England.

Work began on October 1, 1910. Club members scouted routes, hacked through undergrowth, and cut trail with hand tools. By the fall of 1911, they had cleared 29 miles from Camel's Hump north to Smugglers Notch. The Long Trail was completed in 1930 — the first long-distance hiking trail in the United States, and proof that a great deal can be accomplished with hand tools, optimism, and a complete disregard for the probable [Green Mountain Club](https://www.greenmountainclub.org/the-long-trail/).

The Long Trail is not merely a historic footnote. It is the direct ancestor of the Appalachian Trail, which shares 100 miles of its route through southern Vermont. Benton MacKaye, the visionary forester who conceived the AT, studied the Long Trail in detail before publishing his 1921 proposal in the Journal of the American Institute of Architects. He adopted its shelter system, its white-blaze marking system, and its cooperative management model — lessons learned from a trail that had been built, as one early member put it, "by a handful of men with axes and a great deal of optimism" [VermontBiz](https://vermontbiz.com/news/2012/august/16/vermont-had-significant-role-development-appalachian-trail).

What a handful of people with axes had demonstrated was that a long trail could exist as a cooperative venture — maintained by volunteers, supported by members, and held in trust for everyone. It was an idea so durable that it has now been replicated across thousands of miles of footpath on every continent — which is how one knows it was built for love, not for profit.

## III. The Law That Saved the Mountains

The same year the Green Mountain Club began cutting the Long Trail, the White Mountains were being cut to pieces. Logging had accelerated so rapidly that visitors to Crawford Notch could hear the saws from the hotel verandas. The watersheds were eroding. The hillsides, stripped of their hardwood cover, looked, in the words of one Boston journalist, "like a battlefield after the fight."

What saved them was the Weeks Act of 1911 — a piece of legislation that the AMC had lobbied for relentlessly over the preceding decade. Allen Chamberlain, a former AMC president and a prolific journalist, wrote article after article for Boston newspapers, hammering the message that eastern forests needed federal protection. The AMC organized exhibits at the Boston Sportsman's Show, mobilized members to write Congress, and sent delegations to Washington. When the bill finally passed, on March 1, 1911, President William Howard Taft signed it into law, authorizing the federal government to purchase private lands for the protection of watersheds — and, incidentally, creating the mechanism that would establish the White Mountain National Forest [AMC Weeks Act History](https://www.outdoors.org/resources/amc-outdoors/history/the-legislation-that-saved-the-white-mountain-region/).

The scale of its impact resists easy summary. Nearly 20 million acres of forestland have been protected under the Weeks Act, including more than 40 national forests across the eastern United States. The White Mountain National Forest alone — 800,000 acres of hiking trails, ski slopes, trout streams, and old-growth stands — exists because people who walked in the mountains arrived at a rare conclusion: that walking alone, however satisfying, does not preserve what one walks through. One must also fight for it.

## IV. The Numbers That Defy the Imagination

In 1876, the AMC could count its members in the hundreds. The trails they walked were measured in miles, not thousands of miles. The notion that anyone would bother to count visitors to a footpath would have struck them as absurd — a category error, like counting visitors to the sky.

In 2025, the Appalachian Trail Conservancy and the National Park Service released the first-ever accurate visitation estimate for the Appalachian Trail: 16.9 million recreation visits in a single year [ATC Visitation](https://appalachiantrail.org/protect/trail-management/visitor-use-management/visitation/). The sections managed by the National Park Service accounted for 6.2 million of those visits, making the Appalachian National Scenic Trail the ninth most visited unit in the entire National Park System — ahead of the Grand Canyon, ahead of Yosemite, behind only the Blue Ridge Parkway and the Golden Gate National Recreation Area — a ranking that its founders, who could scarcely have imagined the Grand Canyon as a tourist destination, would find more bewildering than flattering [The Trek](https://thetrek.co/appalachian-trail/thanks-to-a-new-counting-method-we-finally-know-how-many-people-actually-use-the-appalachian-trail-each-year/).

The New England states alone account for a significant share: New Hampshire recorded 2.88 million visits to the AT (second only to Virginia, which has far more miles of trail); Massachusetts, 1.08 million; Connecticut, 535,000; Vermont, 533,000; Maine, 85,000. The White Mountain National Forest, meanwhile, draws approximately six million visitors per year — more than Yosemite or Yellowstone National Parks [UNH News](https://www.unh.edu/news/2025/08/whos-visiting-new-hampshires-white-mountains).

The numbers would baffle the AMC's founders. The impulse behind them would not: the same desire to walk into a landscape and be altered by it, now multiplied by millions and enabled by the infrastructure those founders began building with their own hands.

## V. The Philosophy of the Footpath

Why do they do it? The question is older than the AMC — older, in fact, than most of the trails its members walk. It was being asked the first time a New Englander walked into the woods for reasons other than survival.

Henry David Thoreau, who died in 1862, fourteen years before the AMC was founded, wrote the region's definitive answer in his essay "Walking," first delivered as a lecture in 1851. "I wish to speak a word for Nature," he began, "for absolute freedom and wildness, as contrasted with a freedom and culture merely civil — to regard man as an inhabitant, or a part and parcel of Nature, rather than a member of society" [Thoreau, Walking](https://gutenberg.org/files/1022/1022-h/1022-h.htm).

Thoreau derived the word "sauntering" from the medieval "Sainte-Terrer" — a Holy-Lander, one who walked to the Holy Land. "For every walk is a sort of crusade," he wrote. But his insight was not merely etymological. He understood that walking was a form of thinking — that the rhythm of the feet freed the mind from its usual circuits. "The saunterer, in the good sense, is no more vagrant than the meandering river," he wrote. "He who sits still in a house all the time may be the greatest vagrant of all."

Modern science has spent the last four decades confirming what Thoreau knew before he had finished his first paragraph. The Attention Restoration Theory, developed by environmental psychologists Rachel and Stephen Kaplan in the 1980s, proposes that natural environments — with their "soft fascination," their gentle sensory engagement — restore directed attention in ways that urban environments cannot [Frontiers in Public Health, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2025.1700325/full). A 2017 randomized crossover trial published in PLOS One found that three hours of mountain hiking produced significantly greater improvements in mood, activation, and calmness than the same duration of indoor treadmill walking — and significantly reduced anxiety and fatigue [PLOS One, 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0177719). A 2024 systematic review of 17 studies confirmed that nature-based walking interventions improve mood, optimism, and mental well-being while reducing stress, anxiety, and negative rumination [ResearchGate, 2024](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373236134_Effectiveness_of_nature-based_walking_interventions_in_improving_mental_health_in_adults_a_systematic_review).

The biophilia hypothesis — the evolutionary argument that humans have an innate affinity for natural systems — adds another layer. We are, the theory suggests, a species that evolved in savannas and forests, and our nervous systems have not fully adapted to a world of concrete, screens, and fluorescent light. Walking in the woods is not, in any serious sense, a leisure activity. It is a homecoming — a return to an environment our nervous systems still recognize as home, even if our calendars insist otherwise.

But the deeper answer may be simpler still. The walker on the trail is not, despite appearances, escaping meaning. She is creating the conditions under which meaning has room to arrive on its own. The path becomes a kind of template: follow it, and the mind follows its own hidden contours. The 19th-century AMC members, climbing Mount Willard in stiff collars and hobnailed boots, understood this even if they lacked the vocabulary for it. They walked because walking was the most direct route to a kind of attention that modern life — with its genius for interruption — had rendered impossible indoors.

## VI. The Weight of 150 Years

The AMC of 2026 is a very different organization from the one that met in the Rogers Building in 1876. Its operating budget runs into the tens of millions. It manages more than 114,000 acres of conservation land in Maine alone. Its All Out plan — the club's current five-year strategic vision — calls for protecting an additional 30,000 acres of Maine's 100-Mile Wilderness, building accessible "all persons trails" in southern New England, and cutting operational greenhouse gas emissions by 45% [AMC All Out](https://digitalcommons.dartmouth.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2148&context=appalachia).

And yet the core of its work has remained stubbornly, almost defiantly, unchanged. It still builds and maintains trails. It still educates people about the natural world. It still fights to protect the landscapes its members love. To mark its 150th anniversary, the club organized a 2,000-mile relay from Virginia to Maine — a walking procession that traced the spine of the Appalachians and honored the people and places that shaped the club's legacy [AMC 150th](https://www.outdoors.org/about/history/).

And the people still come. On any given summer weekend, the trailheads of the White Mountains fill by dawn. The Franconia Ridge trail counter — an electronic monitor embedded in the alpine zone — recorded days exceeding 900 visitors in 2024, a 300% increase from a decade earlier [Mountain Steward](https://www.mountainsteward.net/2025/06/25/the-numbers-game/). The parking lot at the Lincoln Woods trailhead overflows onto the Kancamagus Highway by 8 a.m. The AMC's eight White Mountain huts — which require reservations months in advance — serve 40,000 guests per season, each of whom has discovered that the finest hotel in New Hampshire is a hut you have to walk to.

What Thoreau called "the art of Walking" has become, in the aggregate, a regional economic engine. New England's outdoor recreation industry supports 432,000 jobs and generates $51.6 billion in consumer spending annually [Wildlands & Woodlands](https://www.wildlandsandwoodlands.org/sites/default/files/The%20Economic%20Case%20for%20Conservation.pdf). The Bureau of Economic Analysis reports that the outdoor recreation sector contributed $696.7 billion to national GDP in 2024 — 2.4 percent of the entire economy [BEA 2024](https://www.bea.gov/news/2026/outdoor-recreation-economic-statistics-us-and-states-2024). Massachusetts led the nation in growth at 6.9%, generating $14.6 billion for the state's economy [Mass.gov](https://www.mass.gov/news/new-report-massachusetts-leads-us-in-growth-of-outdoor-recreation-contributing-over-146-billion-to-states-economy). New Hampshire's outdoor recreation sector contributed $4.2 billion, supporting 33,000 jobs [NH Business Review](https://www.nhbr.com/federal-data-outdoor-recreation-generates-4-2b-for-nh-economy/).

The numbers have grown so large that they risk obscuring the simplicity of the act that generates them: one person, putting one foot in front of the other, on a path through the woods. Every statistic in this article traces back to that single motion — one foot after the other — repeated until it became an economy.

## VII. The Trail Ahead

The challenges facing New England's trail system are formidable, which is a polite way of saying they are deeply alarming. Climate change is shortening winter recreation seasons and stressing alpine ecosystems. Invasive species — the hemlock woolly adelgid, the emerald ash borer — are rewriting the forests that the trails pass through. The surge in post-COVID visitation has placed unprecedented pressure on trail infrastructure and on the volunteer networks that maintain it. The White Mountain National Forest's most recent visitor use survey, conducted in 2025, may confirm that the pandemic-era explosion in outdoor recreation was not a spike but a permanent shift [NHPR](https://www.nhpr.org/nh-news/2025-09-22/they-want-to-what-youre-up-to-in-the-white-mountain-national-forest).

But the tradition that began on a January evening in 1876 has proven resilient before. The AMC has survived two world wars, the Great Depression, the decline of the railroad, the rise of the automobile, the invention of Gore-Tex, the digitization of everything, and a pandemic. It has outlasted the industries that seemed, in their time, like permanent features of the New England landscape. It has done so by treating its methods as negotiable and its purpose as sacred: to help people know and love the outdoors, and to protect the places they walk.

The woman from Somerville, standing on the summit of Mount Flume, did not arrive there by accident. The trail beneath her feet was scouted by AMC volunteers in the 1920s, maintained by generations of trail crews, and protected by legislation that the club helped pass more than a century ago. The suspension bridge she crossed was built with donations from people she will never meet. The alpine zone she stood in — fragile, treeless, older than the country itself — exists because people who came before believed that some things are worth more than the timber that could be cut from them.

She may not have known any of this. The trail does not offer plaques explaining its own history. It offers something better: the clean white blaze on a tree, pointing the way forward; the sound of wind through spruce; the hard, satisfying work of climbing. The tradition is invisible only because it has been so thoroughly absorbed into the earth beneath her feet.

Perhaps that is the truest monument to what the founders built. A trail that has been walked for 150 years no longer needs to explain itself. It simply is. And the people who walk it — whether for an afternoon or a season or a lifetime — become part of something they may never fully name.

"The saunterer," Thoreau wrote, "is no more vagrant than the meandering river." One hundred and seventy-five years after he wrote it, sixteen million people a year prove him right. They are not lost. They are finding their way — one step at a time, the only method by which anyone has ever truly arrived anywhere.
