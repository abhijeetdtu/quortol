# The Horse and the Machine

## I.

In the summer of 1829, a glue manufacturer from New York City named Peter Cooper walked into a coach-maker's shop in Baltimore carrying two disassembled muskets. He was thirty-eight, which is old enough to know when a thing cannot be done and young enough to do it anyway. He had purchased a small steam engine of about one horsepower — the cylinder was three and a half inches in diameter, the stroke fourteen and a quarter inches — and had improvised a boiler from sheet iron. But he could not find iron pipes in the United States to connect the boiler to the engine. There were none for sale in the country. So he took the two muskets, broke off the wooden stocks, and used the barrels as tubing. He laid them side by side, one on each side of the boiler, and joined them to the engine. Then he mounted the whole contraption on four wheels borrowed from the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad.

He called it the Tom Thumb, because it was so insignificant.

[Source: Peter Cooper's 1875 recollection, reprinted in the American Railway Master Mechanics' Association, quoted in *Forgotten History of Ellicott City & Howard County, Maryland*](http://historichomeshowardcounty.blogspot.com/2017/01/tom-thumb-steam-engine-vs-horse.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Thumb_(locomotive))

The muskets Cooper broke apart had been made somewhere — perhaps at the federal armory in Springfield, Massachusetts, or at Harpers Ferry, Virginia. They had been produced by the same system that had armed the American Revolution half a century earlier: hand-forged barrels, hand-carved stocks, hand-fitted locks, each part filed and shaped by a man who had learned the trade over years. No two parts were the same. No two guns were truly identical. Each was a unique object — a quality we admire in art and regret in warfare.

Cooper shoved the barrels of those guns into a boiler and made the future happen faster than it had ever happened before. The horse that the musket had once been carried by would, within a year, be racing the machine that the musket had become.

## II.

When the Revolutionary War began in 1775, the United States had no national armory, no standardized weapon, and no industrial capacity for producing them at scale. A soldier carried a musket — a smoothbore, muzzle-loading flintlock that could be aimed with reasonable hope of hitting a man at about eighty yards and very little hope past a hundred. The weapon was handmade: the barrel forged by a blacksmith, the stock carved by a woodworker, the lock assembled by a gunsmith. If a part broke on campaign, you could not swap it with a dead comrade's weapon, because his parts would not fit yours. Each gun was a unique object, and if you were the soldier carrying it, you learned to value uniqueness a little less.

The country itself moved at the same pace. Roads were rutted paths through forest. Communication went by mounted rider or sailing ship. News of the Treaty of Paris — the document that ended the war in 1783 — took six weeks to reach the interior of the country. Agriculture was done with a scythe and a cradle: a good man could harvest two acres of grain in a day, if the weather held and his body did not give out.

[Source: *Grain Harvesting*, Virginia Tech; [American Battlefield Trust](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/small-arms-across-three-wars)]

This was the world the horse had made. It was not slow because people were lazy. It was slow because energy came only from muscle — human and animal — and from wind and falling water. There was no other way to make things move. There was no other way to make things at all.

## III.

The first hint that this world could change came from a Yale graduate who had gone to Georgia to study law and ended up inventing a machine for removing seeds from cotton. Eli Whitney's cotton gin, patented in 1794, was a cylinder studded with wire hooks that pulled cotton fiber through a grid of narrow slots. The seeds were too large to pass through and fell away. His petition to Secretary of State Thomas Jefferson contained a number that must have seemed impossible:

> "With this Ginn, if turned with horses or by water, two persons will clean as much cotton in one Day, as a Hundred persons could cleane in the same time with the ginns now in common use."

A single device, Whitney claimed, could produce **fifty pounds** of cleaned cotton in a day.

[Source: [Whitney's petition to Jefferson, June 20, 1793](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/aia/part3/3h1517t.html); [National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/patent-for-cotton-gin)]

Whitney's machine worked. Cotton became profitable. The yield of raw cotton doubled every decade after 1800. By 1850, America was growing three-quarters of the world's supply. But the gin solved only the cleaning problem. The cotton still had to get to market — and moving anything in America in 1794 meant a horse, a wagon, a sailboat, or a man's own back.

The real breakthrough was not in the field. It was in the workshop of a thirty-year-old inventor from Sutton, Massachusetts, named Thomas Blanchard.

Blanchard had already invented a tack-making machine at age eighteen that could produce **five hundred tacks per minute**. By 1818, working at the Springfield Armory, he had built a lathe that could cut the exterior surface of a musket barrel automatically, using a cam to guide the cutter. The stock makers at the Armory watched this machine and felt, one imagines, the particular dread of a craftsman observing a machine that has not yet learned its place. According to the legend that survives at Springfield, one of them expressed doubt that any machine could ever be invented to replicate the skill of a man who carved wood by hand.

Blanchard took the dare. In 1819, he patented his wood-turning lathe for cutting irregular forms. The machine worked like a key cutter: an iron master form in the shape of a musket stock rotated slowly, a guide wheel traced its contours, and a cutting wheel made identical movements on a block of walnut. A semi-skilled operator could now produce a gunstock in minutes that had once taken a master carver hours.

[Source: [NPS Springfield Armory](https://www.nps.gov/spar/learn/historyculture/thomas-blanchard-and-his-lathe.htm); [The Henry Ford](https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/articles/thomas-blanchards-wood-copying-lathe)]

Blanchard added thirteen more specialized machines to his system — a milling machine for inletting, a machine for cutting the lock mortise, a machine for shaping the barrel channel. All of them were installed at Springfield and Harpers Ferry. By 1825, the Springfield Armory had become what the business historian Alfred Chandler would later call "an even more important prototype of the modern factory than the integrated textile mill."

The machine that could not be built had been built. And the man who had said it could not be built had been replaced by it.

## IV.

It is difficult to convey what a passage on the most controversial vessel in America could cost in the summer of 1807, but the introductory fare was **$3** — set to match sailing-vessel prices, as if to apologize for the offense of arriving sooner. The regular advertised fare was **$7** for the full route. Robert Fulton's *North River Steamboat* — a hundred-and-fifty-foot-long, thirteen-foot-wide, steam-belching craft that a farmer famously described as "the devil going up the river in a sawmill" — began scheduled passenger service between New York City and Albany in September 1807. The journey took about thirty-six hours. Before Fulton, the same trip by sloop could take five days against the wind.

Fulton described the morning of his first departure in a letter to a friend:

> "The morning I left New York, there were not perhaps **thirty persons** in the city who believed that the boat would ever move one mile an hour, or be of the least utility, and while we were putting off from the wharf, which was crowded with spectators, I heard a number of sarcastic remarks."

[Source: [Fulton's letter to Joel Barlow, 1807](https://www.historycentral.com/documents/Fultonship.html); [EyeWitness to History](http://www.eyewitnesstohistory.com/fulton.htm)]

The boat moved. It made the trip upriver in thirty-two hours, downriver in thirty. "I overtook many sloops and schooners, beating to windward," Fulton wrote, "and parted with them as if they had been at anchor." The sarcastic remarks stopped. It is always satisfying when a machine renders its critics ridiculous, and Fulton had the good fortune to experience this satisfaction within a single day.

But the steamboat was limited to navigable rivers. The country's future lay inland, where the rivers did not reach. And so the railroad became the next frontier.

## V.

The Baltimore and Ohio Railroad was chartered in 1827. Horses pulled the first trains. The B&O's directors, watching English locomotives struggle with sharp curves, had settled into the comfortable conviction that steam power would never work on American tracks.

Peter Cooper — the glue manufacturer with the musket-barrel boiler — disagreed. He had invested in land along the proposed B&O route, and no man who has invested in land can afford to believe that the railroad will fail. In August 1830, he loaded the Tom Thumb with B&O directors and friends and drove it thirteen miles from Baltimore to Ellicott's Mills. John H. B. Latrobe, the railroad's counsel, was aboard and wrote down what happened:

> "The trip was most interesting. The curves were passed without difficulty at a speed of fifteen miles an hour; the grades were ascended with comparative ease; the day was fine, the company in the highest spirits, and some excited gentlemen of the party pulled out memorandum books, and when at the highest speed, which was **eighteen miles an hour**, wrote their names and some connected sentences, to prove that even at that great velocity it was possible to do so."

On the return trip, the owners of a stagecoach company — one of those professions that the railroad was about to make irrelevant — challenged Cooper to a race. They hitched a gray horse to a passenger car on the parallel track, and at the Relay House, the starting signal was given.

> "Away went horse and engine," Latrobe wrote, "the snort of the one and the puff of the other keeping time and tune."

The horse jumped ahead. The Tom Thumb needed time for its wheels to spin the blower that fed the fire. For a quarter mile, the gap widened. Then the safety valve lifted, excess steam shot out in a thin blue vapor, and the little locomotive began to gain. It lapped the horse. It passed the horse. The passengers shouted.

And then the blower belt slipped off the pulley. The safety valve stopped screaming. The engine began to wheeze. Cooper, serving as his own engineer and fireman, lacerated his hands trying to replace the belt, threw light wood into the firebox, but the horse pulled ahead and crossed the finish line first.

[Source: [John H. B. Latrobe, *The Baltimore and Ohio Railroad: Personal Recollections* (1868)](https://minnstate.pressbooks.pub/ushistory1/chapter/first-american-steam-railroad-1830/)]

The horse won the race. But the B&O retired its horses within the year and adopted steam locomotives permanently. The horse had the glory; the steam engine got the contract. The *Tom Thumb* itself was salvaged for parts in 1834 — the musket barrels that had boiled its water probably went back into the stream of scrap metal that fed the country's hungry new foundries.

## VI.

The technologies that followed arrived like a drumbeat. Cyrus McCormick's mechanical reaper was demonstrated at Steele's Tavern in Virginia in July 1831. Cyrus was twenty-two years old. He had taken over his father's failed project six weeks earlier and built a machine that combined a reciprocating knife, a revolving reel, a platform, and a master wheel. Pulled by a horse, it cut as much grain in a day as four or five men with cradles — or twelve to sixteen men with the old reaping hooks. But farmers, who are by nature conservative about anything that works, were wary. It took McCormick nine years to sell a significant number of machines.

[Source: [Lemelson-MIT](https://lemelson.mit.edu/resources/cyrus-mccormick); [Richmond Fed](https://www.richmondfed.org/-/media/richmondfedorg/publications/research/econ_focus/2016/q3-4/economic_history.pdf)]

Samuel Morse sent the first telegraph message from the Supreme Court chamber in the Capitol to Baltimore on May 24, 1844. It was not a transmission of vital news — it was, as these things tend to be, a gesture. Annie Ellsworth, the seventeen-year-old daughter of the patent commissioner, had chosen the text from the Book of Numbers: **"What hath God wrought."** Morse inscribed the words on a paper tape, then Annie traced over them in heavy pen and ink. The tape still exists at the Library of Congress. In Morse's first official report to Congress, he noted that during the test, a messenger sent from the Capitol to find a congressman received a reply by telegraph within **ten minutes** — a round trip that would have taken an hour by horse.

Morse wrote to his brother Sidney a week later:

> "That sentence of Annie Ellsworth's was divinely [chosen], for it is in my thoughts day and night, 'What hath God wrought.' It is his work, and he alone could have carried me thus far through all my trials, and enabled me to triumph over the obstacles physical and moral which opposed me."

[Source: [Library of Congress, Morse Papers](https://www.loc.gov/item/mcc.019/); [Senate Document 270, June 6, 1844](https://www.senate.gov/about/resources/pdf/house-document-270-1844.pdf)]

The steamboat, the railroad, the telegraph, the reaper, the cotton gin — they were not separate inventions. They were an emerging system. The railroad carried the cotton that the gin had cleaned. The telegraph coordinated the trains. The reaper fed the workers who built the locomotives. By 1850, the United States had **9,000 miles** of railroad track and was laying more every year.

## VII.

On March 8, 1862, the Confederate ironclad *Virginia* — built on the hull of the scuttled Union frigate *Merrimack* — steamed out of Norfolk and attacked the wooden Union fleet at Hampton Roads. It sank the *Cumberland*, forced the *Congress* to surrender, and ran the *Minnesota* aground. The age of wooden warships ended in a single afternoon.

That night, the Union's own ironclad arrived — John Ericsson's *Monitor*, a vessel so strange that a soldier who saw it wrote home that it looked like "a long board with a hogshead setting on it." It was a steam-powered iron raft with a revolving turret, drawing only **10½ feet** of water. The *Virginia* drew **22 feet** and was confined to the channel.

The next morning, the two ironclads met. Acting Paymaster William F. Keeler, aboard the *Monitor*, wrote to his wife:

> "We fought her at **20 feet** distance a part of the time, the two vessels were touching. My hands are all dirt & powder smoke as you will discover by the paper."

[Source: [PBS NOVA](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/monitor/battle.html); [Military Images Magazine](https://www.militaryimagesmagazine-digital.com/2022/11/12/the-water-tiger-vs-the-long-board-with-a-hogshead/)]

Neither ship could penetrate the other's armor. The battle was a draw. But the implications were not. Wooden navies were obsolete. Steam had replaced sail. Iron had replaced oak. The world's first battle between ironclads was also the last battle in which a sailing warship could matter.

On land, the transformation was being measured in bodies. The Springfield Model 1861 rifled musket — the standard infantry weapon of the Union Army — was accurate to **500 yards**, more than six times the effective range of the smoothbore muskets carried at Yorktown. A skilled soldier with a Model 1861 could hit a man at distances the Revolutionary soldier could not have imagined possible. The sights on the Springfield had two folding leaves, marked "3" for 300 yards and "5" for 500. The base setting — both leaves folded down — was 100 yards. This was the distance at which the training manuals told soldiers to fire. But they did not fire at 100 yards. They fired at engagement ranges that had not changed since the Revolution — often **under 100 yards** — and the results were catastrophic.

The technology of killing had outstripped the tactics of war.

[Source: [Essential Civil War Curriculum](https://www.essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com/small-arms-and-ammunition.html); [American Battlefield Trust](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/small-arms-across-three-wars)]

And the production system that made this possible had been built at Springfield. In 1863, the Springfield Armory turned out an average of **600 rifle-muskets per day** — more than the entire annual production of the armory in the 1790s. Between 1861 and 1865, the United States produced over **1 million** Model 1861 rifled muskets, distributed among 20 contractors, several of whom had never built guns before the war. The parts were interchangeable. A Springfield lock would fit a Colt-made musket. A Colt barrel would fit a Providence Tool Company stock. This was the American System of Manufacturing, and it was born in the government armories built to supply a war that had ended before most of the technologies of production had existed.

## VIII.

The Tom Thumb was scrapped in 1834. The musket barrels that had boiled its water were likely melted down, re-forged, turned into something else. The railroads that Cooper helped launch eventually connected the East Coast to the Mississippi, and then to the Pacific. The telegraph lines followed the tracks, and then the tracks followed the telegraph lines, and the country became a single nervous system pulsing with electricity and steam.

In 1875, forty-five years after the race, Peter Cooper described the Tom Thumb to the American Railway Master Mechanics' Association. He was eighty-four years old. He had founded the Cooper Union for the Advancement of Science and Art in New York. He had patented the precursor to Jell-O — a shelf-stable powdered gelatin — in 1845. He had watched the country he had helped accelerate go through a civil war powered by the very machines he had demonstrated.

> "I went into a coach-maker's shop and made this locomotive," he said, "which I called the 'Tom Thumb,' because it was so insignificant."

The boiler was about the size of a kitchen range. The engine weighed one ton. The musket barrels lay side by side, the ghosts of the old world inside the new.

The horse that beat the Tom Thumb is gone. The gray that ran that day is not even a name in the history books — it is just "the horse," the organism that could accelerate faster than a steam engine in 1830 and could not outrun it in 1831. The man who lacerated his hands fixing the blower belt is gone. The railroad he helped create still runs.

What remains is the pace: the speed that America adopted and never relinquished, the belief that any problem could be solved by making things move faster. The musket barrels in the boiler were not a symbol of anything. They were a practical solution to a shortage of iron pipe in a country that did not yet know how to make iron pipe. But they were also a preview of everything that followed: the old technologies broken open and repurposed, the old speeds abandoned, the horse left standing at the finish line while the machine that lost the race remade the world in its image.
