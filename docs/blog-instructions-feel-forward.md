# Blog Workflow: Feel-Forward, Data-Backed

A companion to `blog-instructions.md`. The original document covers the *operations* of creating a data-driven blog (file paths, tool sequencing, source standards). This document covers the *craft* — how to make a data-backed article that a reader experiences rather than merely processes.

The central difference: in a standard data article, the numbers are the destination. In a feel-forward article, the numbers are the transport. The reader should arrive at a different emotional place, not a different informational one.

---

## Core principle: One object, one reversal

Every feel-forward data article needs two structural components before anything else can be built:

**1. An anchoring object.** Something physical, specific, and sensory that the story can orbit. In the Alcott article, this was the shelf desk — a hinged wooden board between two windows, with an inkwell recess and a painted panel of calla lilies beside it. The desk appeared in the first sentence and the last, and resurfaced at every major turn.

The anchoring object can be:
- A physical artifact (a machine, a building, a piece of clothing, a tool)
- A specific place (a room, a street corner, a landscape feature)
- A document (a letter, a ledger, a photograph, a map)
- A body (a person's hands, a scar, a posture)

What it cannot be: abstract (an economic indicator, a trend, a dataset). Those are supporting structures, not anchors.

**2. A reversal arc.** The reader should encounter the data in an order that generates surprise. Typically: small numbers first, large numbers later. The reader must feel scarcity before they can feel the scale of abundance. They must witness the problem before they can grasp the solution.

Structure the arc as: *This is what was at stake → This is what someone did → This is what happened → This is what it meant.* The data serves each stage, but the sequence is driven by narrative logic, not by data taxonomy.

## Stage 1: Gather with your senses (research sweep)

This is the same research sweep as `blog-instructions.md` — `websearch`, `webfetch`, primary sources only, government data, peer-reviewed papers, institutional reports. The difference is *what you look for inside each source once you open it.*

For each source you fetch, extract three things and write them in your `todowrite` notes before moving to the next source:

**1. The best physical detail.** Every primary source contains something you can see, hear, touch, or smell — even if it's buried inside dry language. A census record contains a person's address. A cost ledger contains the price of a single item. A patent contains a diagram. A field report contains a description of weather. Extract one sensory detail per source. You will not use all of them, but you cannot find the one you need if you do not collect them.

**2. The best 20 words of someone speaking.** If the source contains human language — a diary, a letter, a deposition, an interview transcript, a lab notebook, a memo — find the sentence that sounds least like writing and most like a person talking. Capture it verbatim. This will become a blockquote in the article. The reader needs to hear a human voice, not just a narrator's summary.

**3. The smallest number.** Not the total, not the aggregate, not the percentage change. The single smallest concrete figure in the source — the $5, the 50¢, the 22 moves, the 2,000 copies, the 7 weeks. This number will appear early in the article. It establishes the granularity of the reader's world.

Track each source you fetch (URL, title, publisher) as the original instructions require. Mark each category complete in `todowrite` only after you have extracted all three elements.

## Stage 2: The direction check

Before you write anything, present your findings to the user. Follow the same structure as `blog-instructions.md` — use `question` with concrete options. But ask these specific feel-forward questions alongside the operational ones:

**a) What is the anchoring object?**
Give the user 2-3 candidates from your research and let them choose. The user will often pick a different object than you expected, and the user's instinct is usually better because they know the audience.

**b) What is the reversal arc?**
Describe the shape of the story in one sentence: "We start with a woman earning $65/year and end with her out-earning Henry James 8 to 1." The user should confirm this shape or redirect it. If the user wants a different emotional arc — tragedy instead of triumph, stasis instead of reversal — you need to know before you sequence the sections.

**c) What visual treatment fits the feel?**
Same options as the original instructions (charts only, photos only, mixed, none) but with a feel-forward framing: "The chart will appear in the second half, after the reader has absorbed the human-scale data through prose. Photos will be archival and specific to the anchoring object."

**d) What draft format?**
Same options as the original instructions (outline first, full draft, short brief). The feel-forward addition: if the user asks for an outline, structure it by emotional payload (Stage 3 of this document), not by data category.

Present these questions alongside the operational ones from `blog-instructions.md` (article format, visual treatment, draft approach). Let the user decide before proceeding.

## Stage 3: Find the sensory entry point

After the direction is set, before writing a word, answer three questions for yourself. Capture the answers in your `todowrite` notes:

**a) What is the one physical thing a reader could touch that would make them understand the story?**
If the answer is "nothing," you haven't found your anchor yet. Search for it. It might be hiding in a photograph, a museum catalog, a court exhibit, a patent drawing, a field report.

**b) What is the smallest number in the story?**
Not the aggregate. Not the total. The smallest, most human-scaled figure — the $5 story, the 50¢ room, the single musket ball in the dirt. This number will appear early, often in the lede or first thematic section. It establishes the granularity of the world the reader is entering.

**c) Who is the one person whose own words should interrupt the narrative?**
Find a primary voice — someone who was there, who wrote about it, who said something in their own idiom. This voice will appear in blockquote format, not paraphrased. It does the emotional work that exposition cannot.

## Stage 4: Source with sensory intention

The existing `blog-instructions.md` defines source standards (primary sources, government data, peer-reviewed research). Follow those. But add two feel-forward filters during collection:

**Filter 1: Does this source contain a physical detail?**
When you find a source, extract not just the data point but the sensory details nested inside it. The Alcott article used a Houghton Library finding aid not just for its data (she kept account books) but for the detail that she recorded them "in a firm, unadorned hand." That detail came from the same source as the numbers. It cost nothing to include and it moved the reader closer to the person.

**Filter 2: Does this source contain a quote that sounds like a real person talking?**
If your source is a diary, a letter, a deposition, a transcript, a field interview, a set of instructions written under duress — extract the best 20 words of vernacular speech. You will place this in blockquote somewhere in the first half of the article. It is the reader's first direct encounter with a human being who is not the narrator.

---

## Stage 5: Build the sequence (not the outline)

Traditional data-article outlines group information by category: "Economic Impact," "Historical Context," "Demographics." A feel-forward article sequences information by emotional payload.

Build a sequence by asking: *What does the reader need to feel before they can understand the next thing?*

For the Alcott article, the sequence was:

| Step | Reader feeling | What they learn |
|------|----------------|-----------------|
| 1 | She was poor | $65/year, sewing, teaching |
| 2 | The poverty was structural | Bronson's failures, 22 moves, 50¢ rooms |
| 3 | She didn't believe in the book | "Never liked girls… I doubt it" |
| 4 | The book sold anyway | 2,000 copies, 2 weeks |
| 5 | It sold more | 13,000 in a month |
| 6 | She got very rich | $200,000 vs. James/Whitman |
| 7 | She paid the debts that mattered | Lizzie's doctor bill, furnace, Paris |

The reversal only works because steps 1-3 create a feeling of constraint that steps 4-7 release.

To build your own sequence:

1. List every data point you have
2. Sort them by magnitude (smallest to largest)
3. Check if the smallest is also the most human. If not, find a human-scale sub-point within the largest data category
4. Arrange thematic sections so that the reader encounters the small numbers before the large ones
5. Insert a primary-voice blockquote in at least every other section — more often in the early sections

## Stage 6: The chart rule

One chart per article. Two at most, and only if they serve distinct and separate points (e.g., one showing change over time, one showing a comparison).

The chart must not appear in the first half of the article unless the article is very short (under 1,500 words). The reader must earn the visualization by reading. When the chart arrives, it should feel like a confirmation of what the prose has already established — not like a reveal.

Chart placement criteria:
- The reader should already know, from the prose, what the chart is going to show
- The chart should confirm the scale of something the reader has already been told about at the human level
- The chart should never be the lede, never be the closing image, and never appear without a prose anchor immediately before and after it

---

## Stage 7: Delayed-reveal sentences

A feel-forward article uses sentence structure to control when the reader receives emotional information. The technique: put the most emotionally charged word or phrase at the end of the sentence, after a series of neutral descriptors.

*Before (data-forward):* "She used her earnings to pay a decade-old doctor's bill for Lizzie, her sister who died of scarlet fever."

*After (feel-forward):* "It was a doctor's bill from a decade earlier — the physician who had attended her younger sister Lizzie during the final stages of scarlet fever."

In the second version, the reader doesn't learn that Lizzie died until the end of the sentence. They walk through "doctor's bill" → "decade earlier" → "physician" → "attended" → "younger sister" → "final stages." The delay creates a moment of anticipatory grief that the straightforward version collapses into two words ("Lizzie's doctor bill").

Apply this technique to the one or two most emotionally consequential facts in the article. Do not use it everywhere — its power depends on scarcity.

---

## Stage 8: The closing must not summarize

A feel-forward article ends on the anchoring object, not on the data. The last paragraph should contain no new information and no synthesis of old information. It should return the reader to the physical world of the opening, transformed by what they have learned.

Structure: three sentences.

- Sentence 1: A statement about the anchoring object that reveals something new about it (the desk has no lock)
- Sentence 2: A statement about what the protagonist did with or at that object
- Sentence 3: A statement that reframes the object in light of the story's arc (it is the most hopeful object in American literature because it is empty and waiting)

The closing should feel inevitable but not predictable. The reader should sense that the article could not have ended any other way, but they could not have written the ending before reading the article.

---

## Stage 9: The Wilde pass (or equivalent)

Every feel-forward article benefits from a stylistic pass by a voice that is not the reporting voice. The goal is not to make the article *funnier* — it is to create a tonal relationship between the narrator and the subject that gives the reader permission to feel something complex.

The voice should be:
- Wry about institutions (publishers, governments, corporations, bureaucracies)
- Tender about people (subjects, witnesses, the vulnerable)
- Amused by paradox (the book she doubted made her fortune)
- Never mocking of suffering (poverty, illness, loss, failure)

Apply this tone unevenly. The early sections (where the stakes are highest for the protagonist) should be warmer. The middle sections (where the systems and institutions are described) can be sharper. The closing should be the most restrained — the reader should feel the writer's presence receding as the object takes over.

## Stage 10: Self-check before sign-off

Before marking the article complete, answer these questions:

- [ ] Can a reader describe the anchoring object from memory after reading?
- [ ] Does the smallest number in the article appear in the first two sections?
- [ ] Is there at least one blockquote from a primary voice in the first half?
- [ ] Does the chart appear at or after the midpoint?
- [ ] If the chart were removed, would the article's emotional arc still function?
- [ ] Does the closing paragraph contain zero new data points?
- [ ] Does the closing paragraph return to the anchoring object?
- [ ] Is every period of creative or financial success preceded by a period of constraint or scarcity?
- [ ] Would the article still be interesting if every number were removed? (Not accurate — but interesting.)

If the answer to any of these is no, the article is probably still data-forward. Revise toward feel.

## Why this isn't a template

A template prescribes section structure: Section 1 is History, Section 2 is Economics, Section 3 is Impact. This workflow prescribes a *relationship* between data and narrative, not a container for it.

Applied to different domains:

- **A technology article** would anchor on a specific device or component (the first chip, the prototype, the failed design). The reversal arc would move from what the technology could not do → what it learned to do → what it made possible. The blockquote would come from an engineer's lab notes or a user's first impression.

- **A science article** would anchor on a specific specimen, field site, or instrument. The reversal arc would move from what was not known → what the data revealed → what is now at stake. The blockquote would come from a researcher's field journal or a peer review comment.

- **An economics article** would anchor on a specific transaction, balance sheet, or physical currency. The reversal arc would move from scarcity → intervention → outcome. The blockquote would come from a participant in the market or a regulator's testimony.

- **A sports article** would anchor on a specific play, equipment, or venue. The reversal arc would move from limitation → adaptation → transformation. The blockquote would come from an athlete's memoir or a coach's sideline remark.

The machinery changes. The relationship between data and reader does not.
